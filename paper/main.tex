\begin{abstract}
The Segment Anything Model (SAM) makes large-scale pseudo-labeling feasible for autonomous-driving perception, but its masks drift at boundaries, hallucinate regions, and miss small objects, with errors concentrated in safety-critical cyclist and pedestrian classes.
Vision–language models (VLMs) are increasingly proposed to filter such labels, yet it is unclear when their reasoning helps versus when a cheaper, domain-adapted signal suffices.

We answer this through a semi-supervised pipeline on the Zenseact Open Dataset (ZOD): 2{,}319 curated frames train a domain-adapted Swin Transformer that scores SAM mask quality; a zero-shot VLM provides independent object-level verification.
Both signals score masks from 4{,}135 unannotated flagged frames; a Swin-driven discovery module additionally recovers objects SAM missed.
We evaluate with two open-weight VLMs (LLaVA-1.6-34B and Qwen2.5-VL-72B) using identical fixed prompts for both, measuring downstream effect by training a camera–LiDAR fusion model on every annotation variant.

All variants are evaluated against a single human-curated reference on a common test set, with paired bootstrap confidence intervals.
Under this fixed-prompt, zero-shot setting, the domain-adapted Swin subsumes the VLM: Swin-only filtering reaches 47.6 mIoU, significantly exceeding both the curated SAM baseline trained on the clean partition (44.4 mIoU; $\Delta{=}{+}3.3$, 95\% CI $[+1.5,+5.5]$) and unfiltered SAM pseudo-labels (43.2 mIoU), while adding VLM verdicts to the triage rule yields no significant further gain.
Swin-driven discovery recovers thousands of objects SAM missed, but VLM-confirmed discovery still trails Swin-only filtering, and its downstream cost tracks the volume and precision of the confirmed candidate set rather than mask geometry.
Applying the same prompts to Qwen2.5-VL-72B shows that fixed prompts do not transfer neutrally, and that the failure is prompt-specific rather than a uniform model disposition: Qwen returns \texttt{valid} on 71.8\% of mask crops against LLaVA's 62.1\%, while on discovery the two models agree closely overall (82.5\% vs.\ 83.9\% confirmation of the same candidates) yet diverge sharply on the one prompt covering the safety-critical human classes (43.5\% vs.\ 84.9\%).
We further report an operational failure mode that annotation pipelines rarely document: a VLM serving fault returned content-free responses for 72\% of one model's frames, which the pipeline silently replaced with safe defaults, manufacturing a large and entirely plausible cross-model finding that did not survive instrumented re-annotation.
We describe the response-level monitoring that detects it, and separate every verdict rate from its safe-default share so that a defaulted verdict is never counted as a judgement.
All decisions trace to explicit outputs with no learned fusion weights, enabling full reproducibility without re-running VLM inference.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Pixel-level annotations for autonomous driving are expensive, yet the Segment Anything Model (SAM) \cite{kirillov2023sam} has made large-scale pseudo-labeling feasible.
The challenge is reliability: SAM masks drift at boundaries, hallucinate in texturally uniform regions, and miss small traffic participants, with errors concentrated in safety-critical cyclists, pedestrians, and signs.
Geometric filtering approaches \cite{zou2019confidence,lee2013pseudo,jaritz2020xmuda} cannot detect such semantic failures: a mask with a plausible shape but the wrong class label passes every geometric check and degrades downstream training.
Vision–language models (VLMs) \cite{liu2024llava,wang2024qwen2vl} offer richer semantic priors, but their effectiveness for SAM mask refinement, especially on small object crops and multimodal camera–LiDAR inputs, remains unclear.

We study this problem using a semi-supervised pipeline on the Zenseact Open Dataset (ZOD) \cite{alibeigi2023zod}.
A clean partition of 2{,}319 curated frames is used to train a domain-adapted Swin Transformer \cite{liu2021swin} that provides per-pixel class agreement scores for every SAM mask.
A zero-shot VLM supplies an independent object-level verdict on tight crops.
Rejection requires concordance between multiple signals (Swin agreement, VLM verdict, and a deterministic LiDAR consistency check), reducing the risk that any single erroneous judge silently removes valid labels.
A Swin-driven discovery module identifies objects missed by SAM, addressing both false positives and false negatives.
Downstream impact is measured by training CLFTv2 \cite{gu2024clft}, a camera–LiDAR fusion model, on each annotation variant and evaluating performance across weather conditions and a second dataset.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{a controlled comparison} of domain-adapted segmentation and zero-shot VLM verification under identical fixed prompts, showing when each signal is reliable and when it is not,
    \item \textbf{a prompt-portability analysis} showing that identical prompts elicit measurably different acceptance and discovery behaviour from two open VLMs, that the divergence is concentrated in individual prompts rather than spread uniformly across a model's outputs, and that it is largest on the prompt covering the safety-critical human classes,
    \item \textbf{a Swin-driven discovery mechanism} that recovers objects missed by SAM, together with a downstream analysis showing that discovery quality tracks candidate-set volume and precision rather than mask geometry,
    \item \textbf{a reliability analysis of VLM-as-judge annotation}, reporting a serving fault that silently converted 72\,\% of one model's verdicts into safe defaults and produced a spurious cross-model result, the response-level monitoring that detects such faults while a run is in progress, and a reporting discipline that separates a substituted default from an answer,
    \item and \textbf{a downstream study} under a single curated evaluation reference with paired bootstrap confidence intervals, quantifying how each annotation variant affects camera–LiDAR fusion performance beyond mask agreement.
\end{itemize}





\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

\subsection{Semantic Segmentation and Pseudo-Labeling in Autonomous Driving}

Semantic segmentation underpins scene understanding in autonomous driving, with camera-based architectures such as FCN, PSPNet, and DeepLab \cite{long2015fcn,zhao2017psp,chen2018deeplab} establishing dense prediction baselines and later work extending these to camera--LiDAR fusion through feature-level alignment \cite{valada2019selfsupervised,jaritz2019multimodal}. 
Despite strong performance on established benchmarks, these models depend on pixel-accurate supervision that is expensive to collect at scale.

Pseudo-labeling and self-training methods \cite{lee2013pseudo,zou2019confidence} reduce labeling cost by using model predictions as supervision, while cross-modal consistency constraints \cite{jaritz2020xmuda} and LiDAR-projection checks \cite{behley2019semantickitti,caesar2020nuscenes} leverage geometric redundancy to filter erroneous labels. 
These approaches improve annotation efficiency but rely on fixed thresholds and cannot detect semantic errors: a mask with geometrically plausible shape but an incorrect class label passes all geometric checks and degrades downstream training. 
Rare classes such as cyclists and pedestrians, which occupy small pixel regions and appear infrequently, remain particularly challenging for pseudo-labeling pipelines.

\subsection{Foundation Models for Scalable Annotation}

The Segment Anything Model (SAM) \cite{kirillov2023sam} has changed the economics of mask generation by enabling class-agnostic proposals without per-class prompts or labeled training data. 
This has motivated large-scale pseudo-annotation pipelines for autonomous driving, where SAM proposals are matched to semantic categories using sensor overlays or downstream classifiers. 
However, SAM's class-agnostic design means it cannot verify the semantic correctness of its own output. 
Boundary drift arises where object edges are ambiguous in RGB; hallucinated regions appear in texturally uniform areas such as sky and vegetation; and small objects are frequently missed when their footprint falls below SAM's effective resolution. 
Post-hoc semantic validation is therefore a necessary complement to SAM-based annotation.

\subsection{Vision--Language Models and Multi-Signal Filtering}

Vision--language models (VLMs) combine image encoders with autoregressive language decoders, enabling structured reasoning over visual content \cite{liu2024llava,ye2023mplug,wang2024qwen2vl}. 
The LLM-as-judge paradigm \cite{zheng2023judging} shows that language models can evaluate the quality of other models' outputs when given structured prompts and constrained output formats, providing a scalable alternative to human review in certain settings. 
In annotation pipelines, this suggests VLMs may serve as semantic judges over mask proposals, verifying object identity and flagging implausible regions, without requiring fine-tuning on task-specific labels.

A complementary line of work studies ensemble and multi-signal filtering for label-noise reduction \cite{malach2017decoupling,han2018co}. 
Requiring agreement between independent classifiers before rejecting a sample reduces the risk that any single erroneous judge silently discards valid training examples. 
This principle motivates our concordance-based triage rule: rejection requires at least two negative signals among Swin agreement, VLM verdict, and LiDAR consistency, so that disagreement escalates the mask rather than deleting it. 
Prior work has not applied such concordance-based filtering to SAM mask validation over camera--LiDAR inputs, nor compared pixel-level segmentation and zero-shot VLM signals as independent quality judges within a unified annotation pipeline.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

The system refines SAM-generated masks using a set of specialized visual--LLM agents and a domain-adapted segmentation model. 
Most agents receive a multimodal input bundle consisting of an RGB crop, a mask overlay, a LiDAR depth projection, and structured metadata, and return a categorical decision. 
The BBox Agent is an exception and receives only the RGB crop to reduce scene-level bias. 
All agent outputs feed into a deterministic triage rule designed for safety-critical annotation workflows.

\subsection{Multimodal Input Construction}

For each SAM proposal, we extract a tight crop around the bounding box and generate three aligned channels: the RGB crop, the mask overlay, and a projected LiDAR depth map. 
Metadata describing geometric properties and LiDAR support is provided as structured text. 
Object-scale cropping is essential: small traffic participants often occupy only 10--30 pixels, and full-frame VLM queries struggle to resolve them reliably. 
Each crop is upscaled to at least $224{\times}224$ pixels and processed independently; the full frame is used only as optional context.

\subsection{Visual--LLM Agents}

The system decomposes mask assessment into three VLM agents (BBox, Failure--Mode, and Correction), one Swin-based quality agent, and one deterministic geometric check. 
Per-mask execution order is: Consistency Check, BBox Agent, Swin agreement, then Correction on the refine path only.

All VLM agents use a single fixed prompt per role, applied identically to both LLaVA-1.6-34B and Qwen2.5-VL-72B without any model-specific calibration.
This reflects the realistic deployment constraint of large-scale annotation pipelines, where per-model prompt tuning is infeasible, and it enables a direct test of how well fixed prompts transfer across VLMs of different architectures and capability levels.

\paragraph{Consistency Check}
A deterministic LiDAR-support test is run first, before any VLM call: the fraction of mask pixels with projected LiDAR returns must exceed $\tau{=}0.1$. 
This check reliably detects masks leaking into sky or void regions at zero inference cost.

\paragraph{Bounding-Box Agent}
Performs blind forced-choice classification over a fixed vocabulary of object classes and distractors. 
The agent is not told the expected class to avoid agreement bias. 
Traffic lights and pole-mounted road lighting are included in the vocabulary and mapped to the \texttt{sign} class following ZOD annotation conventions. 
Outputs are: \texttt{valid}, \texttt{background}, or \texttt{invalid}. 
The agent sees only the RGB crop; overlays and depth channels are withheld to reduce the risk of classifying surrounding context rather than the marked object.

\paragraph{Quality Agent}
Evaluates mask correctness using a Swin Transformer \cite{liu2021swin} trained on clean ZOD frames. 
A single forward pass produces a per-pixel class map for the entire frame; per-mask agreement~$\alpha$ is computed from that shared prediction without an additional forward pass. 
Because Swin achieves higher mIoU on large objects than on cyclists and pedestrians, we use class-specific thresholds: $\tau_q^{\text{large}}{=}0.30$ and $\tau_q^{\text{small}}{=}0.15$; this choice targets mask-level recall on rare classes, and its downstream effect is ablated in Table~\ref{tab:ablation}.
For ablation studies, all agents are called on every mask to enable direct signal comparison; bypass thresholds ($0.70$ for large classes, $0.40$ for small classes) are used only for deployment efficiency and are not applied during evaluation.

\paragraph{Correction Agent}
Called only on the refine path (\texttt{bbox=valid}, $\alpha{\geq}\tau_q$, \texttt{consistency=fail}).
Determines whether the mask can be improved and proposes a single refinement step (\texttt{refine} or \texttt{no\_refine}).
This is the one agent whose prompt failed to transfer outright.
Qwen2.5-VL-72B returned an empty response to all 1{,}205 correction calls in the paired frame set, so every one fell back to \texttt{no\_refine} and the refine path never fired, whereas LLaVA answered 1{,}068 of 1{,}075 calls with \texttt{refine}.
The behaviour reproduces in the discarded campaign as well as the instrumented re-run, so it is a property of this model and prompt rather than the serving fault of Section~\ref{par:serving}.
It is reported here as a null result and no refine-path claim is made for Qwen; the prompt differs from the others in requesting a judgement about a hypothetical corrective action rather than a description of visible content, which is a plausible but untested explanation.

\paragraph{Failure-Mode Agent}
Classifies the dominant error type (\texttt{boundary\_drift}, \texttt{hallucination}, \texttt{occlusion\_miss}, \texttt{fragmentation}). 
Diagnostic only; does not affect triage outcomes.

All VLM agents return constrained categorical outputs to ensure reproducibility.

\subsection{Swin-Based Object Discovery}

Triage removes false positives but cannot detect objects SAM missed. 
We therefore introduce a discovery step that compares the Swin class map to the SAM annotation at $384{\times}384$ resolution. 
Pixels where Swin predicts a non-background class but SAM predicts background form candidate regions. 
Connected components below a minimum size are discarded; the largest remaining components (up to $K_{\text{disc}}$ per frame) are cropped and verified by a VLM. 
For vehicles and signs, the VLM confirms presence; for humans, it also disambiguates cyclist vs.\ pedestrian. 
Confirmed regions are added as new masks, modifying only pixels still labeled as background.

\subsection{Risk-Aware Deterministic Triage}

Instead of weighted scoring, we use a deterministic rule requiring concordant evidence for rejection. 
Rejection is destructive: deleted pixels cannot be recovered, so relying on any single negative signal would compound false positives across agents. 
Let $\mathcal{N}=\{\texttt{invalid},\texttt{bad},\texttt{fail}\}$ denote negative signals.

A mask is \textbf{rejected} if at least two signals in $\mathcal{N}$ are present. 
A \texttt{background} verdict counts as two signals and rejects immediately. 
To avoid over-rejection of small objects, when \texttt{bbox=invalid} but the Quality Agent scores the mask as \texttt{good}, the \texttt{invalid} verdict is downgraded to a single negative signal. 
The mask is then routed to human review when consistency passes, or rejected only when consistency additionally fails.

A mask is \textbf{accepted} if the BBox agent outputs \texttt{valid}, the Quality Agent outputs \texttt{good}, and the Consistency Check outputs \texttt{pass}.

A mask is routed to \textbf{refinement} if it is valid and of good quality but fails the Consistency Check and the Correction Agent recommends refinement.

All remaining cases, with exactly one dissenting judge, are sent to \textbf{human review}. 
Review masks are retained but flagged, ensuring that no single erroneous judgment silently deletes valid labels.

\subsection{Interpretability and Reproducibility}

All prompts, thresholds, and decision rules are fixed before evaluation. 
Every triage outcome can be traced to explicit agent outputs, and no learned fusion weights or continuous scores are used, supporting reproducibility across datasets and models.

We evaluate the proposed multi-agent annotation system on the Zenseact Open Dataset (ZOD) \cite{alibeigi2023zod} using SAM-generated mask proposals from our upstream annotation pipeline (released alongside this work; see Reproducibility below).
All agents return categorical decisions, and all triage outcomes follow the deterministic rules in Section~\ref{sec:methodology}. 
No continuous scores or weighted combinations are used.

\paragraph{Computational Cost}
Table~\ref{tab:timing} reports inference time extracted from per-mask and per-frame timings logged during annotation, on the paired frame set of Section~\ref{par:comparability}.
The BBox agent dominates cost: a single crop verification call takes 3.54s (LLaVA) or 11.63s (Qwen) on average, and is issued once per mask, so per-mask latency scales almost linearly with model size.
The Correction agent, triggered only for the 1.5--1.8\% of masks that fail the LiDAR consistency check, adds a comparable per-call cost but touches few masks overall.
The Swin Quality Agent runs once per frame at negligible cost ($<$0.01s/mask), confirming that domain-adapted CNN triage is essentially free relative to VLM inference.
Annotating the paired 3{,}535 frames (73{,}708 masks) on a single A100 with 4 concurrent frame workers took 108.6 GPU-hours with LLaVA-1.6-34B versus 356.2 GPU-hours with Qwen2.5-VL-72B, a $3.3\times$ cost increase, or 27.1 against 89.0 wall-clock hours.
The Qwen figure is a deployed rather than a fault-free cost: it includes the retry and reload traffic generated by the 17 monitor-triggered restarts described above.
Against that, the larger model's triage verdicts are less selective, not more (Table~\ref{tab:agent_behavior}), and its confirmation rate on the same discovery candidates is marginally lower overall while differing sharply on one prompt, so the $3.3\times$ premium buys a different annotation distribution rather than a better one on any axis we measure.
Estimated VRAM footprint (quantized weights plus a bounded KV cache at the deployed parallelism) is $\sim$22GB for LLaVA-1.6-34B versus $\sim$51GB for Qwen2.5-VL-72B, comfortably within the single A100-80GB used for both models.

\subsection{Serving Reliability and Response-Level Monitoring}
\label{par:serving}
An initial Qwen2.5-VL-72B campaign had to be discarded and re-run, and the reason is worth reporting rather than hiding, because the failure was silent by construction and any VLM-as-judge pipeline is exposed to it.
A few hours after each model load, the serving runtime stopped emitting a stop token and answered every request with a short run of non-alphanumeric characters, for 14 to 44 hours at a stretch, four times over six days.
No component raised an error.
Each agent treated the unreadable reply as a parse failure, exhausted its retries, and substituted its \textsc{safe\_default} --- which for the BBox agent is \texttt{valid} and for discovery is \emph{not confirmed} --- so a total serving outage was recorded as a decisive, and opposite, judgement on each of the two tasks.
The run completed without warning signs, and 2{,}972 of 4{,}135 frames (72\,\%) carried defaulted verdicts.
The resulting numbers were not obviously wrong: they read as a model that accepts almost every mask while confirming almost no discovery candidate, which is a coherent and publishable behavioural profile. It was an artifact of the defaults, and it did not reproduce.

The degenerate responses are stored verbatim under \texttt{discovered[].vlm\_response}, which makes the fault diagnosable after the fact: a frame whose discovery replies contain no alphanumeric character at all was processed inside an outage window.
We now also detect it while a run is in progress.
Every response is classified usable or degenerate on arrival; a sliding window over the last 200 calls aborts the run when the degenerate fraction exceeds 50\,\% over at least 60 samples, rather than writing defaulted verdicts; a startup canary probe rejects an already-degraded server; and the batch script treats the resulting distinct exit status as a signal to reload the model and resume from the frames on disk, while still failing immediately on any other error, which a reload cannot fix.
Replaying the recorded incident detects 43{,}784 corrupted responses with no misses and no false positives.
Detection also changes the cost profile: an episode is now caught within roughly 60 calls instead of persisting for a day, and the re-run absorbed 17 automatic reload-and-resume cycles, with 5.25\,\% of its 132{,}390 calls degenerate but only 3.7\,\% of masks left carrying a defaulted verdict.
Per-agent parse outcomes are recorded per mask from this point on, which is what allows Tables~\ref{tab:agent_behavior} and~\ref{tab:agent_performance} to report the safe-default share alongside every rate.
The practical lesson is that a VLM judge must be monitored at the level of individual response content, since availability checks, exit statuses, and aggregate verdict distributions all reported this run as healthy.

\subsection{Cross-Model Comparability}
\label{par:comparability}
Because only the Qwen campaign was re-run under the monitor, the two runs are instrumented asymmetrically, and the cross-model comparison is constructed to be valid despite that rather than assuming it away.
Three measures are taken.

First, all cross-model numbers are computed on the frames \emph{both} runs completed (3{,}535 frames, 73{,}708 masks), not on each run's full output.
The comparison is paired at mask level, and we verify that the two runs saw identical VLM-independent inputs on it: the same mask identifiers, bit-identical Swin agreement and LiDAR support values, and the same number of Swin-proposed discovery candidates in every frame.
Any verdict difference is therefore attributable to the VLM alone.
Triage outcomes are recomputed offline under the current deterministic rule for both runs, so a rule revision between campaigns cannot masquerade as a model difference.

Second, BBox verdict rates are reported \emph{as-recorded}, meaning that masks whose verdict is a substituted default are included, because that is the verdict the pipeline acted on and wrote into the annotations that train the downstream models.
For Qwen we additionally report the default share (13.3\,\% of masks) and the rate over answered masks only.
The equivalent decomposition is not available for LLaVA: raw BBox responses were never stored, only the parsed verdict, so its defaulted masks are indistinguishable offline from confidently valid ones and cannot be recovered without re-running the model.
This does not, however, leave the comparison undetermined.
Since the BBox \textsc{safe\_default} is \texttt{valid}, every defaulted mask is counted as valid, so any run's valid rate is a weighted mean of its answered rate and 100\,\%, giving
\begin{equation}
\text{valid}_{\text{answered}} \;\leq\; \text{valid}_{\text{as-recorded}}
\label{eq:default_bound}
\end{equation}
for \emph{any} run, whatever its default share.
LLaVA's answered-only valid rate is thus at most its as-recorded 62.1\,\%, while Qwen's is measured at 67.5\,\%.
Qwen is therefore the more permissive model on masks both models actually answered, by at least 5.3 points, and LLaVA's unmeasured defaults can only widen that gap, never close it.
The direction of the effect is settled without re-running LLaVA; only its magnitude remains a lower bound.

Third, discovery is compared on ground that is fully measured on both sides.
Both runs store each discovery response verbatim, so we re-derive the outcome for both with a single parser and separate four cases the stored \texttt{confirmed} flag collapses into one: the model named a class the prompt offered, the model declined (\texttt{other}, a genuine negative), the model replied outside the offered vocabulary, or no content was returned at all.
Confirmation rates are then taken over answered candidates only.
Pooling those cases is precisely what made a degraded server read as a sceptical model, and separating them also distinguishes model-side non-answering (out-of-vocabulary text: 1.8\,\% for LLaVA, 0.0\,\% for Qwen) from server-side non-answering (content-free replies: 0.0\,\% and 2.9\,\%), which have opposite causes and opposite interpretations.

One limitation is not removable by any of this and is stated plainly: the Qwen re-run was still in progress at the time of writing, at 3{,}535 of 4{,}135 frames, and 750 of its completed frames still carry at least one defaulted verdict pending a targeted redo.
The rates below are therefore provisional, and the downstream results in Table~\ref{tab:ablation} that depend on Qwen annotations predate the re-run entirely (see Section~\ref{sec:experiments}).


\subsection{Downstream Segmentation}

We train CLFTv2-Base (RGB--LiDAR fusion) on all annotation variants.
VLM-dependent variants use the flagged partition (4{,}135 frames, train split from Table~\ref{tab:splits}); a curated SAM baseline uses the clean partition (2{,}319 frames) to provide a quality-controlled reference at a smaller annotation volume.
Rejected pixels are mapped to \texttt{ignore} in all VLM-annotated variants.

\paragraph{Evaluation Protocol}
Comparing annotation variants requires a reference that is independent of every variant's own training labels: scoring a model against the same pseudo-labels it was trained on measures label self-consistency rather than segmentation quality, and inflates precisely the variants with the most idiosyncratic labels.
We therefore evaluate every trained model on the same held-out frames, the five per-weather test subsets of the clean partition (565 frames), against the manually curated annotations, the only human-verified reference available.
Results are reported as mIoU averaged over vehicle, sign, and human (cyclist + pedestrian combined), where the overall mIoU is the mean of the five per-weather mIoUs.
Uncertainty is quantified with a paired bootstrap: test frames are resampled with replacement 10{,}000 times within each weather stratum, with identical resample indices for all variants, yielding 95\% confidence intervals for each variant and for pairwise mIoU differences.
One caveat is inherent to this protocol: the curated reference is itself SAM-derived, so objects that SAM missed are absent from it, and a model that correctly segments such objects is penalised.
Discovery-related results below should be read with this conservative bias in mind.
We additionally include a modality ablation (RGB, LiDAR, Fusion) on the Swin + disc.\ variant to quantify each sensor stream's contribution independently of annotation quality.

\paragraph{Superseded Qwen-dependent variants}
The annotation variants that consume Qwen verdicts were trained before the serving fault of Section~\ref{par:serving} was identified, so their training labels contain the defaulted verdicts rather than the model's judgements.
Those rows are marked with a dagger in Table~\ref{tab:ablation} and are reported for completeness only; we draw no conclusions from them, and every claim below is stated over the VLM-independent and LLaVA-based variants, which are unaffected.
Two consequences are worth stating in advance, because they retract results a previous version of this analysis reported.
First, the two-VLM consensus variants are derived by intersecting the two confirmed sets, so a Qwen set that was suppressed to roughly a quarter of its true size produced a correspondingly small intersection; on the re-run, Qwen confirms 36{,}596 of the same candidates rather than 9{,}878, and the intersection can no longer act as the aggressive precision filter that the earlier consensus result attributed to it.
Second, any account of Qwen's downstream behaviour resting on selective discovery confirmation is withdrawn, since that selectivity was the artifact itself.
Retraining these variants on the corrected annotations, and re-deriving the consensus sets from them, is in progress and is the remaining gap in this evaluation.

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} reports vehicle, sign, and combined human-class IoU (cyclist + pedestrian) for all variants.
Several observations emerge from these results.

\textbf{Swin-filtered pseudo-labels surpass the curated baseline.}
Swin-only filtering achieves 47.6 mIoU, the best result of any variant, exceeding the curated SAM baseline trained on 2{,}319 manually reviewed frames (44.4; paired $\Delta{=}{+}3.3$, 95\% CI $[+1.5,+5.5]$, $P(\Delta{\leq}0){=}0.0004$) and unfiltered raw SAM (43.2; $\Delta{=}{+}4.5$, CI $[+3.0,+6.2]$).
Annotation volume combined with a domain-adapted quality signal therefore compensates for the label-quality gap of pseudo-labels: filtering alone, with no VLM involvement, converts 4{,}135 flagged frames into better training data than 2{,}319 hand-curated ones.

\textbf{VLM triage adds no significant gain over Swin.}
Full triage (Swin + VLM + consistency) reaches 44.5 mIoU with LLaVA, \emph{below} Swin-only filtering ($\Delta{=}{-}3.2$, CI $[-5.1,-1.5]$), and VLM-only triage without the Swin signal is statistically indistinguishable from full triage (LLaVA: 43.7 vs.\ 44.5, $\Delta{=}{+}0.9$, CI $[-1.2,+2.9]$).
Crop-based VLM rejection removes masks that pixel-level Swin agreement retains, and the removals hurt more than they help under the fixed-prompt configuration studied here.

\textbf{Discovery regions carry a volume-dependent annotation cost, distinct from mask geometry.}
To separate the effect of mask geometry from candidate volume, we trained each discovery variant under two painting schemes: candidates added as their exact connected-component masks versus as filled bounding boxes.
Geometry matters only when candidates are unverified: the no-VLM variant collapses under bounding-box painting (21.1 vs.\ 39.5 mIoU with exact masks) while the confirmed variant is nearly unchanged (LLaVA: 41.0 vs.\ 40.5), so coarse-mask noise scales with the number of unconfirmed candidates and contributes little once a VLM has trimmed the set.
All discovery results below use exact connected-component masks.
Even so, discovery reduces mIoU relative to Swin-only: Swin-only (0 regions, 47.6) $>$ LLaVA-confirmed (45{,}882 regions, 40.5; $\Delta{=}{+}7.2$, CI $[+5.6,+9.1]$) $\approx$ unconfirmed (all 55{,}630 candidates, 39.5; $\Delta{=}{+}8.2$, CI $[+6.5,+9.9]$).
Counts here are over the full flagged partition the downstream models were trained on, and so exceed the paired-subset counts in Table~\ref{tab:agent_behavior}.
Because the curated evaluation reference is itself SAM-derived, it cannot credit genuine recoveries (see Evaluation Protocol), so part of this cost is an artifact of the reference rather than the annotations.
The remainder tracks candidate-set precision: LLaVA's confirmed set is large and barely selective, keeping 45{,}882 of 55{,}630 candidates, and it lands statistically alongside the unconfirmed case (39.5 vs.\ 40.5, CI $[-0.0,+1.9]$, not clearly separable), which is the expected result for a confirmation gate that discards under a fifth of what it is shown.
We previously placed a much more selective confirmed set at the other end of this ordering and reported that it retained most of Swin-only's advantage; that set was the artifact of Section~\ref{par:serving} and the claim is withdrawn.
On the instrumented re-run Qwen confirms these candidates at a rate close to LLaVA's (Table~\ref{tab:agent_behavior}), so a second model no longer supplies a selective gate at all.
Testing whether genuine selectivity recovers Swin-only performance therefore requires an explicitly thresholded discovery variant rather than a second VLM, and we identify that as the direct follow-up to this result.

\textbf{The triage limitation fixes show no significant downstream effect at this scale.}
Section~\ref{sec:experiments} identifies two triage weaknesses from qualitative inspection: unilateral rejection on a bare \texttt{background} verdict, and a LiDAR support threshold that is not class-aware.
Applying both fixes (\texttt{background\_protected} and \texttt{lidar\_class\_aware}) rescues 1{,}002 masks (914{,}277 pixels: 892 signs, 66 cyclists, 37 pedestrians, 7 vehicles) from rejection under offline replay, matching the classes and mechanism of the qualitative examples in Fig.~\ref{fig:limitations}.
Retraining LLaVA's Swin + disc.\ variant with the fixed triage rule, holding the discovery set constant, gives 39.1 mIoU versus 40.5 for the unfixed rule ($\Delta{=}{-}1.2$, CI $[-2.3,+0.1]$), not a significant difference.
The rescued masks are a small fraction of the 90{,}761-mask training set, and their effect may be swamped by the same discovery-volume cost identified above; isolating the triage fix from a no-discovery baseline is left to future work.

\textbf{Rule ablations: concordance protects rare classes at an mIoU cost.}
Rejecting on any single negative signal (disjunctive reject) collapses LLaVA's human-class IoU from 38.7 to 26.5, deleting exactly the safety-relevant masks the concordance rule is designed to protect, at an overall mIoU that is lower than the concordance rule's (42.2 vs.\ 44.5).
We therefore retain concordance as the safe default and report its mIoU cost transparently.
Whether a more permissive VLM inverts this trade-off, as the superseded Qwen rows suggested, is exactly the question the retraining above will settle, and we do not claim it here.
Uniform versus class-aware Swin thresholds are downstream-indistinguishable (LLaVA: 44.6 vs.\ 44.5, within CI); class-aware thresholds are retained for mask-level recall on cyclists and pedestrians rather than for any downstream gain.

\textbf{Fixed prompts do not transfer neutrally, and the divergence is prompt-specific.}
Table~\ref{tab:agent_behavior} compares per-VLM agent outputs under identical prompts on the paired set of Section~\ref{par:comparability}: 3{,}535 frames and 73{,}708 masks with verified-identical Swin, LiDAR, and candidate inputs.

On mask verification, Qwen is the more permissive model.
It returns \texttt{valid} on 71.8\% of crops against LLaVA's 62.1\% as-recorded, and by the bound of~\eqref{eq:default_bound} the gap on answered masks is at least 5.3 points in the same direction.
The divergence is not a uniform shift, however, but a redistribution across classes: relative to LLaVA, Qwen rejects far fewer signs (10.8\% vs.\ 26.7\%) and cyclists (33.8\% vs.\ 48.1\%), yet substantially \emph{more} vehicles (16.2\% vs.\ 5.0\%) and pedestrians (35.1\% vs.\ 17.3\%).
The two models agree on only 56.8\% of masks, so identical prompts leave them disagreeing on well over four crops in ten.
Neither ordering is uniformly more selective, which is the practical point: a single aggregate acceptance rate does not predict which classes a model will protect.

On discovery, the two models are close in aggregate and far apart on one prompt.
Given the same 45{,}669 Swin-proposed candidates, they confirm at nearly the same rate over the candidates they answered, 82.5\% for Qwen against 83.9\% for LLaVA, and Qwen is in fact marginally more permissive on the vehicle (87.5\% vs.\ 83.3\%) and sign (89.1\% vs.\ 84.2\%) prompts.
The entire divergence sits in the third prompt, the one that must disambiguate a human candidate into cyclist or pedestrian: Qwen confirms 43.5\% of those candidates against LLaVA's 84.9\%, yielding 2{,}497 human objects against 4{,}777 from the identical candidate stream.
This localisation matters more than a pooled rate would suggest.
The failing prompt is the only one carrying a three-way discrimination rather than a presence check, and it governs the two classes the pipeline is least able to afford losing.
A portability check that reports one aggregate confirmation rate per model would have scored these two models as near-equivalent on discovery and missed a 41-point deficit concentrated exactly on cyclists and pedestrians.

We previously reported this comparison as a near-unconditional acceptance rate of 93.3\% paired with a 17.8\% discovery confirmation rate, and read the pattern as a coherent behavioural profile in which permissiveness in triage traded off against scepticism in discovery.
Both figures were products of the serving fault of Section~\ref{par:serving}, in opposite directions, and neither survived re-annotation.
The corrected picture is narrower and less tidy: prompts transfer imperfectly, the imperfection is concentrated rather than global, and its sign varies by class and by prompt.
That the artifact was more interesting than the truth is itself the cautionary result, and it is the reason we now report the safe-default share next to every rate.

\textbf{Within-model prompt sensitivity is currently unmeasured.}
The cross-model divergence above could in principle reflect stable model dispositions rather than prompt effects, and separating the two requires holding the model fixed while changing only the prompt.
A development-stage revision of the prompt set previously supplied that test, over a 1{,}000-frame run of Qwen2.5-VL-72B under the original prompts, and we reported that the revision flipped a quarter of Qwen's BBox verdicts.
That run was produced during the same period as the discarded campaign and roughly a third of its frames were served degenerate responses, so its rates are diluted by safe defaults in the same way and to an unknown degree per prompt; the stored results have since been deleted and are not recoverable.
We therefore withdraw the within-model prompt-sensitivity result rather than restate corruption-diluted numbers, and the corresponding table is removed from this revision.
Re-running the original prompts over the same frames under the health monitor is inexpensive relative to the main campaign, and restoring this control is the one experiment still outstanding for the portability claim: without it, the paper establishes that fixed prompts transfer unevenly across models but cannot attribute that unevenness to the prompts as opposed to the models.
The discovery evidence above is nonetheless suggestive on this point, since the divergence is confined to a single prompt while the other two behave almost identically across models, which is difficult to explain as a global model disposition.

Table~\ref{tab:agent_performance} quantifies signal agreement using Swin quality as the reference on the 72{,}989 paired masks with Swin scores available (geometry-prefiltered masks are excluded).
LLaVA achieves balanced precision and recall (71.8 / 71.4, F1 71.6), indicating selective verdicts that align with Swin.
Qwen is the higher-recall, lower-precision judge (78.5 / 68.3, F1 73.0), consistent with the more permissive verdict distribution in Table~\ref{tab:agent_behavior}; restricting Qwen to answered masks raises both precision and accuracy (75.1 and 67.3), which quantifies how much of its apparent permissiveness was the safe-default substitution rather than the model.
Under the discarded run the same measurement returned 95.5\% recall at 64.3\% precision, a profile that is close to what a judge answering \texttt{valid} unconditionally would produce, and in retrospect that near-degenerate signature was the clearest warning available in the aggregate statistics.
The LiDAR consistency check behaves similarly (Prec 64.2, Rec 97.0, F1 77.3): it reliably passes Swin-valid masks but rarely rejects Swin-invalid ones, since it detects only sky or void leaks rather than semantic misclassification.
Cross-VLM BBox agreement is 56.8\%, meaning the two models disagree on more than four in ten masks despite using identical prompts.

\subsection{Cross-Dataset Generalisation}

To assess whether annotation quality affects the transferability of learned representations, we fine-tune CLFTv2-Base CrossFusion on the iSEAuto dataset starting from three initialisations: ImageNet weights only (scratch), and ZOD-trained checkpoints from raw SAM and Swin + disc.\ (no VLM).
All runs use a reset learning rate; the comparison focuses on \emph{convergence speed}: a checkpoint encoding higher-quality semantic features should reach a given validation mIoU in fewer epochs.
iSEAuto contains vehicle and human classes only (no sign); mIoU is averaged over those two classes.

Figure~\ref{fig:iseauto_convergence} shows the full convergence trajectory (5-epoch trailing-smoothed mIoU, sampled every 5 epochs from epoch 10 to 90) using the exact-mask (\texttt{\_ccm}) Swin + disc.\ checkpoint.
Ranking the three initialisations at each of the 17 sampled epochs, ZOD raw SAM finishes last at 15 of 17 points (mean rank 2.9 of 3), while Swin + disc.\ finishes first at 10 of 17 (mean rank 1.4) and scratch (ImageNet only) is second (mean rank 1.7).
The gap is consistent rather than concentrated at one epoch: averaged over epochs 10--30, Swin + disc.\ leads scratch by $+0.4$ mIoU and raw SAM by $+1.4$; over epochs 35--65 it trails scratch slightly ($-0.3$) but still leads raw SAM by $+0.7$; over epochs 70--90 it again leads both ($+0.1$ and $+0.9$).
Raw SAM trails scratch throughout, by $+0.8$ to $+1.0$ mIoU in every phase, indicating that pretraining on unfiltered SAM pseudo-labels can transfer \emph{worse} than no ZOD pretraining at all.
All three converge to within 1 mIoU of each other by epoch 90 ($\approx$67).
The direction is consistent with the ZOD ablation (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}): Swin filtering, which also gives the best ZOD-domain mIoU, is the only initialisation that is never the worst performer on transfer, while raw, unfiltered pseudo-labels are consistently the worst initialisation for transfer despite scoring above Swin + disc.\ on the ZOD-domain reference itself.
This single-run comparison cannot rule out training-run variance at any individual epoch; the consistency of raw SAM's shortfall across 15 of 17 sampled points and all three training phases is what supports the directional claim.

\subsection{Qualitative Analysis}

Fig.~\ref{fig:qualitative} walks through five situations the pipeline actually ran into on the flagged partition (LLaVA-1.6-34B), showing not just what each agent decided but why.

\textbf{(a) Hallucination.} Sometimes SAM proposes a mask where the labeled object simply isn't there, and the BBox agent catches it by returning \texttt{background}. One mask claims a \texttt{vehicle} inside a dense hedge; the other claims a \texttt{sign} on a gantry's bare steel support lattice; the real sign panel is a few pixels away, just outside the mask. Both are correctly rejected.

\textbf{(b) Consistency failure.} A parked bicycle is a real object: the BBox agent says \texttt{valid} and Swin agrees (\texttt{quality=good}, 82\% agreement), yet only 9.1\% of its mask carries a LiDAR return, short of the 10\% threshold, so the Consistency Check fails. The bottom crop shows why: a bicycle's thin wire frame reflects far fewer LiDAR beams than a solid object of the same size, so most beams pass straight through the mask and hit the wall behind it instead (bright, dense returns on the wall; sparse returns inside the green outline). Since this pattern is indistinguishable from a genuine sky leak, the pipeline can't just accept the mask, but it also should not delete a real bicycle, so it sends the case to the Correction Agent to refine instead of rejecting.

\textbf{(c) Depth-support rejection.} A pedestrian-shaped statue on a plinth fools the BBox VLM into calling it \texttt{valid}, but the other two signals disagree with it and with each other's certainty. Swin's own dense prediction sees no person at all (0\% agreement with \texttt{human}; it reads the statue's rigid, single-material, post-mounted shape as sign or background instead), and only 8.4\% of the mask has LiDAR support. Two votes against one are enough to reject the mask, overriding the VLM's mistake; this is the concordance rule doing its job, catching an error that a single judge would have missed. The bottom crop is a simpler case: SAM has merged a real car's roofline with a large blurred artifact into one mask, and here all three agents agree it is bad, so there is no disagreement to resolve.

\textbf{(d) Agent disagreement (human review).} Three separate road signs get merged into one SAM mask. The BBox agent sees an oddly-shaped compound object and calls it \texttt{invalid}, but the Quality Agent recognizes the pixels as genuinely sign-like (\texttt{quality=good}) and disagrees. Rather than trusting either judge alone, the downgrade rule from Section~\ref{sec:methodology} sends the mask to a human instead of deleting real sign pixels. The bottom crop shows the same pattern on a bike rack: several parked bicycles are merged into one \texttt{cyclist} mask, nobody is riding so BBox again says \texttt{invalid}, but Swin scores it \texttt{good} (92\% agreement; ZOD labels parked bikes as cyclists too), so it is again escalated to a human rather than discarded.

\textbf{(e) Discovery.} SAM missed both of these people entirely: a cyclist carrying a child, and a pedestrian standing by a tree, so neither ever had a mask to judge in the first place. Instead, they were found by watching where Swin's own dense, per-pixel prediction disagrees with SAM: the cyan overlay is Swin confidently predicting \texttt{human} pixels that SAM left as background. Once enough of that disagreement clusters together (the orange box), it is cropped out and shown to the VLM, which confirms the two objects as \texttt{cyclist} and \texttt{pedestrian}. In the bottom crop, the recovered mask traces the pedestrian's outline so tightly it could pass for a hand-drawn label, showing how precisely a missed object can be localized before a VLM ever sees it.

\textbf{Limitations observed.} Curating these examples surfaced two systematic weaknesses that the aggregate metrics of Table~\ref{tab:ablation} do not expose, illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:limitations}. First, a \texttt{background} verdict is treated as two negative signals and triggers immediate, irreversible rejection with no human-review fallback, unlike the \texttt{invalid}+\texttt{good} downgrade rule that protects panel (d) of Fig.~\ref{fig:qualitative}. This proved risky in practice: several masks over real, correctly-shaped objects in our candidate pool, including a legible road sign at night (Fig.~\ref{fig:limitations}b) and a clearly visible bus (Fig.~\ref{fig:limitations}a), were rejected purely because the BBox agent misread a dark or motion-blurred crop as empty, even though Swin's independent quality score was high for the sign case (0.605 agreement, above threshold). A single VLM misjudgment on a real object is therefore sufficient to silently discard a valid label, undermining the safety guarantee the concordance rule otherwise provides.
Second, the three-signal design assumes Swin, the VLM, and LiDAR consistency fail independently, but several rejected masks show correlated failure instead: sparse or dot-matrix objects, an LED matrix road sign (Fig.~\ref{fig:limitations}c) and a pedestrian captured only from the waist up at close range (Fig.~\ref{fig:limitations}d), drove both Swin agreement and LiDAR support to near zero simultaneously, even though the BBox agent correctly judged them \texttt{valid}. Because rejection requires only two of three signals, a shared physical cause, low solid-surface fraction within the mask, can defeat the concordance requirement rather than being caught by it, and correctly proposed, safety-relevant sign and human masks are lost as a result. Both weaknesses are correctable with rule changes, exempting the \texttt{background} verdict from unilateral rejection when Swin agreement is high, and making the LiDAR support threshold class-aware, and Section~\ref{sec:experiments} evaluates exactly these fixes: they rescue 1{,}002 masks dominated by the classes shown here, but produce no significant downstream mIoU change at this scale (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}, Triage (fixed) + disc.). The examples in Fig.~\ref{fig:limitations} thus document a real precision failure of the triage rule whose repair is worthwhile for label safety even though its effect is not measurable in aggregate downstream metrics.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This work evaluates a multi-agent refinement pipeline for SAM-based annotation in autonomous driving, combining a domain-adapted Swin Transformer with fixed-prompt visual--LLM agents and deterministic decision rules. Across experiments on ZOD, the Swin Quality Agent, trained on a small clean partition, provides a stable signal for both mask triage and object discovery, enabling refinement with a single forward pass per frame and supporting fully reproducible offline ablations.

Under a single curated evaluation reference with paired bootstrap confidence intervals, Swin-only filtering is the strongest annotation strategy: pseudo-labels from 4{,}135 flagged frames filtered by the Swin agreement signal outperform a curated SAM baseline trained on 2{,}319 clean frames ($\Delta{=}{+}3.3$ mIoU, 95\% CI $[+1.5,+5.5]$), while adding VLM verdicts to the triage rule yields no significant further gain under the fixed-prompt configuration studied here.
Annotation volume combined with a cheap domain-adapted quality signal therefore compensates for initial label noise without any VLM inference.
Swin-driven discovery recovers thousands of objects SAM missed, and its downstream cost tracks confirmed candidate-set volume and precision rather than mask geometry: a large, barely selective confirmed set trails Swin-only filtering nearly as much as adding every unconfirmed candidate does.
Because the curated reference is itself SAM-derived and cannot credit genuine recoveries, some of this cost is an artifact of evaluation rather than annotation quality; resolving the two apart requires human-verified evaluation labels, which we identify as the most important next step.

Applying identical prompts to Qwen2.5-VL-72B shows that fixed prompts do not transfer neutrally between open VLMs, and that the failure is localised rather than global. Qwen is the more permissive mask judge, by at least 5.3 points on masks both models answered, but it redistributes rather than uniformly relaxes its rejections: it rejects fewer signs and cyclists than LLaVA and more vehicles and pedestrians, and the two models agree on only 56.8\% of crops. On discovery they confirm the same candidate stream at almost the same aggregate rate, with the entire divergence concentrated in the single prompt that disambiguates cyclist from pedestrian, where Qwen confirms 43.5\% against LLaVA's 84.9\%. Portability validation must therefore be per-prompt and per-class: one aggregate acceptance rate per model would have scored these two as near-equivalent on discovery while missing a large deficit on exactly the two safety-critical classes.

The most transferable lesson of this work is a negative one about measurement. An earlier version of this comparison reported a far cleaner story, in which the same prompts elicited near-unconditional acceptance from Qwen alongside strong discovery scepticism. Both halves were produced by a serving fault that returned content-free responses which each agent silently replaced with its safe default, in opposite directions on the two tasks, across 72\,\% of frames. Nothing in the pipeline's exit status, its availability checks, or its aggregate verdict distributions distinguished that run from a healthy one, and the resulting numbers were more coherent and more publishable than the corrected ones. We therefore recommend that VLM-as-judge pipelines monitor individual response content rather than service availability, record per-call parse outcomes so that a substituted default is never reported as a judgement, and treat a verdict distribution approaching the pipeline's own default as a fault signature rather than a finding.

Cross-dataset transfer to iSEAuto shows that ZOD pretraining on raw, unfiltered SAM pseudo-labels transfers \emph{worse} than no ZOD pretraining at all: it trails ImageNet-only scratch initialisation at 15 of 17 sampled epochs, while Swin-filtered pretraining is never the worst of the three and leads at 10 of 17; all three converge to the same final level by epoch 90. Annotation quality therefore does not merely affect in-domain ZOD mIoU, poor pseudo-labels can actively harm downstream transfer relative to using no domain-specific pretraining at all, which is a practical risk for any pipeline that pretrains on unfiltered pseudo-labels. The architecture is backend-agnostic and suitable for local deployment. Future work includes human-verified evaluation labels, multi-seed replication of the transfer comparison, evaluation on additional datasets, adaptive discovery thresholds, and extensions to panoptic or temporal segmentation.
