\begin{abstract}
The Segment Anything Model (SAM) makes large-scale pseudo-labeling feasible for autonomous-driving perception, but its masks drift at boundaries, hallucinate regions, and miss small objects, with errors concentrated in safety-critical cyclist and pedestrian classes.
Vision--language models (VLMs) are increasingly proposed to verify such labels, yet their accuracy is almost always established by agreement with another automated signal rather than against human judgement, which cannot say which of two disagreeing judges is right.

We study this on the Zenseact Open Dataset (ZOD) with a pipeline whose signals are deliberately independent: a domain-adapted Swin Transformer trained on a curated partition scores SAM mask quality, a zero-shot VLM supplies an object-level verdict on tight crops, and a Swin-driven discovery module proposes objects SAM missed entirely.
Two open-weight VLMs are run under identical fixed prompts.
The evaluation rests on a human pass over the flagged partition in which every SAM proposal and every discovery candidate in a frame is judged directly, so each automated signal is scored against human verdicts rather than against another model.
Downstream effect is measured by training a camera--LiDAR fusion model on each annotation variant.

We report four results.
First, mask verification cannot improve a pseudo-label set for free: because the fraction of masks that are actually wrong is small, every rejection rule, from a single-signal threshold to a concordance requirement across independent signals, raises label purity only by discarding correct labels, and the rules differ only in where they sit on that trade-off.
Rule engineering within a fixed signal set moves this operating point very little; the operating point depends far more on which signals are consulted and how their disagreement is resolved.
Second, the criterion matters more than the rule: because a retained wrong mask injects wrong pixels while a deleted correct mask only omits them, pooled metrics such as F1 rank these rules in the opposite order to their downstream effect, rewarding whichever rule keeps the most labels.
Scored on the two error directions separately, a free deterministic segmentation signal dominates zero-shot VLM triage on the direction that matters, at a fraction of the cost.
Third, neither half of the pipeline earns its inference budget: removing masks is bounded by how few are wrong, and confirming candidate objects the proposal stage never generated adds only a few points of precision on genuinely missed objects while carrying no information at all on the boundary fringes that dominate the candidate pool. Downstream, the free deterministic quality signal is worth $+3.9$ mIoU over unfiltered SAM labels while object discovery costs $9.4$, and human deletion of 5.3\,\% of the object pixels --- at fixed training-set size --- is worth $+2.5$, so label purity rather than label volume is what binds.
Fourth, cross-model agreement is not a proxy for accuracy: the ranking of the two VLMs inverts when they are scored against human verdicts instead of against each other, the aggregate ordering is set by the class composition of the corpus rather than by competence, and prompt portability fails per-prompt rather than uniformly.
We close with the cost accounting the human reference makes possible: at this model scale, verifying a frame with a VLM consumes several times more machine time than a human needs to verify the same frame by hand, which reframes where verification effort should be spent.
All decisions trace to explicit agent outputs with no learned fusion weights, so every variant is reproducible from stored responses without re-running VLM inference.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Pixel-level annotations for autonomous driving are expensive, yet the Segment Anything Model (SAM) \cite{kirillov2023sam} has made large-scale pseudo-labeling feasible.
The challenge is reliability: SAM masks drift at boundaries, hallucinate in texturally uniform regions, and miss small traffic participants, with errors concentrated in safety-critical cyclists, pedestrians, and signs.
Geometric filtering approaches \cite{zou2019confidence,lee2013pseudo,jaritz2020xmuda} cannot detect such semantic failures: a mask with a plausible shape but the wrong class label passes every geometric check and degrades downstream training.
Vision–language models (VLMs) \cite{liu2024llava,wang2024qwen2vl} offer richer semantic priors, but their effectiveness for SAM mask refinement, especially on small object crops and multimodal camera–LiDAR inputs, remains unclear.

A further difficulty is how such filtering is usually validated. A VLM judge is typically scored by how far it agrees with the segmentation signal it is meant to complement, which measures concordance between two automated judges rather than the accuracy of either: when they disagree, agreement statistics cannot say which one is right, and when they fail together they look consistent.

We study this problem using a semi-supervised pipeline on the Zenseact Open Dataset (ZOD) \cite{alibeigi2023zod}.
A clean partition of curated frames trains a domain-adapted Swin Transformer \cite{liu2021swin} that provides per-pixel class agreement scores for every SAM mask.
A zero-shot VLM supplies an independent object-level verdict on tight crops.
Rejection requires concordance between multiple signals (Swin agreement, VLM verdict, and a deterministic LiDAR consistency check), reducing the risk that any single erroneous judge silently removes valid labels.
A Swin-driven discovery module identifies objects missed by SAM, so the pipeline addresses false positives and false negatives through separate mechanisms, which we find are not equally productive.
To score every signal against human judgement rather than against the other signals, we verify the entire flagged partition by hand: each SAM proposal and each discovery candidate is judged directly, in one pass, without the labeller being told which model produced what.
Downstream impact is then measured by training CLFTv2 \cite{gu2024clft}, a camera–LiDAR fusion model, on each annotation variant.
Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{a human-verified reference for SAM pseudo-labels}, in which every mask proposal and every discovery candidate of the flagged partition is judged by a human, with each region carrying the decision every automated signal made about it, so one labelling pass scores all of them and attribution is performed in software rather than asked of the labeller,
    \item \textbf{a characterisation of mask verification as a purity--volume trade-off}: because the proportion of pseudo-labels that are actually wrong is small, rejection rules raise label purity by discarding correct labels, and we quantify where each rule sits on that trade-off,
    \item \textbf{an error-budget criterion for annotation rules}, separating masks wrongly kept from masks wrongly deleted, which we show ranks rules in the opposite order to pooled metrics such as F1 and in the same order as their downstream effect,
    \item \textbf{evidence that neither half of a verification pipeline earns its inference budget} on this data: removal is bounded by how few proposals are wrong, and confirmation of objects the proposal stage never generated adds only a few points of precision on genuinely missed objects and none on the boundary fringes that dominate the candidate pool,
    \item \textbf{a controlled cross-model comparison} of two open VLMs under identical fixed prompts, scored against human verdicts rather than against each other, showing that agreement between models is not evidence of accuracy and that prompt portability fails per-prompt rather than uniformly,
    \item \textbf{a downstream study} under a single reference with paired bootstrap confidence intervals, quantifying how each annotation variant affects camera–LiDAR fusion performance beyond mask-level agreement,
    \item and \textbf{a cost accounting against human effort}, which the human reference makes possible: we compare per-frame VLM inference time against the time a human takes to verify the same frame, and quantify how much of the annotation error a ranked review queue reaches for a given verification budget.
\end{itemize}





\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

\subsection{Semantic Segmentation and Pseudo-Labeling in Autonomous Driving}

Semantic segmentation underpins scene understanding in autonomous driving, with camera-based architectures such as FCN, PSPNet, and DeepLab \cite{long2015fcn,zhao2017psp,chen2018deeplab} establishing dense prediction baselines and later work extending these to camera--LiDAR fusion through feature-level alignment \cite{valada2019selfsupervised,jaritz2019multimodal}. 
Despite strong performance on established benchmarks, these models depend on pixel-accurate supervision that is expensive to collect at scale.

Pseudo-labeling and self-training methods \cite{lee2013pseudo,zou2019confidence} reduce labeling cost by using model predictions as supervision, while cross-modal consistency constraints \cite{jaritz2020xmuda} and LiDAR-projection checks \cite{behley2019semantickitti,caesar2020nuscenes} leverage geometric redundancy to filter erroneous labels. 
These approaches improve annotation efficiency but rely on fixed thresholds and cannot detect semantic errors: a mask with geometrically plausible shape but an incorrect class label passes all geometric checks and degrades downstream training. 
Rare classes such as cyclists and pedestrians, which occupy small pixel regions and appear infrequently, remain particularly challenging for pseudo-labeling pipelines.

\subsection{Foundation Models for Scalable Annotation}

The Segment Anything Model (SAM) \cite{kirillov2023sam} has changed the economics of mask generation by enabling class-agnostic proposals without per-class prompts or labeled training data. 
This has motivated large-scale pseudo-annotation pipelines for autonomous driving, where SAM proposals are matched to semantic categories using sensor overlays or downstream classifiers. 
However, SAM's class-agnostic design means it cannot verify the semantic correctness of its own output. 
Boundary drift arises where object edges are ambiguous in RGB; hallucinated regions appear in texturally uniform areas such as sky and vegetation; and small objects are frequently missed when their footprint falls below SAM's effective resolution. 
Post-hoc semantic validation is therefore a necessary complement to SAM-based annotation.

\subsection{Vision--Language Models and Multi-Signal Filtering}

Vision--language models (VLMs) combine image encoders with autoregressive language decoders, enabling structured reasoning over visual content \cite{liu2024llava,ye2023mplug,wang2024qwen2vl}. 
The LLM-as-judge paradigm \cite{zheng2023judging} shows that language models can evaluate the quality of other models' outputs when given structured prompts and constrained output formats, providing a scalable alternative to human review in certain settings. 
In annotation pipelines, this suggests VLMs may serve as semantic judges over mask proposals, verifying object identity and flagging implausible regions, without requiring fine-tuning on task-specific labels.

A complementary line of work studies ensemble and multi-signal filtering for label-noise reduction \cite{malach2017decoupling,han2018co}. 
Requiring agreement between independent classifiers before rejecting a sample reduces the risk that any single erroneous judge silently discards valid training examples. 
This principle motivates our concordance-based triage rule: rejection requires at least two negative signals among Swin agreement, VLM verdict, and LiDAR consistency, so that disagreement escalates the mask rather than deleting it. 
Prior work has not applied such concordance-based filtering to SAM mask validation over camera--LiDAR inputs, nor compared pixel-level segmentation and zero-shot VLM signals as independent quality judges within a unified annotation pipeline.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

The system refines SAM-generated masks using a set of specialized visual--LLM agents and a domain-adapted segmentation model. 
Most agents receive a multimodal input bundle consisting of an RGB crop, a mask overlay, a LiDAR depth projection, and structured metadata, and return a categorical decision. 
The BBox Agent is an exception and receives only the RGB crop to reduce scene-level bias. 
All agent outputs feed into a deterministic triage rule designed for safety-critical annotation workflows.

\subsection{Multimodal Input Construction}

For each SAM proposal, we extract a tight crop around the bounding box and generate three aligned channels: the RGB crop, the mask overlay, and a projected LiDAR depth map. 
Metadata describing geometric properties and LiDAR support is provided as structured text. 
Object-scale cropping is essential: small traffic participants often occupy only 10--30 pixels, and full-frame VLM queries struggle to resolve them reliably. 
Each crop is upscaled to at least $224{\times}224$ pixels and processed independently; the full frame is used only as optional context.

\subsection{Visual--LLM Agents}

The system decomposes mask assessment into three VLM agents (BBox, Failure--Mode, and Correction), one Swin-based quality agent, and one deterministic geometric check. 
Per-mask execution order is: Consistency Check, BBox Agent, Swin agreement, then Correction on the refine path only.

All VLM agents use a single fixed prompt per role, applied identically to both LLaVA-1.6-34B and Qwen2.5-VL-72B without any model-specific calibration.
This reflects the realistic deployment constraint of large-scale annotation pipelines, where per-model prompt tuning is infeasible, and it enables a direct test of how well fixed prompts transfer across VLMs of different architectures and capability levels.

\paragraph{Consistency Check}
A deterministic LiDAR-support test is run first, before any VLM call: the fraction of mask pixels with projected LiDAR returns must exceed $\tau{=}0.1$. 
This check reliably detects masks leaking into sky or void regions at zero inference cost.

\paragraph{Bounding-Box Agent}
Performs blind forced-choice classification over a fixed vocabulary of object classes and distractors. 
The agent is not told the expected class to avoid agreement bias. 
Traffic lights and pole-mounted road lighting are included in the vocabulary and mapped to the \texttt{sign} class following ZOD annotation conventions. 
Outputs are: \texttt{valid}, \texttt{background}, or \texttt{invalid}. 
The agent sees only the RGB crop; overlays and depth channels are withheld to reduce the risk of classifying surrounding context rather than the marked object.

\paragraph{Quality Agent}
Evaluates mask correctness using a Swin Transformer \cite{liu2021swin} trained on clean ZOD frames. 
A single forward pass produces a per-pixel class map for the entire frame; per-mask agreement~$\alpha$ is computed from that shared prediction without an additional forward pass. 
Because Swin achieves higher mIoU on large objects than on cyclists and pedestrians, we use class-specific thresholds: $\tau_q^{\text{large}}{=}0.30$ and $\tau_q^{\text{small}}{=}0.15$; this choice targets mask-level recall on rare classes, and its downstream effect is ablated in Table~\ref{tab:ablation}.
For ablation studies, all agents are called on every mask to enable direct signal comparison; bypass thresholds ($0.70$ for large classes, $0.40$ for small classes) are used only for deployment efficiency and are not applied during evaluation.

\paragraph{Correction Agent}
Called only on the refine path (\texttt{bbox=valid}, $\alpha{\geq}\tau_q$, \texttt{consistency=fail}).
Determines whether the mask can be improved and proposes a single refinement step (\texttt{refine} or \texttt{no\_refine}).
This is the only agent whose prompt failed to transfer.
Qwen2.5-VL-72B returned an empty response to all 1{,}449 correction calls in the paired frame set, so every one fell back to \texttt{no\_refine} and the refine path never fired, whereas LLaVA answered 1{,}304 of 1{,}311 calls with \texttt{refine}.
We report this as a null result and make no refine-path claim for Qwen.
The prompt differs from the others in requesting a judgement about a hypothetical corrective action rather than a description of visible content; this is a plausible but untested explanation.

\paragraph{Failure-Mode Agent}
Classifies the dominant error type (\texttt{boundary\_drift}, \texttt{hallucination}, \texttt{occlusion\_miss}, \texttt{fragmentation}). 
Diagnostic only; does not affect triage outcomes.

All VLM agents return constrained categorical outputs to ensure reproducibility.

\subsection{Swin-Based Object Discovery}

Triage removes false positives but cannot detect objects SAM missed. 
We therefore introduce a discovery step that compares the Swin class map to the SAM annotation at $384{\times}384$ resolution. 
Pixels where Swin predicts a non-background class but SAM predicts background form candidate regions. 
Connected components below a minimum size are discarded; the largest remaining components (up to $K_{\text{disc}}$ per frame) are cropped and verified by a VLM. 
For vehicles and signs, the VLM confirms presence; for humans, it also disambiguates cyclist vs.\ pedestrian. 
Confirmed regions are added as new masks, modifying only pixels still labeled as background.

\subsection{Candidate Geometry: Standalone Recoveries and Boundary Fringes}
Because SAM's existing coverage is subtracted before components are formed, a discovery candidate on an
object SAM already found is only the rim left over, whereas a candidate on an object SAM missed entirely
is the whole object as Swin sees it. These are different findings, and we separate them geometrically:
a candidate whose surrounding ring is largely SAM-covered is a \emph{fringe}, and one whose ring is free
is \emph{standalone}. Over the 55{,}252 candidates in this corpus, 16{,}402 (29.7\,\%) are standalone and
38{,}850 (70.3\,\%) are fringes. Precision on standalone candidates measures what discovery recovers;
precision on fringes measures boundary completion on objects the pipeline already had. We report the two
separately throughout, since a pooled rate is dominated by fringes and would read as a recall gain.

\subsection{Human Verification of the Annotation Set}
\label{sec:human_verification}
Mask agreement and downstream mIoU are both indirect measures of annotation quality: the first compares
two automated signals to each other, the second passes the annotations through a training run before
anything is observed. We therefore verify the annotation set directly. Every SAM proposal and every
discovery candidate on all 4{,}110 flagged frames, 145{,}428 regions in total, is presented to a human in
a single pass, painted as pixels in the frame it belongs to, and the human marks the errors.
The job was built over the entire partition and the pass is closed at \humancoverage, so the
measurements below are a sample of that job rather than a census of it.
Labelling proceeds in job order, which is not stratified by scene content, so rather than assume the
sample is random we test the realised subset against the frames never reached. It is not
distinguishable from a random draw of the same size on weather condition (permutation test,
$p{=}0.36$), on the presence of cyclist or pedestrian pixels ($p{=}0.47$), or on
annotated pixel volume per frame ($p{=}0.47$); the class composition of the annotated pixels
matches to within $0.9$ points on every class. Rates measured on it therefore estimate the
partition, and all confidence intervals resample frames rather than masks, since masks within a frame
share a scene and a labeller's calibration.

One property of the reference does not average out, and we report it here rather than leave it for a
reader to discover. The standard is \emph{non-stationary}: the share of SAM proposals the labeller
accepts falls monotonically with labelling order, by 2.25 points per 100 frames ($r{=}-0.902$), and the
second half of the pass is steeper than the first rather than converging.
It is a change of standard, not of material --- scene composition, mask size and mean Swin agreement are
flat across the pass, yet within every fixed band of Swin agreement the acceptance rate still falls,
from 95.9\,\% to 91.6\,\% on the most confident band and from 65.2\,\% to 41.8\,\% on the least.
Two consequences follow, and we apply both throughout. Absolute rates pooled over the pass are a
mixture over a moving standard, so wherever an absolute rate carries an argument we give it for the
final 200 frames as well, which is the current calibration. Paired comparisons are immune, because
both sides are judged by the same person on the same mask at the same moment, and every ranking in
this paper rests on a paired comparison rather than on an absolute rate.

The labeller is never told which model produced which region, and does not need to be.
Mask proposals are extracted deterministically, so both VLM runs scored the same proposals and a
human verdict on a mask is run-independent. Each region carries the decision every automated source made
about it, so one pass scores all of them; attribution is a join performed afterwards rather than a
question put to the labeller. This also removes an order effect, since a labeller who knew which model
kept a mask could not judge it neutrally, and it makes the human judgement reusable if further annotation
variants are added later.

The resulting verdicts serve two purposes. They give a direct precision measurement for each automated
decision, reported in Table~\ref{tab:human_agreement}, in which a false accept (wrong pixels retained)
and a false reject (correct pixels deleted) are kept apart rather than pooled into one accuracy, since
their costs to a downstream model are not symmetric. They also define a human-verified annotation set that
is itself trainable, which turns the ablation from a comparison among automated variants into a
comparison against the labels a human would have produced on the same frames.

\subsection{Risk-Aware Deterministic Triage}

Instead of weighted scoring, we use a deterministic rule requiring concordant evidence for rejection. 
Rejection is destructive: deleted pixels cannot be recovered, so relying on any single negative signal would compound false positives across agents. 
Let $\mathcal{N}=\{\texttt{invalid},\texttt{bad},\texttt{fail}\}$ denote negative signals.

A mask is \textbf{rejected} if at least two signals in $\mathcal{N}$ are present. 
A \texttt{background} verdict counts as two signals and rejects immediately. 
To avoid over-rejection of small objects, when \texttt{bbox=invalid} but the Quality Agent scores the mask as \texttt{good}, the \texttt{invalid} verdict is downgraded to a single negative signal. 
The mask is then routed to human review when consistency passes, or rejected only when consistency additionally fails.

A mask is \textbf{accepted} if the BBox agent outputs \texttt{valid}, the Quality Agent outputs \texttt{good}, and the Consistency Check outputs \texttt{pass}.

A mask is routed to \textbf{refinement} if it is valid and of good quality but fails the Consistency Check and the Correction Agent recommends refinement.

All remaining cases, with exactly one dissenting judge, are sent to \textbf{human review}. 
Review masks are retained but flagged, ensuring that no single erroneous judgment silently deletes valid labels.

\subsection{Interpretability and Reproducibility}

All prompts, thresholds, and decision rules are fixed before evaluation. 
Every triage outcome can be traced to explicit agent outputs, and no learned fusion weights or continuous scores are used, supporting reproducibility across datasets and models.

\section{Experiments and Results}

We evaluate the proposed multi-agent annotation system on the Zenseact Open Dataset (ZOD) \cite{alibeigi2023zod} using SAM-generated mask proposals from our upstream annotation pipeline (released alongside this work; see Reproducibility below).
All agents return categorical decisions, and all triage outcomes follow the deterministic rules in Section~\ref{sec:methodology}. 
No continuous scores or weighted combinations are used.

This partition provides large-scale weak supervision for evaluating triage behavior, while a stratified subset of proposals is labeled at mask level for agent-level evaluation.

\paragraph{Computational Cost}
Table~\ref{tab:timing} reports inference time extracted from per-mask and per-frame timings logged during annotation, on the paired frame set of Section~\ref{par:comparability}.
The BBox agent dominates cost: a single crop verification call takes 3.54s (LLaVA) or 11.63s (Qwen) on average, and is issued once per mask, so per-mask latency scales almost linearly with model size.
The Correction agent, triggered only for the 1.5--1.8\% of masks that fail the LiDAR consistency check, adds a comparable per-call cost but touches few masks overall.
The Swin Quality Agent runs once per frame at negligible cost ($<$0.01s/mask), confirming that domain-adapted CNN triage is essentially free relative to VLM inference.
Annotating the paired 3{,}535 frames (73{,}708 masks) on a single A100 with 4 concurrent frame workers took 108.6 GPU-hours with LLaVA-1.6-34B versus 356.2 GPU-hours with Qwen2.5-VL-72B, a $3.3\times$ cost increase, or 27.1 against 89.0 wall-clock hours.
The Qwen figure is a deployed rather than a fault-free cost: it includes the retry and reload traffic generated by the 17 monitor-triggered restarts described above.
Against that, the larger model's triage verdicts are less selective, not more (Table~\ref{tab:agent_behavior}), and its confirmation rate on the same discovery candidates is marginally lower overall while differing sharply on one prompt, so the $3.3\times$ premium yields a different annotation distribution and not a better one on any axis we measure.
Estimated VRAM footprint (quantized weights plus a bounded KV cache at the deployed parallelism) is $\sim$22GB for LLaVA-1.6-34B versus $\sim$51GB for Qwen2.5-VL-72B, comfortably within the single A100-80GB used for both models.

\subsection{Cross-Model Comparability}
\label{par:comparability}
We take three measures to keep the two runs comparable.

First, all cross-model numbers are computed on the frames \emph{both} runs completed (3{,}535 frames, 73{,}708 masks), not on each run's full output.
The comparison is paired at mask level, and we verify that the two runs saw identical VLM-independent inputs on it: the same mask identifiers, bit-identical Swin agreement and LiDAR support values, and the same number of Swin-proposed discovery candidates in every frame.
Any verdict difference is therefore attributable to the VLM alone.
Triage outcomes are recomputed offline under the current deterministic rule for both runs, so a rule revision between campaigns cannot masquerade as a model difference.

Second, BBox verdict rates are reported \emph{as-recorded}, meaning that masks whose verdict is a substituted default are included, because that is the verdict the pipeline acted on and wrote into the annotations that train the downstream models.
For Qwen we additionally report the default share (13.3\,\% of masks) and the rate over answered masks only.
The equivalent decomposition is not available for LLaVA: raw BBox responses were never stored, only the parsed verdict, so its defaulted masks are indistinguishable offline from confidently valid ones and cannot be recovered without re-running the model.
Since the BBox \textsc{safe\_default} is \texttt{valid}, every defaulted mask is counted as valid, so any run's valid rate is a weighted mean of its answered rate and 100\,\%, giving
\begin{equation}
\text{valid}_{\text{answered}} \;\leq\; \text{valid}_{\text{as-recorded}}
\label{eq:default_bound}
\end{equation}
for \emph{any} run, whatever its default share.
LLaVA's answered-only valid rate is thus at most its as-recorded 62.1\,\%, while Qwen's is measured at 67.5\,\%.
Qwen is therefore the more permissive model on masks both models answered, by at least 5.3 points, and LLaVA's unmeasured defaults can only widen that gap.
The direction of the effect thus holds without re-running LLaVA, and only its magnitude remains a lower bound.

Third, discovery is compared on ground that is fully measured on both sides.
Both runs store each discovery response verbatim, so we re-derive the outcome for both with a single parser and separate four cases the stored \texttt{confirmed} flag collapses into one: the model named a class the prompt offered, the model declined (\texttt{other}, a genuine negative), the model replied outside the offered vocabulary, or no content was returned at all.
Confirmation rates are then taken over answered candidates only.
Pooling those cases is what made a degraded server look like a sceptical model.
Separating them also distinguishes model-side non-answering (out-of-vocabulary text: 1.8\,\% for LLaVA, 0.0\,\% for Qwen) from server-side non-answering (content-free replies: 0.0\,\% and 2.9\,\%), which have different causes and different interpretations.

One limitation remains: 25 of the 4{,}135 flagged frames could not be annotated cleanly and are excluded, leaving 4{,}110. The excluded set is not distinguishable from a random draw of the same size by mask count (permutation test, $p{=}0.30$), so the exclusion is not expected to bias the comparison.
The rates below are therefore provisional, and the downstream results in Table~\ref{tab:ablation} that depend on Qwen annotations predate the re-run entirely (see Section~\ref{sec:experiments}).


\subsection{Human Verification Results}
\label{sec:human_results}

The figures below are computed by a single script over the closed export
(\texttt{analyze\_human\_verification.py}), and cover \humancoverage.
Redundancy is one labeller per region by design --- the task is a click-what-is-wrong toggle rather
than an expert adjudication --- so no inter-annotator agreement statistic is available, and the
drift measured in Section~\ref{sec:human_verification} is the property of the reference we report in
its place.

\textbf{Most SAM proposals are already correct, which bounds what any filter can win.}
Humans accept 79.6\,\% of the SAM proposals they are shown (Table~\ref{tab:manual_verification}),
68.8\,\% under the final calibration.
A rejection rule therefore operates on a minority of genuinely wrong masks, and every
rule we measure buys purity by deleting correct labels as well. Table~\ref{tab:human_agreement} makes
the trade-off explicit by keeping the two error directions apart. Keeping every proposal has, by
construction, a 100\,\% false-accept rate and no false rejects; the Swin threshold cuts false
accepts to 23.5\,\% at the cost of deleting 26.7\,\% of the correct masks; VLM triage
sits at the opposite end, retaining 59.5\,\% (Qwen) and 54.9\,\% (LLaVA) of the masks a
human rejected while deleting 11.4\,\% and 14.1\,\% of the correct ones.
The Swin threshold is also the rule least disturbed by the drift. Between the first and last 200 frames
of the pass the base rate fell by 16.7 points, yet its two error rates moved by 2.0 and 6.2; the
no-triage baseline, by contrast, has an F1 equal to the base rate by construction and therefore tracks
the labeller's permissiveness one-for-one.

This is where a single accuracy figure misleads. On F1 the ranking is the reverse of the downstream
ranking: keeping everything scores 88.6, above every triage rule, while the Swin threshold
scores 81.7 (Table~\ref{tab:ablation} reports whether it is nonetheless the variant that trains best).
Keeping everything scores an F1 equal to the base rate by construction, which is the cleanest
demonstration that the metric rewards the rule that deletes nothing.
The asymmetry that F1 pools is real and directional --- a retained wrong mask paints wrong pixels into
a training label, whereas a deleted correct mask only leaves those pixels unlabelled --- so annotation
rules should be ranked on the two error rates separately, and we do so throughout.
The same correction applies to the deterministic LiDAR check, whose apparently strong
86.8 F1 is an artifact of passing nearly everything: it retains 93.6\,\% of the masks a
human rejected, the worst false-accept rate of any rule measured.

\textbf{Error rates are strongly class-dependent, and concentrated where the pipeline is weakest.}
Humans reject 33.7\,\% of cyclist and 34.1\,\% of pedestrian proposals against
18.0\,\% of vehicles and 16.2\,\% of signs. The classes with the worst SAM proposals are
the safety-critical ones, and they are also the classes where the Swin quality signal is least reliable
and where concordance triage was introduced to prevent over-rejection.

\textbf{Scored against humans, the cross-model ranking is a corpus-composition effect.}
Qwen's triage is closer to the human verdict than LLaVA's by $+0.5$ to $+1.4$ F1 (paired
frame-clustered bootstrap). Split by class the ordering inverts: Qwen leads on signs
(88.6 vs.\ 85.4) and cyclists (76.7 vs.\ 71.0), while LLaVA leads on
pedestrians (83.2 vs.\ 76.4) and vehicles (89.5 vs.\ 88.6).
Signs are 50.5\,\% of the judged masks, so the aggregate ordering is set by the class mix of
this corpus rather than by a competence ordering that would transfer to another one. We therefore do
not report a cross-model winner without the class breakdown.
The aggregate gap is also a function of the reference: it was $+1.7$ to $+2.9$ F1 over the first 450
frames and decays as the labeller tightens, because Qwen's advantage is concentrated in signs and
signs are the class whose acceptance rate fell most. An aggregate cross-model number is therefore a
statement about a corpus and a standard together, not about either model.

\textbf{Discovery confirmation carries no information, and is anti-correlated on fringes.}
Standalone candidates are 27.3\,\% real before any model sees them, and only 7.4\,\% under the final
calibration. Confirmation moves that by $+1.4$ points for Qwen and $+0.2$ for LLaVA --- within noise of
doing nothing. On fringe candidates, which are 70.3\,\% of the candidate pool, the signal inverts
outright: the stratum neither model confirmed is 43.4\,\% real against 34.1\,\% for the stratum both
confirmed, and both models' confirmation lift is negative. A gate that is uninformative on a third of
what it is shown and actively misleading on the rest cannot justify one VLM call per candidate.
Because the human judged the candidate region itself, this measurement is not subject to the
SAM-derived reference bias that affects the downstream comparison, and it is the more direct evidence
of the two.

\subsection{Downstream Segmentation}

We train CLFTv2-Base (RGB--LiDAR fusion) on each annotation variant, in three groups that answer three
different questions and are kept apart because they cannot share an evaluation.

\paragraph{Evaluation Protocol}
Comparing annotation variants requires a reference that is independent of every variant's own training labels: scoring a model against the same pseudo-labels it was trained on measures label self-consistency rather than segmentation quality, and inflates precisely the variants with the most idiosyncratic labels.
The human pass supplies that reference, and it also dictates how the frames are split.
The 1{,}001 verified frames are the only frames carrying an admissible reference, so for the annotation
ladder (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}) they are the test set and nothing else can be: every variant trains on
the 2{,}642 flagged frames that have no human labels and is scored on all 1{,}001 that do. No human
label is spent on training, and no variant is graded against its own labels.
Rejected pixels are mapped to \texttt{ignore} in all VLM-annotated variants.
Results are reported as mIoU averaged over vehicle, sign, and human (cyclist + pedestrian combined), where the overall mIoU is the mean of the five per-weather mIoUs.
Uncertainty is quantified with a paired bootstrap: test frames are resampled with replacement 10{,}000 times within each weather stratum, with identical resample indices for all variants, yielding 95\% confidence intervals for each variant and for pairwise mIoU differences.
The paired difference is the statistic this design supports, and per-variant marginal intervals are
reported only for completeness.

The ladder cannot answer whether human cleaning itself helps, because the human labels cover 1{,}001
frames and the automated variants 4{,}110, so a human row inside it would confound label quality with
annotation volume. That question gets its own controlled experiment (Table~\ref{tab:cleaning}), in which
both arms train on the same 651 frames and differ in exactly one field: whether the masks the human
rejected are erased or kept.
A third group holds the labels fixed and varies the input modality (Table~\ref{tab:modality_ablation});
it is the one comparison in which scoring against the shared training labels carries no bias, since all
three arms were trained on them.
One caveat is inherent to the first two groups: the reference is the same annotator's work that the
clean arm was trained on. Test frames are held out from every arm, so no model has seen its own test
labels, but the honest reading of a gap is that a model agrees more closely with a human, not that it
is closer to a neutral ground truth.

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} reports vehicle, sign, and combined human-class IoU (cyclist + pedestrian) for
every step of the ladder. The three VLM-independent rows are final; the four VLM rows are supplied by
runs still in progress and are marked \pending{pending} rather than filled from the earlier training
campaign, whose checkpoints were trained on pre-correction annotations and scored against a
SAM-derived reference and so answer neither the question this table asks nor against the reference it
uses. They are not reported here in any form.

\textbf{The free deterministic signal is the only filtering stage that helps, and the margin is large.}
The ladder is nested, so each step adds exactly one pipeline stage and any change is attributable to
that stage. Applying the Swin agreement threshold to the raw SAM proposals is worth
$+3.9$ mIoU (40.3 $\to$ 44.2, 95\,\% CI $[+2.5, +5.3]$, $P(\Delta\leq0)<10^{-4}$), and the gain is
present in all three classes and all five weather conditions (Table~\ref{tab:weather_miou}). It is
largest on signs ($+6.8$ IoU) and smallest on vehicles ($+2.1$), matching where SAM's proposals are
worst relative to their pixel volume. One forward pass per frame, at $<$0.01\,s per mask
(Table~\ref{tab:timing}), buys the entire measurable filtering effect in this study.

\textbf{Does VLM triage add anything over Swin alone?}
The mask-level evidence of Section~\ref{sec:human_results} predicts the answer and the ladder tests it:
crop-based VLM rejection retains more than half of the masks a human rejected, against under a quarter
for the free Swin threshold, so the VLM stage should not improve a training label unless deleting
correct masks costs more downstream than keeping wrong ones. The VLM rows are pending, so we state the
prediction and the standard it will be judged against rather than the result: the Swin-only row is the
reference column, and a triage stage that costs 110.6--362.7\,s of GPU time per frame has to beat 44.2
mIoU to have earned it.

\textbf{Discovery is strongly negative, and the human pass had already bounded it.}
Adding all Swin-detected discoveries to the Swin-filtered labels costs $-9.4$ mIoU
(44.2 $\to$ 34.8, CI $[-10.4, -8.4]$) --- more than twice the size of the gain the filtering stage
provides, and negative even against the unfiltered raw-SAM baseline ($-5.5$, CI $[-6.8, -4.1]$). The
damage is not confined to the classes discovery adds: vehicle IoU falls 9.0 points and sign 12.6, so
the added regions degrade the model globally rather than trading one class against another. This is
what the candidate-level measurement predicts: standalone candidates are 27.3\,\% real and
confirmation moves that by about a point, while on fringes --- 70.3\,\% of the candidate pool --- the
confirmed stratum is \emph{less} precise than the unconfirmed one. A stage that adds regions at those
precisions cannot improve a label set except by accident, and at this volume it does not merely fail to
help but overwhelms the one stage that does. Region geometry compounds the precision problem:
candidates are Swin connected components computed at $384{\times}384$ and upsampled, so even a correct
confirmation contributes an object with an approximate boundary, and the ladder cannot separate the
cost of wrong regions from the cost of coarse ones.
The discovery rows also carry an evaluation caveat the other rows do not: the human reference was
built by judging SAM proposals and discovery candidates, so an object that neither stage ever proposed
is absent from it, and a model that segments such an object is penalised. This biases the discovery
comparison conservatively, in the direction the mask-level evidence already points --- though a
$-9.4$ point deficit is far outside what an unmeasured-object penalty of this kind can plausibly
explain.
All discovery variants paint candidates as their exact connected-component masks rather than as filled
bounding boxes.

\textbf{Two triage refinements are not separable from the shipped rule.}
Section~\ref{sec:experiments} identifies two triage weaknesses from qualitative inspection: unilateral
rejection on a bare \texttt{background} verdict, and a LiDAR support threshold that is not class-aware.
Applying both fixes rescues 1{,}002 masks (914{,}277 pixels: 892 signs, 66 cyclists, 37 pedestrians,
7 vehicles) from rejection under offline replay, matching the classes and mechanism of the qualitative
examples in Fig.~\ref{fig:limitations}. Scored against the human verdicts, however, they buy a
1.7-point reduction in masks wrongly deleted at the cost of a 1.8-point rise in masks wrongly kept
(Qwen: 9.7\,\% and 61.3\,\% against the shipped rule's 11.4\,\% and 59.5\,\%), which is a move along
the same trade-off curve rather than a move off it.
We therefore do not train them: a variant that the human reference cannot separate from the shipped
rule will not be separable in a downstream mIoU comparison either, and every rule we measured within
this fixed signal set sits within about a point of its neighbours on both axes. Rule engineering moves
the operating point far less than the choice of which signals are consulted.

\textbf{Does human cleaning of the same labels raise mIoU? Yes, and out of proportion to its size.}
Table~\ref{tab:cleaning} isolates it. The intervention is deliberately small --- deleting the rejected
masks removes 5.3\,\% of the object pixels, and adds nothing --- yet it is worth $+2.5$ mIoU
(36.3 $\to$ 39.0, CI $[+0.7, +4.0]$, $P(\Delta\leq0)=0.003$) on 651 training frames. For scale --- and
the two experiments use different training sets and different test sets, so this is an order-of-magnitude
remark and not a paired comparison --- erasing one object pixel in twenty is worth about two thirds of
what the Swin filter is worth on a training set four times larger. Label \emph{purity} rather than
label \emph{volume} is the binding constraint on this corpus.

The lower block is the more informative half, because it reads the change in each class's IoU against
the share of that class the human actually deleted --- the dose that class received. The three doses
differ by a factor of six and the response follows them: vehicle, at a 3.5\,\% deletion rate, moves
$+0.9$ IoU, while sign (12.2\,\%) moves $+3.4$ and the human classes (20.9\,\%) $+2.4$. The two
heavily-cleaned classes gain three to four times what the lightly-cleaned one does, so the aggregate is
not a diffuse regularisation effect but a response localised to where the deletions were. That sign
gains slightly more than the human classes despite a smaller dose is consistent with the class
composition of the corpus rather than with a reversal: signs are 50.5\,\% of judged masks, so a given
deletion rate removes far more contaminated pixels there, and the human classes are the smallest and
noisiest of the three, whose per-class IoU is correspondingly the least stable at 201 test frames.
The direction and the ordering against dose are what this experiment supports; the exact per-class
magnitudes are not separately powered.

Two limits on the reading. The gap says a model trained on cleaned labels agrees more closely with the
annotator whose cleaning it was trained on, not that it is closer to a neutral ground truth --- the
reference is that same annotator's work, on held-out frames. And the arms differ by 5.3\,\% of object
pixels, so this bounds what cleaning is worth at \emph{this} dose; it does not extrapolate to a
hypothetical arm in which every mask was redrawn rather than deleted.

\textbf{Fixed prompts do not transfer neutrally, and the divergence is prompt-specific.}
Table~\ref{tab:agent_behavior} compares per-VLM agent outputs under identical prompts on the paired set of Section~\ref{par:comparability}: 3{,}535 frames and 73{,}708 masks with verified-identical Swin, LiDAR, and candidate inputs.

On mask verification, Qwen is the more permissive model.
It returns \texttt{valid} on 70.9\% of crops against LLaVA's 62.1\% as-recorded, and by the bound of~\eqref{eq:default_bound} the gap on answered masks is at least 5.1 points in the same direction.
The divergence is not a uniform shift, however, but a redistribution across classes: relative to LLaVA, Qwen rejects far fewer signs (10.8\% vs.\ 26.7\%) and cyclists (33.8\% vs.\ 48.1\%), yet substantially \emph{more} vehicles (16.2\% vs.\ 5.0\%) and pedestrians (35.1\% vs.\ 17.3\%).
The two models agree on only 56.8\% of masks, so identical prompts leave them disagreeing on well over four crops in ten.
Neither model is uniformly more selective, so a single aggregate acceptance rate does not predict which classes a model will protect.

On discovery, the two models are close in aggregate and far apart on one prompt.
Given the same 45{,}669 Swin-proposed candidates, they confirm at nearly the same rate over the candidates they answered, 82.5\% for Qwen against 83.9\% for LLaVA, and Qwen is in fact marginally more permissive on the vehicle (87.5\% vs.\ 83.3\%) and sign (89.1\% vs.\ 84.2\%) prompts.
The entire divergence sits in the third prompt, the one that must disambiguate a human candidate into cyclist or pedestrian: Qwen confirms 45.3\% of those candidates against LLaVA's 85.9\%, yielding 3{,}506 human objects against 6{,}376 from the identical candidate stream.
The failing prompt is the only one carrying a three-way discrimination rather than a presence check, and it governs the two classes the pipeline can least afford to lose.
A portability check reporting one aggregate confirmation rate per model would have scored these two models as near-equivalent on discovery and missed a 41-point deficit concentrated on cyclists and pedestrians.

We previously reported this comparison as a near-unconditional acceptance rate of 93.3\% paired with a 17.8\% discovery confirmation rate, and read the pattern as a behavioural profile in which permissiveness in triage traded off against scepticism in discovery.
The corrected picture is narrower: prompts transfer imperfectly, the imperfection is concentrated rather than global, and its sign varies by class and by prompt.
The earlier reading was an artifact of the serving fault, which is why we now report the safe-default share next to every rate.

\textbf{Limitations observed.} Curating these examples exposed two systematic weaknesses that the aggregate metrics of Table~\ref{tab:ablation} do not show, illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:limitations}. First, a \texttt{background} verdict is treated as two negative signals and triggers immediate, irreversible rejection with no human-review fallback, unlike the \texttt{invalid}+\texttt{good} downgrade rule that protects panel (d) of Fig.~\ref{fig:qualitative}. Several masks over real, correctly-shaped objects in our candidate pool, including a legible road sign at night (Fig.~\ref{fig:limitations}b) and a clearly visible bus (Fig.~\ref{fig:limitations}a), were rejected because the BBox agent misread a dark or motion-blurred crop as empty, even though Swin's independent quality score was high for the sign case (0.605 agreement, above threshold). A single VLM misjudgment on a real object is therefore sufficient to discard a valid label, undermining the safety guarantee the concordance rule otherwise provides.
Second, the three-signal design assumes Swin, the VLM, and LiDAR consistency fail independently, but several rejected masks show correlated failure instead: sparse or dot-matrix objects, an LED matrix road sign (Fig.~\ref{fig:limitations}c) and a pedestrian captured only from the waist up at close range (Fig.~\ref{fig:limitations}d), drove both Swin agreement and LiDAR support to near zero simultaneously, even though the BBox agent correctly judged them \texttt{valid}. Because rejection requires only two of three signals, a shared physical cause, low solid-surface fraction within the mask, can defeat the concordance requirement rather than being caught by it, and correctly proposed, safety-relevant sign and human masks are lost as a result. Both weaknesses are correctable with rule changes, namely exempting the \texttt{background} verdict from unilateral rejection when Swin agreement is high, and making the LiDAR support threshold class-aware. Section~\ref{sec:experiments} evaluates these fixes against the human verdicts: they rescue 1{,}002 masks dominated by the classes shown here, but move the two error rates by 1.7 and 1.8 points in opposite directions, which is a move along the trade-off curve rather than off it, so we do not train them as a separate ladder row. The examples in Fig.~\ref{fig:limitations} document a precision failure of the triage rule whose repair is worthwhile for label safety even though the human reference cannot separate it from the shipped rule in aggregate.

\subsection{Practical Guidance: Where Annotation Effort Should Go}
\label{sec:practice}

Taken together, the human verification and the downstream ladder answer a practical question that
neither answers alone: given a fixed budget, how should a complex driving scene actually be annotated?
The guidance below follows from the measurements above and is stated in the form a practitioner needs,
with the caveat that the verification figures are still provisional.

\textbf{Verification cost must be compared against human cost, not against zero.}
Annotating the paired frame set cost 108.6 GPU-hours with LLaVA-1.6-34B and 356.2 with
Qwen2.5-VL-72B over 3{,}535 frames (Table~\ref{tab:timing}), or 110.6\,s and 362.7\,s of GPU time per
frame, and produced a mask filter whose false-accept rate is more than twice the Swin threshold's.
A human labeller judging every proposal in a frame, presented as pixels and toggled in one pass, takes
27\,s per frame at the median, or 37\,s as a mean once the frames on which the labeller paused are
trimmed, which extrapolates to $\approx$31--42 hours for the entire 4{,}110-frame partition.
Per frame, the large VLM therefore spends $\approx$13$\times$ more machine time than the human
spends human time on the same frame ($\approx$4$\times$ for the 34B model), while the human is
by construction the reference the VLM is being scored against. Zero-shot mask verification at this model scale is not a cheap approximation of human
verification; on this corpus it is the expensive option.

\textbf{Keep the deterministic quality signal, drop the VLM triage stage.}
The Swin agreement filter is the only automated stage measured so far with a positive downstream
effect: $+3.9$ mIoU over unfiltered SAM labels (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}), for one forward pass per
frame at $<$0.01\,s per mask. It also dominates VLM triage on the false-accept axis
(23.5\,\% vs.\ 59.5\,\%), and its operating point is the one that does not move as the human reference
tightens. The VLM triage rows are still training, so the comparison they will settle is not yet
closed; but the bar those runs must clear is a stage that is essentially free, and the mask-level
evidence has them on the wrong side of the error direction that matters. We would not deploy the VLM
triage stage as an automatic filter on this data.

\textbf{Drop discovery, or gate it on something other than a second VLM.}
Discovery is negative downstream at every configuration measured, and by a wide margin: $-9.4$ mIoU
against the Swin-filtered labels, which is more than twice the filter's own contribution and leaves
the labels worse than doing nothing at all. Section~\ref{sec:human_results} shows why at the level of
the candidates themselves: confirmation lifts
standalone precision by about a point and inverts on fringes. Its regions are also coarse,
being Swin components computed at $384{\times}384$ and upsampled, so even a correct confirmation adds
an object with an approximate boundary.

\textbf{Spend the freed budget on human verification, and target it by mask, not by frame.}
The cleaning contrast (Table~\ref{tab:cleaning}) is what makes this more than a preference: deleting
the 5.3\,\% of object pixels a human rejected, on 651 training frames and adding nothing else, is
worth $+2.5$ mIoU, and the per-class response tracks the per-class deletion dose. At 27--37\,s of human
time per frame, that is the highest return per unit of budget anywhere in this study --- and the same
labelling pass produces the reference every other comparison is scored against.
Table~\ref{tab:review_targeting} quantifies the targeting. Ranking masks by the free Swin signal alone
reaches 44.0\,\% of all human-rejected masks within the top 20\,\% of masks, against
20.0\,\% for random order; adding the large VLM as a \emph{ranker} rather than a decision-maker
raises that to 47.1\,\%, and more sharply at the smallest budget (27.0\,\% vs.\ 21.7\,\%
at 10\,\%), while adding nothing by 30\,\%. The gain is real, confined to small budgets, and costs one
crop call per mask; on this hardware it does not pay for itself.
The VLM verdict also has to be used as a tie-break rather than as the primary key: ordering by it first
and by the free signals second is \emph{worse} than the free signals alone past a 20\,\% budget,
because it promotes high-agreement masks the model disliked ahead of low-agreement masks it did not.
Spending the budget on whole frames instead is far weaker: 88\,\% of
frames contain at least one rejected mask, so frame-level ranking barely separates
(25.2\,\% vs.\ 19.9\,\% random at a 20\,\% budget). Errors are spread thin across frames
and concentrated within them, which is an argument for mask-level review queues over frame sampling.
Class priority follows the same evidence: cyclist and pedestrian proposals are wrong
$\approx$34\,\% of the time, and the low-Swin subset of those classes is
55\,\% of their masks while holding 81\,\% of their errors.

\textbf{A confirm-or-reject stage cannot repair a boundary.}
The one structural limit behind all of the above: every automated stage in this pipeline can only
delete a mask or add a coarse one. It has no mechanism to redraw a boundary, and boundary quality is
what the human verdicts most often turn on. The verification interface records a rejection without
recording its reason, so we do not report a share here, but the direction is visible in the
discovery result: fringe candidates are rims around objects SAM already found, and confirming them is
the one thing the pipeline can do about a boundary --- which the human pass shows to be
anti-correlated with the boundary actually being wrong.
Pipelines aimed at pseudo-label quality for complex scenes should therefore invest in correction
mechanisms, whether SAM re-prompting or human redrawing, rather than in further verification rules,
whose ceiling is set by the 20.4\,\% of proposals that are wrong in the first place.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This work evaluates a multi-agent refinement pipeline for SAM-based annotation in autonomous driving, combining a domain-adapted Swin Transformer with fixed-prompt visual--LLM agents and deterministic decision rules. Across experiments on ZOD, the Swin Quality Agent, trained on a small clean partition, provides a stable signal for both mask triage and object discovery, enabling refinement with a single forward pass per frame and supporting fully reproducible offline ablations.

Under a single evaluation reference with paired bootstrap confidence intervals, we compare filtering strategies that use no VLM against those that do. That reference is now the human pass itself: the verified frames are held out of training entirely and every ladder variant is scored on all of them, which removes the SAM-derived circularity that limited the earlier comparison; the VLM-dependent rows are supplied by runs still in progress and are reported as pending rather than carried over from the superseded checkpoints, which were trained on pre-correction annotations and scored against a different reference.
The VLM-independent ladder is complete and its ordering is unambiguous. The free deterministic quality signal is the one automated stage that helps: thresholding on Swin agreement is worth $+3.9$ mIoU over unfiltered SAM labels for one forward pass per frame. Swin-driven object discovery is strongly negative, $-9.4$ mIoU against the same filtered labels and $-5.5$ against doing nothing at all, uniform across weather conditions and present in all three classes rather than confined to the ones it adds --- which is what the candidate-level precisions predicted and the aggregate confirms at more than twice the magnitude of the filter's gain.
Human cleaning of the same labels, in a controlled arm where training-set size is held fixed and the only difference is whether rejected masks are erased, is worth $+2.5$ mIoU for the deletion of 5.3\,\% of object pixels, with a per-class response that tracks the per-class deletion dose. On this corpus, label purity is the binding constraint and human deletion is the cheapest way to buy it.
It does not remove every limitation --- an object that neither SAM nor discovery ever proposed is absent from the reference, so a model that segments it is still penalised --- but the bias now runs only against the discovery variants, in the same direction the candidate-level measurement already establishes.

Applying identical prompts to Qwen2.5-VL-72B shows that fixed prompts do not transfer neutrally between open VLMs, and that the failure is localised rather than global. Qwen is the more permissive mask judge, by at least 5.1 points on masks both models answered, but it redistributes rather than uniformly relaxes its rejections: it rejects fewer signs and cyclists than LLaVA and more vehicles and pedestrians, and the two models agree on only 57.0\% of crops. On discovery they confirm the same candidate stream at almost the same aggregate rate, with the entire divergence concentrated in the single prompt that disambiguates cyclist from pedestrian, where Qwen confirms 45.3\% against LLaVA's 85.9\%. Portability validation must therefore be per-prompt and per-class: one aggregate acceptance rate per model would have scored these two as near-equivalent on discovery while missing a large deficit on the two safety-critical classes.


The architecture is backend-agnostic and suitable for local deployment.
Future work follows from the two limits this study reached rather than from its successes.
The human reference drifts, and the honest remedy is not more labelling under the same protocol but a
protocol that pins the standard --- a fixed calibration set re-judged at intervals, or a rubric that
makes the boundary criterion explicit --- since a reference that moves by twenty points across a pass
bounds what any absolute rate measured against it can mean.
Beyond that: correction mechanisms rather than further verification rules, cross-dataset transfer to
establish whether annotation quality affects more than in-domain mIoU, multi-seed replication,
adaptive discovery thresholds, and extensions to panoptic or temporal segmentation.
