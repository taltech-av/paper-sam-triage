\begin{abstract}
Training reliable multi-modal perception models for autonomous driving still depends heavily on dense pixel-level annotations, which remain costly and slow to produce at scale. 
Although the Segment Anything Model (SAM) can generate large numbers of pseudo-labels, its masks often drift at object boundaries, miss small traffic participants, or introduce spurious segments. 
These issues are especially problematic for vulnerable road users and other long‑tail classes that already suffer from limited representation in driving datasets. 
Recent vision–language models (VLMs) offer strong semantic priors and can reason about object presence and spatial layout directly from images, making them a promising tool for improving pseudo-label quality. 
This work introduces a simple refinement framework where a VLM is prompted with class-specific queries and used to validate SAM predictions, suppress clear hallucinations, and recover small objects that SAM frequently overlooks. 
The refined masks are then used to supervise the CLFTv2 camera–LiDAR fusion network, allowing us to study how improved pseudo-labels affect downstream segmentation. 
Experiments on the Zenseact Open Dataset and ISEAuto show that VLM-guided refinement consistently improves IoU for small and safety‑critical classes, while also reducing common SAM failure cases. 
Models trained on the refined labels achieve better overall accuracy and more stable performance across weather and illumination conditions. 
These results indicate that VLM-assisted refinement is a practical way to raise pseudo-label quality without additional manual annotation effort. 
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

High-quality semantic annotations remain one of the main bottlenecks in building reliable perception systems for autonomous driving.  
Modern multi-modal datasets contain millions of camera--LiDAR frames, yet only a small portion can be annotated at pixel level because each mask must be checked for geometric plausibility, occlusion handling, and cross-sensor consistency.  
Foundation models such as the Segment Anything Model (SAM) \cite{kirillov2023sam} help reduce the cost of mask generation, but their outputs often struggle in driving scenes, showing boundary drift, hallucinated regions, missed occlusions, and sensitivity to weather or illumination.  
As a result, large-scale annotation pipelines are limited not by generating masks, but by the effort required to validate and correct them.

Existing semi-automated pipelines address part of this problem using geometric priors, multi-sensor consistency checks, and threshold-based filtering \cite{zou2019confidence,jaritz2020xmuda}.  
These methods are efficient, but they rely on fixed rules that can reject valid masks, accept subtle errors, and provide little insight into why a decision was made.  
At the same time, recent open-source large language models have shown strong capabilities in structured reasoning and multi-step decision-making, suggesting that they may be better suited to coordinating annotation workflows than replacing vision models directly.

This paper introduces a multi-agent annotation system driven by visual LLMs for validating and refining SAM-generated segmentation masks in autonomous-driving datasets.  
Instead of using a single classifier, we decompose the validation process into specialized agents responsible for bounding-box filtering, mask-quality assessment, failure-mode classification, correction proposal generation, and cross-modal consistency checking.  
Each agent operates on structured metadata extracted from masks, detector outputs, and LiDAR projections, and their decisions are combined into an interpretable triage outcome.  
This modular design keeps the process transparent while allowing each failure mode to be handled independently.

Our contributions are threefold.  
First, we formulate mask validation as a risk-aware triage problem over structured multi-modal metadata.  
Second, we design a modular multi-agent architecture that separates failure detection, correction suggestion, and geometric consistency reasoning.  
Third, we evaluate the system on ZOD and ISEAuto using annotation-quality metrics, failure-mode agreement, cross-modal consistency, and downstream segmentation performance.  
Together, these results position visual LLMs as reasoning agents for scalable annotation quality control rather than as direct perception models.



\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

\subsection{Semantic Segmentation and Dataset Annotation in Autonomous Driving}

Semantic segmentation is a core component of autonomous-driving perception because it provides dense scene understanding for planning, tracking, and safety-critical decision-making.  
Early camera-based architectures such as FCN, U-Net, and PSPNet established strong baselines for pixel-level scene parsing \cite{long2015fcn,ronneberger2015unet,zhao2017psp}.  
Later work extended these ideas to multi-modal settings by integrating LiDAR through early-, late-, and feature-level fusion strategies \cite{valada2019selfsupervised,jaritz2019multimodal}.  
Despite these advances, segmentation performance still depends on large volumes of accurate annotations, and dense labels for autonomous-driving datasets remain expensive to produce.

To reduce annotation effort, prior work has explored weak supervision, pseudo-labeling, and geometric priors for semi-automatic labeling \cite{zou2019confidence,lee2013pseudo,jaritz2020xmuda}.  
Projection-based methods further leverage LiDAR geometry to validate or refine image-space labels and improve cross-sensor consistency \cite{behley2019semantickitti,caesar2020nuscenes}.  
These approaches improve efficiency, but they typically rely on fixed rules or task-specific heuristics that struggle with subtle failure cases and do not provide a general reasoning layer for interpreting heterogeneous error signals.

\subsection{Visual LLMs and Multi-Modal Reasoning for Annotation}

Recent progress in visual large language models has shown that combining image encoders with language-based reasoning enables models to interpret complex visual scenes, identify failure modes, and perform structured decision-making \cite{liu2024llava,ye2023mplug,wang2024qwen2vl}.  
Unlike classical vision models, visual LLMs can integrate visual cues, metadata, and textual descriptions within a unified reasoning framework, making them suitable for tasks that require both perception and interpretation.  
Several works have demonstrated that visual LLMs can evaluate segmentation quality, detect inconsistencies, or guide refinement steps by leveraging their ability to reason over multi-modal inputs \cite{zhang2024labelbench,li2023llmdata}.  
These systems highlight the potential of visual LLMs not as direct segmentation models, but as controllers that analyze outputs from existing vision pipelines and make informed decisions about reliability.

Multi-agent visual-LLM frameworks further extend this idea by decomposing complex tasks into specialized agents that collaborate through structured messages or shared metadata \cite{du2023improving,wang2023voyager}.  
Such systems have been applied to planning, tool use, and quality assessment, demonstrating that modular reasoning can outperform monolithic decision modules in transparency and robustness.  
However, their application to autonomous-driving annotation remains limited, and prior work has not explored structured multi-agent reasoning over camera--LiDAR metadata for validating and refining segmentation masks.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

The proposed system performs automatic validation and refinement of SAM-generated masks using a set of specialized visual-LLM agents.
Each agent receives a multimodal input bundle consisting of an RGB crop, a mask overlay, a LiDAR depth projection, and structured metadata.
Agents operate independently and return categorical or binary decisions rather than continuous confidence scores.
This design avoids uncalibrated probability estimates and enables deterministic, interpretable triage suitable for safety-critical annotation workflows.

\subsection{Multimodal Input Construction}

For each SAM mask proposal, we extract a tight crop around the bounding box and generate three aligned visual channels:
(1) the RGB crop,
(2) the mask overlay rendered on the RGB crop,
and (3) a depth map projected into the crop region.
Metadata describing geometric properties, temporal cues, and LiDAR support statistics is provided as structured text.
All agents receive the same multimodal bundle to ensure consistent reasoning across tasks.

\subsection{Visual-LLM Agents}

The system decomposes annotation validation into five independent visual-LLM agents, each responsible for a specific aspect of mask assessment.

\paragraph{Bounding-Box Agent}
Evaluates whether the 2D proposal corresponds to a plausible object region.
Outputs: \texttt{valid} or \texttt{invalid}.

\paragraph{Quality Agent}
Assesses whether the mask visually aligns with object boundaries and exhibits coherent shape.
Outputs: \texttt{good} or \texttt{bad}.

\paragraph{Failure-Mode Agent}
Identifies the dominant error type when the mask is incorrect.
Outputs: \texttt{boundary\_drift}, \texttt{hallucination}, \texttt{occlusion\_miss}, or \texttt{fragmentation}.
This agent is used for analysis and routing but does not contribute numeric scores.

\paragraph{Correction Agent}
Determines whether the mask can be improved and proposes a single actionable refinement step.
Outputs: \texttt{refine} or \texttt{no\_refine}.

\paragraph{Consistency Agent}
Checks whether the mask is supported by LiDAR depth and geometric cues.
Outputs: \texttt{pass} or \texttt{fail}.

All agents are prompted to return strictly constrained categorical outputs to ensure reproducibility and avoid free-form responses.

\subsection{Deterministic Triage Logic}

Instead of combining agent outputs through weighted scoring, the system uses a deterministic rule-based decision function.
This avoids arbitrary coefficients and leverages the strengths of binary visual-LLM judgments.

A mask is \textbf{accepted} if:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the bounding-box agent outputs \texttt{valid},
    \item the quality agent outputs \texttt{good},
    \item and the consistency agent outputs \texttt{pass}.
\end{itemize}

A mask is routed to \textbf{refinement} if:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the quality agent outputs \texttt{good},
    \item the consistency agent outputs \texttt{fail},
    \item and the correction agent outputs \texttt{refine}.
\end{itemize}

A mask is \textbf{rejected} if:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the bounding-box agent outputs \texttt{invalid},
    \item or the quality agent outputs \texttt{bad},
    \item or the failure-mode agent identifies a hallucination.
\end{itemize}

A mask is sent to \textbf{human review} when agents disagree or when no deterministic rule applies.
This includes cases where the correction agent proposes no valid refinement despite conflicting signals.

\subsection{Interpretability and Reproducibility}

The rule-based fusion mechanism ensures that every triage decision can be traced to explicit agent outputs.
No learned weights, thresholds, or continuous scores are used.
All prompts, multimodal inputs, and decision rules are fixed before running experiments, enabling reproducible evaluation across datasets and models.


\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

This section evaluates the proposed multi-agent visual-LLM annotation system using binary agent outputs and deterministic triage rules.
All experiments are designed to measure agreement between each agent and human ground-truth annotations, as well as the overall effectiveness of the triage pipeline.
No continuous confidence scores or weighted combinations are used; all decisions are categorical and directly comparable to expert labels.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

We evaluate the system on a held-out subset of an autonomous driving dataset containing RGB frames, LiDAR sweeps, and SAM-generated mask proposals.
For each mask, we construct the multimodal input bundle described in Section~\ref{sec:methodology}.
Human annotators provide ground-truth labels for bounding-box validity, mask quality, depth consistency, hallucination presence, and final triage outcome.
These labels serve as the reference for all quantitative comparisons.

Each visual-LLM agent is prompted with a constrained binary or categorical question and returns a single token from a fixed vocabulary.
All prompts, input formats, and decision rules are frozen before evaluation to ensure reproducibility.

\subsection{Agent-Level Evaluation}

We evaluate each agent independently by comparing its categorical output to the corresponding ground-truth label.
For binary agents (bounding-box, quality, consistency, hallucination), we compute accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score.
For the failure-mode agent, we compute per-class accuracy and macro-averaged F1 across the four error categories.

Each agent is evaluated on the full set of mask proposals, and results are reported in tables with one row per agent and one column per metric.
This design isolates the contribution of each agent and enables analysis of failure patterns such as hallucination sensitivity or depth-support disagreement.

\subsection{Triage Evaluation}

The final triage decision is produced by the deterministic rule-based logic described in Section~\ref{sec:methodology}.
We compare the system’s triage output (\texttt{accept}, \texttt{refine}, \texttt{reject}, \texttt{review}) to expert triage labels.
We report per-class precision and recall, macro-averaged F1, and confusion matrices to analyze common disagreements.

Because the triage logic is deterministic, every decision can be traced back to specific agent outputs.
This allows us to analyze which agent disagreements most frequently trigger human review and which error types lead to rejection.

\subsection{Ablation Studies}

To understand the contribution of each agent, we perform ablations by removing one agent at a time and recomputing triage outcomes.
For each ablation, we report the change in triage accuracy and the distribution of misclassified cases.
This reveals, for example, how much hallucination detection contributes to reducing false positives, or how depth consistency affects refinement routing.

We also evaluate alternative routing rules (e.g., stricter acceptance criteria, relaxed refinement conditions) to quantify the sensitivity of the deterministic logic.

\subsection{Qualitative Analysis}

We provide qualitative examples illustrating typical successes and failures of each agent.
These include cases where the visual-LLM correctly identifies hallucinations, flags boundary drift, or rejects masks unsupported by depth.
We also show examples where agents disagree, motivating the need for a human-review fallback.

All qualitative examples are selected to illustrate characteristic behaviors rather than to demonstrate performance.

\subsection{Reproducibility}

All prompts, decision rules, and evaluation scripts are fixed and deterministic.
No learned parameters, thresholds, or tuned coefficients are used.
This ensures that results can be reproduced exactly across different runs, datasets, and visual-LLM backends.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This work presents a modular visual-LLM multi-agent system for validating and refining SAM-based semantic segmentation annotations in autonomous driving.  
The central idea is to decompose annotation triage into specialized agents that reason jointly over RGB crops, mask overlays, LiDAR depth projections, and structured metadata.  
By framing annotation decisions as a risk-aware structured prediction problem and aggregating agent outputs through a deterministic fusion rule, the system provides transparent and reproducible triage suitable for safety-critical perception pipelines.  
The methodology emphasizes multimodal reasoning, interpretable intermediate decisions, and a clear separation between visual analysis, failure-mode diagnosis, and correction proposal generation.  
The experimental design focuses on evaluating annotation quality, failure-mode agreement, cross-modal consistency, and downstream segmentation performance using statistically grounded protocols.  
The architecture is intentionally backend-agnostic, enabling the use of different visual LLMs without altering the surrounding pipeline, and supports local deployment for data privacy.  
Future work includes large-scale empirical evaluation, adaptive prompt generation for SAM, multi-agent debate mechanisms, integration with stronger vision-language models, and extensions to panoptic and temporal segmentation.  
By formalizing multimodal reasoning as a core component of annotation workflows, this framework provides a reproducible foundation for studying visual-LLM-guided annotation quality control in autonomous-driving datasets.
