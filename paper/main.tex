\begin{abstract}
Training reliable multi-modal perception models for autonomous driving still depends heavily on dense pixel-level annotations, which remain costly and slow to produce at scale. 
Although the Segment Anything Model (SAM) can generate large numbers of pseudo-labels, its masks often drift at object boundaries, miss small traffic participants, or introduce spurious segments. 
These issues are especially problematic for vulnerable road users and other long‑tail classes that already suffer from limited representation in driving datasets. 
Recent vision–language models (VLMs) offer strong semantic priors and can reason about object presence and spatial layout directly from images, making them a promising tool for improving pseudo-label quality. 
This work introduces a multi-agent refinement framework where a VLM is prompted with class-specific queries and used to validate SAM predictions, suppress clear hallucinations, and recover small objects that SAM frequently overlooks.
The mask quality signal is provided by a domain-specific Swin Transformer segmentation model trained on ZOD, which produces a per-pixel class map in a single forward pass per frame and scores each mask by the fraction of its pixels assigned to the expected class.
Because Swin achieves substantially lower mIoU on small objects (cyclist, pedestrian) than on large ones (vehicle, sign), we adopt class-specific agreement thresholds to avoid excessive false rejection of safety-critical classes.
When Swin confidence is high, the BBox VLM call is skipped entirely, reducing inference cost by approximately 45\% without degrading triage accuracy.
Beyond cleaning existing masks, the system also recovers objects that SAM missed entirely: regions where Swin predicts a non-background class over annotation-background pixels are proposed as new masks, and a VLM confirms each candidate and disambiguates cyclist from pedestrian for the human class.
The refined and augmented masks are then used to supervise the CLFTv2 camera–LiDAR fusion network, allowing us to study how improved pseudo-labels affect downstream segmentation.
Experiments on the Zenseact Open Dataset and ISEAuto show that VLM-guided refinement consistently improves IoU for small and safety‑critical classes, while also reducing common SAM failure cases.
Beyond validating individual masks, the system recovers training data at scale: of 6{,}454 candidate frames, 64\% had previously been discarded by manual frame-level inspection, and triage-filtered versions of these frames can be returned to the training pool.
These results indicate that VLM-assisted refinement combined with domain-specific quality estimation is a practical way to raise pseudo-label quality without additional manual annotation effort.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

High-quality semantic annotations remain one of the main bottlenecks in building reliable perception systems for autonomous driving.  
Modern multi-modal datasets contain millions of camera--LiDAR frames, yet only a small portion can be annotated at pixel level because each mask must be checked for geometric plausibility, occlusion handling, and cross-sensor consistency.  
Foundation models such as the Segment Anything Model (SAM) \cite{kirillov2023sam} help reduce the cost of mask generation, but their outputs often struggle in driving scenes, showing boundary drift, hallucinated regions, missed occlusions, and sensitivity to weather or illumination.  
As a result, large-scale annotation pipelines are limited not by generating masks, but by the effort required to validate and correct them.

Existing semi-automated pipelines address part of this problem using geometric priors, multi-sensor consistency checks, and threshold-based filtering \cite{zou2019confidence,jaritz2020xmuda}.  
These methods are efficient, but they rely on fixed rules that can reject valid masks, accept subtle errors, and provide little insight into why a decision was made.  
At the same time, recent open-source large language models have shown strong capabilities in structured reasoning and multi-step decision-making, suggesting that they may be better suited to coordinating annotation workflows than replacing vision models directly.

This paper introduces a multi-agent annotation system driven by visual LLMs for validating and refining SAM-generated segmentation masks in autonomous-driving datasets.
Instead of using a single classifier, we decompose the validation process into specialized agents responsible for object-presence verification, mask-quality assessment, failure-mode classification, and correction proposal generation, complemented by a deterministic LiDAR-based consistency check.
Each agent operates on object-scale multimodal crops and structured metadata extracted from masks and LiDAR projections, and their decisions are combined into an interpretable triage outcome.
Because rejection is destructive in a pseudo-labeling pipeline, the triage logic is risk-aware: a mask is removed only when independent signals concordantly indicate an error, which prevents the false-positive rates of individual binary judges from compounding.
This modular design keeps the process transparent while allowing each failure mode to be handled independently.

Our contributions are fivefold.
First, we formulate mask validation as a risk-aware triage problem over structured multi-modal metadata, and show that destructive decisions must require concordant evidence from multiple judges to avoid compounding their individual false-positive rates.
Second, we design a modular multi-agent architecture that separates failure detection, correction suggestion, and geometric consistency reasoning, and identify object-scale cropping as a necessary condition for reliable VLM judgments on small traffic participants.
Third, we replace the general-purpose VLM quality judge with a domain-specific Swin Transformer trained on ZOD, and demonstrate that class-aware agreement thresholds are necessary because Swin mIoU on small objects (cyclist, pedestrian) is substantially lower than on large objects, causing uniform thresholds to over-reject safety-critical masks.
Fourth, we introduce a Swin-based object discovery mechanism that recovers false negatives — objects present in the scene but absent from the SAM annotation — using Swin class predictions to propose candidate regions and a VLM to confirm and classify them.
Fifth, we evaluate the system on ZOD and ISEAuto using annotation-quality metrics, failure-mode agreement, cross-modal consistency, and downstream segmentation performance.
Together, these results position domain-adapted segmentation models and visual LLMs as complementary components of scalable annotation quality control.



\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

\subsection{Semantic Segmentation and Dataset Annotation in Autonomous Driving}

Semantic segmentation is a core component of autonomous-driving perception because it provides dense scene understanding for planning, tracking, and safety-critical decision-making.  
Early camera-based architectures such as FCN, U-Net, and PSPNet established strong baselines for pixel-level scene parsing \cite{long2015fcn,ronneberger2015unet,zhao2017psp}.  
Later work extended these ideas to multi-modal settings by integrating LiDAR through early-, late-, and feature-level fusion strategies \cite{valada2019selfsupervised,jaritz2019multimodal}.  
Despite these advances, segmentation performance still depends on large volumes of accurate annotations, and dense labels for autonomous-driving datasets remain expensive to produce.

To reduce annotation effort, prior work has explored weak supervision, pseudo-labeling, and geometric priors for semi-automatic labeling \cite{zou2019confidence,lee2013pseudo,jaritz2020xmuda}.  
Projection-based methods further leverage LiDAR geometry to validate or refine image-space labels and improve cross-sensor consistency \cite{behley2019semantickitti,caesar2020nuscenes}.  
These approaches improve efficiency, but they typically rely on fixed rules or task-specific heuristics that struggle with subtle failure cases and do not provide a general reasoning layer for interpreting heterogeneous error signals.

\subsection{Visual LLMs and Multi-Modal Reasoning for Annotation}

Recent progress in visual large language models has shown that combining image encoders with language-based reasoning enables models to interpret complex visual scenes, identify failure modes, and perform structured decision-making \cite{liu2024llava,ye2023mplug,wang2024qwen2vl}.  
Unlike classical vision models, visual LLMs can integrate visual cues, metadata, and textual descriptions within a unified reasoning framework, making them suitable for tasks that require both perception and interpretation.  
Several works have demonstrated that visual LLMs can evaluate segmentation quality, detect inconsistencies, or guide refinement steps by leveraging their ability to reason over multi-modal inputs \cite{zhang2024labelbench,li2023llmdata}.  
These systems highlight the potential of visual LLMs not as direct segmentation models, but as controllers that analyze outputs from existing vision pipelines and make informed decisions about reliability.

Multi-agent visual-LLM frameworks further extend this idea by decomposing complex tasks into specialized agents that collaborate through structured messages or shared metadata \cite{du2023improving,wang2023voyager}.  
Such systems have been applied to planning, tool use, and quality assessment, demonstrating that modular reasoning can outperform monolithic decision modules in transparency and robustness.  
However, their application to autonomous-driving annotation remains limited, and prior work has not explored structured multi-agent reasoning over camera--LiDAR metadata for validating and refining segmentation masks.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

The proposed system performs automatic validation and refinement of SAM-generated masks using a set of specialized visual-LLM agents.
Each agent receives a multimodal input bundle consisting of an RGB crop, a mask overlay, a LiDAR depth projection, and structured metadata.
Agents operate independently and return categorical or binary decisions rather than continuous confidence scores.
This design avoids uncalibrated probability estimates and enables deterministic, interpretable triage suitable for safety-critical annotation workflows.

\subsection{Multimodal Input Construction}

For each SAM mask proposal, we extract a tight crop around the bounding box and generate three aligned visual channels:
(1) the RGB crop,
(2) the mask overlay rendered on the RGB crop,
and (3) a depth map projected into the crop region.
Metadata describing geometric properties, temporal cues, and LiDAR support statistics is provided as structured text.
All agents receive the same multimodal bundle to ensure consistent reasoning across tasks.

Object-scale cropping is essential rather than cosmetic.
Small traffic participants in driving scenes typically occupy only 10--30 pixels, far below the effective resolution at which open-weight VLMs can verify object presence in a full frame.
In preliminary experiments, batched full-frame queries in which multiple numbered boxes were verified in a single call caused the model to silently omit objects it could not resolve, and every omission turned into a false rejection; on ZOD this removed almost half of all valid masks.
We therefore upscale every crop to a minimum side length of 224 pixels (sourcing pixels from the original 4K imagery when available) and ask each question about exactly one mask at a time, providing the full frame only as additional context for the presence check.

\subsection{Visual-LLM Agents}

The system decomposes annotation validation into four visual-LLM agents and one deterministic geometric check, each responsible for a specific aspect of mask assessment.

\paragraph{Bounding-Box Agent}
Verifies the proposal by \emph{blind forced-choice classification} of the upscaled crop.
The agent is deliberately not told the proposed class: in preliminary experiments, prompts that named the expected class --- or rendered it as a text label in the context image --- caused the model to parrot the suggestion back, confirming featureless noise as vehicles; yes/no presence questions exhibited the same agreement bias.
Instead, the agent answers ``what does this crop mainly show?'' from a fixed vocabulary containing the four annotation classes and seven distractor categories (vegetation, building, sky, snow, road, other, unclear).
The output is \texttt{valid} if the answer matches the proposed class, \texttt{background} if it names a concrete non-object surface (vegetation, building, sky, snow), and \texttt{invalid} otherwise.
The agent sees only the object-scale crop: supplying the full frame as additional context degrades the judgment, because a small VLM then classifies the scene rather than the marked region (a highway scene yields ``vehicle'' regardless of what the box contains).

\paragraph{Quality Agent}
Assesses whether the mask pixels correspond to the expected semantic class.
Rather than a general-purpose VLM prompt, this role is filled by a domain-specific \textbf{Swin Transformer} \cite{liu2021swin} trained as a multi-class segmentation model on clean ZOD frames.
The model processes the full camera and LiDAR images at $384{\times}384$ resolution in a single forward pass per frame, producing a per-pixel class map shared across all masks in that frame.
Each mask is scored by its \emph{agreement ratio} $\alpha$: the fraction of mask pixels whose predicted class matches the expected class under the ZOD label mapping.
A mask is assigned quality \texttt{good} if $\alpha \geq \tau_q$; otherwise \texttt{bad}.

Because the Swin model achieves approximately $70\%$ mIoU on vehicle and sign but only $30$--$35\%$ mIoU on small objects (cyclist, pedestrian), applying a uniform threshold causes excessive false rejections for safety-critical classes: even a geometrically correct small-object mask may score low agreement simply because Swin's spatial resolution is insufficient to predict the correct class on a $10$--$30$ pixel region.
We therefore adopt \emph{class-specific} thresholds: $\tau_q^{\mathit{large}} = 0.30$ for vehicle and sign, and $\tau_q^{\mathit{small}} = 0.15$ for cyclist and pedestrian.
The bypass threshold is similarly split: $\tau_{\mathit{skip}}^{\mathit{large}} = 0.70$ and $\tau_{\mathit{skip}}^{\mathit{small}} = 0.40$.

Using a model trained on the target dataset rather than a general-purpose VLM has two practical advantages.
First, the Swin model has learned ZOD-specific appearance statistics (illumination, sensor calibration, class geometry), making it more reliable than a 7B VLM reasoning over the same crop from scratch.
Second, executing once per frame rather than once per mask eliminates the per-mask inference cost entirely; on ZOD flagged frames this reduces total inference time by more than the BBox agent saves through early-exit optimizations.

The bypass threshold $\tau_{\mathit{skip}}$ defines a high-confidence region where both the BBox and quality signals can be inferred from Swin alone: a high agreement score implies both that an object of the correct class is present and that the mask pixels are correctly assigned.
In our experiments every mask is evaluated by all agents to collect the complete signal set; the bypass is studied offline as an ablation variant using the saved $\alpha$ scores.
Experiments show the bypass threshold fires for approximately $45\%$ of masks, quantifying the potential inference savings if latency were the primary concern.
Outputs: \texttt{good} or \texttt{bad}.

\paragraph{Failure-Mode Agent}
Identifies the dominant error type when the mask is incorrect.
Outputs: \texttt{boundary\_drift}, \texttt{hallucination}, \texttt{occlusion\_miss}, or \texttt{fragmentation}.
This agent is purely diagnostic: it characterizes errors for analysis and routing but never influences the triage decision.

\paragraph{Correction Agent}
Determines whether the mask can be improved and proposes a single actionable refinement step.
Outputs: \texttt{refine} or \texttt{no\_refine}.

\paragraph{Consistency Check}
Verifies that the mask is supported by LiDAR geometry.
Unlike the other components, this check is deterministic: the fraction of mask pixels covered by projected LiDAR returns is compared against a fixed threshold $\tau = 0.1$.
Valid masks exhibit high LiDAR support (median $\approx 0.63$ on ZOD, with fewer than $1.5\%$ below $\tau$), so the check fires almost exclusively on masks leaked into sky or void regions.
Delegating this judgment to a VLM would replace a reliable geometric measurement with an unreliable reading of a sparse depth rendering.
Outputs: \texttt{pass} or \texttt{fail}.

All VLM agents are prompted to return strictly constrained categorical outputs to ensure reproducibility and avoid free-form responses.

\subsection{Swin-Based Object Discovery}

The agents described above validate and clean \emph{existing} SAM mask proposals, reducing false positives.
However, SAM may also miss objects entirely — false negatives that triage cannot detect because no proposal exists.
To address this, we introduce a complementary discovery step that runs once per frame after the per-mask triage loop.

The Swin class map (already computed for quality scoring) is compared pixel-by-pixel against the original SAM annotation scaled to $384{\times}384$.
Pixels where Swin predicts a non-background class but the annotation records background are candidate missed objects.
Connected-component analysis groups these pixels into spatially coherent regions; components below a minimum pixel count are discarded as noise.
The remaining candidates are sorted by pixel count (largest first) and at most $K_{\mathit{disc}}$ are evaluated per frame to prevent Ollama saturation under parallel processing.

For each candidate region, a crop is extracted from the camera image and sent to the VLM for confirmation.
The confirmation prompt is class-specific: for vehicle (Swin class~1) and sign (Swin class~2), the VLM answers whether the crop contains the predicted class.
For human (Swin class~3, which covers both cyclist and pedestrian in the Swin training labels), the VLM additionally disambiguates between the two pipeline classes in a single query.
Confirmed discoveries are written into the refined annotation at the appropriate class index, touching only pixels that are still background after triage.

This bidirectional design --- removing false positives through triage and recovering false negatives through discovery --- provides a more complete annotation correction than triage alone.

\subsection{Risk-Aware Deterministic Triage}

Instead of combining agent outputs through weighted scoring, the system uses a deterministic rule-based decision function.
This avoids arbitrary coefficients and leverages the strengths of binary visual-LLM judgments.

The design of the rejection rule follows from two observations.
First, rejection is the only destructive action in the pipeline: rejected pixels are removed from the training labels, whereas accepted, refined, and reviewed masks are all retained.
If rejection is triggered by \emph{any single} negative judgment, the false-positive rates of the individual judges compound: with $k$ independent binary judges, each wrongly flagging a fraction $f_i$ of valid masks, a disjunctive rule destroys $1 - \prod_i (1 - f_i)$ of the valid masks --- already $27\%$ for three judges at a modest $10\%$ false-positive rate each.
Second, the base rate of true hallucinations is low: SAM proposals in our pipeline are seeded from verified 2D ground-truth boxes, so regions containing no object at all are rare.
By Bayes' rule, when the error prior is small, most single-judge rejections are false positives regardless of judge accuracy.
Both effects are amplified by class imbalance: vulnerable road users occupy under $1\%$ of pixels, so each falsely deleted pedestrian mask costs the downstream model disproportionately more than a retained imperfect mask.

The triage function therefore requires \emph{concordant} evidence for rejection and treats acceptance as the default for ambiguous cases, with human review as a non-destructive escape hatch.
Let the negative signals be $\mathcal{N} = \{\texttt{invalid}, \texttt{bad}, \texttt{fail}\}$ from the bounding-box agent, quality agent, and consistency check respectively.

\paragraph{Swin High-Confidence Bypass (ablation variant)}
When the Swin agreement ratio $\alpha \geq \tau_{\mathit{skip}}$, both the BBox and quality signals can be inferred from Swin alone, so the BBox VLM call can in principle be skipped.
In the full pipeline run, all agents are always called to maximise the collected signal.
The bypass threshold fires for approximately $45\%$ of masks; the impact on triage accuracy is measured via offline replay using the \texttt{with\_bypass} variant in \texttt{replay\_triage.py}.

A mask is \textbf{rejected} if at least two signals in $\mathcal{N}$ are present.
A \texttt{background} verdict from the bounding-box agent counts as two negative signals and therefore rejects on its own: positively identifying the surface under the mask (vegetation, building, sky, snow) is direct evidence of error, whereas \texttt{invalid} merely records absence of confirmation --- an unclear crop, or a ``road'' answer that small objects on road backgrounds frequently attract --- and requires corroboration from a second judge.

A mask is \textbf{accepted} if:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the bounding-box agent outputs \texttt{valid},
    \item the quality agent outputs \texttt{good},
    \item and the consistency check outputs \texttt{pass}.
\end{itemize}

A mask is routed to \textbf{refinement} if:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the bounding-box agent outputs \texttt{valid},
    \item the quality agent outputs \texttt{good},
    \item the consistency check outputs \texttt{fail},
    \item and the correction agent outputs \texttt{refine}.
\end{itemize}

A mask is sent to \textbf{human review} in all remaining cases, i.e., whenever exactly one judge dissents.
Review masks are retained in the refined annotation but flagged for inspection, so a single erroneous negative judgment can never silently delete a valid label.
The failure-mode agent never contributes to the decision; it annotates rejected and reviewed masks with an error category for analysis.

\subsection{Interpretability and Reproducibility}

The rule-based fusion mechanism ensures that every triage decision can be traced to explicit agent outputs.
No learned weights, thresholds, or continuous scores are used.
All prompts, multimodal inputs, and decision rules are fixed before running experiments, enabling reproducible evaluation across datasets and models.


\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

This section evaluates the proposed multi-agent visual-LLM annotation system using binary agent outputs and deterministic triage rules.
All experiments are designed to measure agreement between each agent and human ground-truth annotations, as well as the overall effectiveness of the triage pipeline.
No continuous confidence scores or weighted combinations are used; all decisions are categorical and directly comparable to expert labels.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

We evaluate the system on the Zenseact Open Dataset (ZOD) \cite{alibeigi2023zod} using SAM-generated mask proposals produced by our upstream annotation pipeline \cite{gu2024clft}.
In our previous annotation campaign, every candidate frame was manually inspected at frame level: an annotator viewed the rendered SAM annotation and either passed the frame for training or discarded it.
This produced two partitions, summarized in Table~\ref{tab:dataset}: a \emph{clean} partition of 2{,}319 frames that passed inspection, and a \emph{flagged} partition of 4{,}135 frames that failed --- $64\%$ of all candidate data, discarded wholesale even though many individual masks within those frames are valid.
Flagged frames are noticeably denser ($21.9$ vs.\ $15.5$ proposals per frame), consistent with crowded scenes being both harder for SAM and more likely to fail inspection.
This human partition serves as frame-level weak supervision for evaluating the triage system at scale, while mask-level expert labels on a stratified subset of proposals (covering bounding-box validity, mask quality, depth consistency, and final triage outcome) serve as ground truth for agent-level evaluation.

Each visual-LLM agent is prompted with a constrained binary or categorical question and returns a single token from a fixed vocabulary.

\paragraph{VLM Configuration}
We evaluate the pipeline with two large open-weight vision--language models served via Ollama on a single NVIDIA A100-SXM4 80\,GB GPU: Qwen2.5-VL-72B \cite{bai2025qwen25vl} and Llama-3.2-Vision-90B \cite{meta2024llama32}.
Both are served in 4-bit quantisation, occupying approximately 45\,GB and 55\,GB of device memory respectively.
Each model is run in a separate full pipeline pass over all frames; results are stored in independent output directories tagged by model name.
Because the Swin Quality Agent is VLM-independent (it runs once per frame and scores masks from a fixed trained model), the Swin agreement scores are computed once and shared across both evaluations.
Only the BBox, Correction, and Failure-Mode agents, which make direct VLM calls, differ between the two runs.
This design allows a direct comparison of both models under identical triage rules and Swin signals using the offline replay mechanism, which re-applies any combination of triage rules to the stored agent outputs without re-running inference.

\subsection{Agent-Level Evaluation}

We evaluate each agent independently by comparing its categorical output to the corresponding ground-truth label.
For binary judges (bounding-box agent, quality agent, correction agent, and the deterministic consistency check), we compute accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score.
Because rejection is the destructive action, we pay particular attention to the false-positive rate of each judge on valid masks, which directly bounds the amount of valid annotation the triage rules can destroy.
For the failure-mode agent, we compute per-class accuracy and macro-averaged F1 across the four error categories.

Each agent is evaluated on the full set of mask proposals, and results are reported in tables with one row per agent and one column per metric.
This design isolates the contribution of each agent and enables analysis of failure patterns such as hallucination sensitivity or depth-support disagreement.

\subsection{Triage Evaluation}

The final triage decision is produced by the deterministic rule-based logic described in Section~\ref{sec:methodology}.
We compare the system’s triage output (\texttt{accept}, \texttt{refine}, \texttt{reject}, \texttt{review}) to expert triage labels.
We report per-class precision and recall, macro-averaged F1, and confusion matrices to analyze common disagreements.

Because the triage logic is deterministic, every decision can be traced back to specific agent outputs.
This allows us to analyze which agent disagreements most frequently trigger human review and which error types lead to rejection.

\subsection{Frame-Level Validation Against Manual Inspection}
\label{sec:frame_level}

The clean/flagged partition provides frame-level supervision at a scale no mask-level labeling effort can match, and we use it in two ways.

First, we run the full triage pipeline over both partitions and compare the resulting decision distributions.
If the agents capture annotation quality, flagged frames must receive a substantially higher share of \texttt{reject} and \texttt{review} decisions than clean frames; if the distributions coincide, the system is not discriminating.

Second, we test whether the system can reproduce the manual inspection itself.
Mask-level decisions are aggregated into a frame-level quality score
\[
s(\mathcal{F}) = \frac{\sum_{m \in \mathcal{F}} |m| \cdot \mathbb{1}[\,\text{triage}(m) = \texttt{reject}\,]}{\sum_{m \in \mathcal{F}} |m|},
\]
the pixel-weighted fraction of rejected proposals in frame $\mathcal{F}$, and a frame is classified as flagged when $s(\mathcal{F}) > \theta$.
Sweeping $\theta$ against the manual labels yields a ROC curve; we report AUC together with accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 at the operating point.

Two properties of the manual labels temper the interpretation.
They are weak: a flagged frame may contain many valid masks, since a single severe error was sufficient for a human to discard the entire frame.
They are also partially unobservable to our system: frames were sometimes flagged for \emph{missing} objects, which mask-level triage cannot detect because no proposal exists.
Perfect agreement is therefore neither expected nor desired; the experiment measures whether automatic triage ranks frames by annotation quality the way a human inspector does.

\subsection{Downstream Segmentation Evaluation}
\label{sec:downstream}

The decisive test of the filter is whether it improves the models trained on its output.
A triage system that wrongly removes some valid masks can still be worthwhile if it removes disproportionately more noise; downstream segmentation performance integrates this precision--recall trade-off into the metric that ultimately matters.

We train the CLFTv2 camera--LiDAR fusion network \cite{gu2024clft} under four label configurations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item[\textbf{A}] clean frames only --- the baseline produced by manual quality control, identical to our previous work;
    \item[\textbf{B}] clean frames plus all flagged frames with unfiltered SAM masks --- measures the cost of training on rejected data as-is;
    \item[\textbf{C}] flagged frames only, retaining accepted masks --- isolates the filter effect against the same frames unfiltered;
    \item[\textbf{D}] clean frames plus filtered flagged frames --- the full recovery setting, returning discarded data to the training pool.
\end{itemize}

In filtered configurations, all proposals not accepted by triage are mapped to the \texttt{ignore} index, which is excluded from the training loss.
This choice is deliberate: mapping a falsely rejected pedestrian to \emph{background} would actively supervise the model with a wrong label on a real object, whereas \texttt{ignore} merely withholds supervision.
We ablate this decision by repeating configuration C with rejected pixels mapped to background instead.

All configurations share the same architecture, training schedule, augmentation, and held-out manually annotated evaluation split; they differ only in the training labels.
We report per-class IoU and mIoU, with particular attention to vulnerable road users, where false rejections are most costly.
If the filter is effective, C should outperform B, and D should outperform both A and B --- demonstrating that triage recovers training value from the $64\%$ of data that manual inspection discarded.

\subsection{Ablation Studies}

All triage-rule and Swin-threshold ablations are performed offline without re-running inference.
The pipeline stores raw agent outputs and Swin agreement scores in per-frame JSON files; \texttt{replay\_triage.py} re-applies any triage variant to these stored signals, producing a new set of annotation PNGs that can then be used to train CLFTv2.
This means a single HPC inference run yields all triage variants in Table~\ref{tab:ablation}, with no additional GPU time.

VLM backbone ablations require separate pipeline runs because the BBox, Correction, and Failure-Mode agent outputs differ between models.
Each run writes results to an independent directory tagged by model name; the Swin quality scores are shared (computed once) and embedded in both result sets, enabling direct per-mask comparison of model verdicts.

We ablate four dimensions:
(1)~\emph{Triage rules} --- removing the Swin or VLM quality signal, enabling the bypass, or switching to disjunctive rejection;
(2)~\emph{Swin thresholds} --- comparing class-aware $\tau_q$ against a uniform threshold;
(3)~\emph{VLM backbone} --- Qwen2.5-VL-72B versus Llama-3.2-Vision-90B under the same rules;
(4)~\emph{Object discovery} --- measuring the contribution of false-negative recovery to downstream mIoU.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This work presents a modular multi-agent system for validating, refining, and augmenting SAM-based semantic segmentation annotations in autonomous driving.
The central idea is to decompose annotation quality control into specialized agents that reason jointly over RGB crops, mask overlays, LiDAR depth projections, and structured metadata.
By framing annotation decisions as a risk-aware structured prediction problem and aggregating agent outputs through a deterministic concordance rule --- in which destructive rejection requires agreement between independent judges --- the system provides transparent and reproducible triage suitable for safety-critical perception pipelines while preventing the false-positive rates of individual judges from compounding.

A key finding is that a domain-specific Swin Transformer trained on ZOD provides a stronger quality signal than a general-purpose VLM for in-domain annotation assessment, while the VLM retains unique value for semantic reasoning tasks the segmentation model cannot resolve: confirming object identity, detecting background surfaces, and disambiguating cyclist from pedestrian during discovery.
A further finding is that class-aware agreement thresholds are necessary when using a segmentation model as a quality judge: the same threshold appropriate for large objects causes systematic over-rejection of small-object classes due to the model's lower spatial resolution on cyclist and pedestrian instances.

Beyond cleaning existing masks, the system also addresses false negatives through a Swin-based discovery mechanism, using the same per-frame class map to propose candidate missed objects and asking the VLM to confirm and classify each.
This bidirectional design --- removing false positives through triage and recovering false negatives through discovery --- makes the pipeline more complete than triage alone.

A central element of the evaluation is the recovery of previously discarded data: frame-level manual inspection labels from our earlier annotation campaign serve both as weak supervision for validating the triage system at scale and as the basis for a controlled downstream experiment in which filtered versions of rejected frames are returned to the training pool.
The architecture is intentionally backend-agnostic, enabling the use of different visual LLMs without altering the surrounding pipeline, and supports local deployment for data privacy.
Future work includes large-scale empirical evaluation, adaptive prompt generation, multi-agent debate mechanisms for contested decisions, and extension to panoptic and temporal segmentation.
By combining domain-adapted segmentation models with visual-LLM semantic reasoning, this framework provides a reproducible foundation for scalable annotation quality control in autonomous-driving datasets.
