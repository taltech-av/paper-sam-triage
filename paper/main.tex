\begin{abstract}
Dense pixel-level annotations remain a critical bottleneck for autonomous driving perception, yet the cost of manual labeling limits the scale at which high-quality supervision can be obtained.
Foundation models such as the Segment Anything Model (SAM) enable large-scale pseudo-labeling, but their masks frequently exhibit boundary drift, spurious regions, and missed objects—errors that are especially severe for safety-critical classes such as cyclists and pedestrians.

We present a multi-agent concordance triage framework that validates and augments SAM-generated semantic segmentation masks using two complementary signals: a domain-adapted Swin Transformer that scores each mask by its per-pixel class agreement ratio, and a vision–language model (VLM) that performs object-presence verification on tight, object-scale crops.
Rejection requires at least two independent signals to concur, preventing any single erroneous judgment from silently removing valid annotations.
Class-aware quality thresholds compensate for the Swin model's lower recall on rare traffic participants.
A discovery module identifies objects SAM missed by comparing the Swin class map to SAM background labels and confirming candidates with a VLM, enabling bidirectional correction of both false positives and false negatives.

We evaluate on the Zenseact Open Dataset and iSEAuto using two open-weight VLMs, and study downstream effects by training a CLFTv2 camera--LiDAR fusion model on all annotation variants.
Results show that domain-adapted segmentation filtering is the primary driver of overall mIoU gains, while VLM triage uniquely recovers rare-class performance that pixel-level filtering alone cannot achieve.
All decisions are traceable to explicit agent outputs with no learned fusion weights, enabling full reproducibility and offline ablation without re-running inference.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

High-quality pixel-level annotations are a prerequisite for training reliable perception models in autonomous driving, yet the cost of manual labeling limits the scale at which supervision can be obtained.
Foundation models such as the Segment Anything Model (SAM) \cite{kirillov2023sam} have made large-scale pseudo-labeling feasible, but their masks frequently drift at object boundaries, hallucinate regions with no physical correlate, or miss small traffic participants entirely.
These errors are not random noise: they cluster around the safety-critical classes—cyclists, pedestrians, and signs—that are most consequential for downstream planning, and they propagate directly into trained models when pseudo-labels are used as supervision without additional validation.

Existing filtering strategies offer limited remedies.
Confidence thresholding and geometric priors \cite{zou2019confidence,lee2013pseudo,jaritz2020xmuda} remove only the most extreme outliers and cannot distinguish a well-shaped mask on the wrong object from a correctly placed mask with a rough boundary.
Semi-supervised consistency constraints \cite{jaritz2020xmuda} improve efficiency but depend on fixed heuristics that lack interpretability and do not generalise across sensor configurations.
Vision–language models (VLMs) \cite{liu2024llava,wang2024qwen2vl} offer richer semantic priors and can perform object-level reasoning, but their application to SAM mask triage—particularly over camera–LiDAR multimodal inputs and small per-mask crops—has not been explored.

We address this gap with a multi-agent concordance triage system that combines two complementary signals: a domain-adapted Swin Transformer \cite{liu2021swin} trained on clean frames to assess per-pixel class agreement, and a VLM that verifies object presence on tight, object-scale crops.
The key design principle is that rejection is destructive and irreversible, so a mask is discarded only when at least two independent signals concur—preventing any single erroneous judge from silently removing valid annotations.
Class-aware thresholds compensate for the Swin model's lower recall on small traffic participants.
A complementary discovery module recovers objects SAM missed by finding regions where the Swin class map disagrees with SAM background labels and confirming candidates with a VLM, achieving bidirectional correction of both false positives and false negatives.

We evaluate on the Zenseact Open Dataset (ZOD) \cite{alibeigi2023zod} and iSEAuto using two open-weight VLMs, and measure downstream impact by training a CLFTv2 \cite{gu2024clft} camera–LiDAR fusion model on all annotation variants.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item a concordance-based multi-agent triage framework that combines domain-adapted segmentation and VLM signals with a risk-aware rejection rule,
    \item a domain-adapted Swin quality judge with class-aware thresholds that compensates for low segmentation recall on rare traffic participants,
    \item a VLM-confirmed discovery module that recovers objects missed by SAM, enabling bidirectional pseudo-label correction,
    \item and a systematic evaluation on two datasets measuring annotation quality, downstream segmentation performance across five annotation variants, and cross-dataset generalization.
\end{itemize}





\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

\subsection{Semantic Segmentation and Pseudo-Labeling in Autonomous Driving}

Semantic segmentation underpins scene understanding in autonomous driving, with camera-based architectures such as FCN, PSPNet, and DeepLab \cite{long2015fcn,zhao2017psp,chen2018deeplab} establishing dense prediction baselines and later work extending these to camera--LiDAR fusion through feature-level alignment \cite{valada2019selfsupervised,jaritz2019multimodal}.
Despite strong performance on established benchmarks, these models depend on pixel-accurate supervision that is expensive to collect at scale.

Pseudo-labeling and self-training methods \cite{lee2013pseudo,zou2019confidence} reduce labeling cost by using model predictions as supervision, while cross-modal consistency constraints \cite{jaritz2020xmuda} and LiDAR-projection checks \cite{behley2019semantickitti,caesar2020nuscenes} leverage geometric redundancy to filter erroneous labels.
These approaches improve annotation efficiency but rely on fixed thresholds and cannot detect semantic errors—a mask with geometrically plausible shape but misassigned class label passes all geometric checks and corrupts training.
Long-tail classes such as cyclists and pedestrians, which are underrepresented in both labels and model predictions, are particularly susceptible to this failure mode.

\subsection{Foundation Models for Scalable Annotation}

The Segment Anything Model (SAM) \cite{kirillov2023sam} has fundamentally changed the economics of mask generation: class-agnostic proposals can be produced for every object in a scene without per-class prompts or labeled training data.
This has motivated large-scale pseudo-annotation pipelines for autonomous driving, where SAM proposals are matched to semantic categories via overlaid sensor data or downstream classifiers.
However, SAM's class-agnostic design means it cannot verify the semantic correctness of its own output.
Boundary drift occurs where object edges are ambiguous in RGB alone; hallucinated regions arise in texturally uniform areas such as sky and vegetation; and small objects are frequently missed when their image footprint falls below SAM's effective resolution.
Post-hoc semantic validation of SAM proposals is therefore a necessary complement to any SAM-based annotation pipeline, and it is precisely what our system provides.

\subsection{Vision–Language Models and Multi-Agent Reasoning}

Vision–language models (VLMs) combine image encoders with autoregressive language decoders, enabling structured reasoning over visual content \cite{liu2024llava,ye2023mplug,wang2024qwen2vl}.
The LLM-as-judge paradigm \cite{zheng2023judging} has established that language models can reliably evaluate the quality of other models' outputs when given structured prompts and constrained output formats, providing a scalable alternative to human review for certain quality-assessment tasks.
In the annotation context, this suggests VLMs can serve as semantic judges over mask proposals—verifying object identity and flagging implausible regions—without requiring fine-tuning on task-specific labels.

Multi-agent frameworks extend single-model reasoning by distributing subtasks across specialized agents that exchange structured messages \cite{du2023improving,wang2023voyager}.
Requiring concordance between independent agents before taking a destructive action is a key reliability mechanism: if agents disagree, the case is escalated rather than resolved by majority vote, preserving auditability.
This principle directly motivates our triage rule: rejection, the most consequential decision, requires at least two independent negative signals.
Prior work has not applied multi-agent concordance to SAM mask validation over camera--LiDAR multimodal inputs, nor studied how the complementary strengths of pixel-level segmentation and semantic VLM reasoning interact in a single annotation pipeline.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

The system refines SAM-generated masks using a set of specialized visual–LLM agents and a domain-specific segmentation model. 
Each agent receives a multimodal input bundle consisting of an RGB crop, a mask overlay, a LiDAR depth projection, and structured metadata, and returns a categorical decision. 
All outputs feed into a deterministic triage rule designed for safety‑critical annotation workflows.

\subsection{Multimodal Input Construction}

For each SAM proposal, we extract a tight crop around the bounding box and generate three aligned channels: the RGB crop, the mask overlay, and a projected LiDAR depth map. 
Metadata describing geometric properties and LiDAR support is provided as structured text. 
Object‑scale cropping is essential: small traffic participants often occupy only 10–30 pixels, and full‑frame VLM queries fail to resolve them reliably. 
We therefore upscale each crop to at least 224 pixels and query one mask at a time, using the full frame only as optional context.

\subsection{Visual–LLM Agents}

The system decomposes mask assessment into three VLM agents (BBox, Failure‑Mode, and Correction), one Swin‑based quality agent, and one deterministic geometric check.

\paragraph{Bounding‑Box Agent}
Performs blind forced‑choice classification over a fixed vocabulary of object classes and distractors. 
The agent is not told the expected class to avoid agreement bias. 
Outputs are: \texttt{valid} (matches expected class), \texttt{background} (vegetation, building, sky, snow), or \texttt{invalid}. 
The agent sees only the crop; providing the full frame causes scene‑level bias.

\paragraph{Quality Agent}
Evaluates mask correctness using a Swin Transformer \cite{liu2021swin} trained on clean ZOD frames. 
A single forward pass produces a per‑pixel class map for the entire frame. 
Each mask receives an agreement ratio $\alpha$, the fraction of pixels matching the expected class. 
Because Swin achieves high mIoU on large objects but low mIoU on cyclists and pedestrians, we use class‑specific thresholds: $\tau_q^{\text{large}}{=}0.30$, $\tau_q^{\text{small}}{=}0.15$. 
A bypass threshold $\tau_{\text{skip}}$ ($0.70$ for large classes, $0.40$ for small) is recorded per mask during inference; whether $\alpha \geq \tau_{\text{skip}}$ is stored in the result JSON so that the accuracy–efficiency trade-off of skipping the BBox agent can be evaluated entirely offline from saved outputs.

\paragraph{Failure‑Mode Agent}
Classifies the dominant error type (\texttt{boundary\_drift}, \texttt{hallucination}, \texttt{occlusion\_miss}, \texttt{fragmentation}). 
This agent is diagnostic only and does not affect triage.

\paragraph{Correction Agent}
Determines whether a mask can be improved and proposes a single refinement step (\texttt{refine} or \texttt{no\_refine}).

\paragraph{Consistency Check}
A deterministic LiDAR‑support test: the fraction of mask pixels with projected LiDAR returns must exceed $\tau{=}0.1$. 
This check reliably detects masks leaking into sky or void regions.

All VLM agents return constrained categorical outputs to ensure reproducibility.

\subsection{Swin‑Based Object Discovery}

Triage removes false positives but cannot detect objects SAM missed. 
We therefore introduce a discovery step that compares the Swin class map to the SAM annotation at $384{\times}384$ resolution. 
Pixels where Swin predicts a non‑background class but SAM predicts background form candidate regions. 
Connected components below a minimum size are discarded; the largest remaining components (up to $K_{\text{disc}}$ per frame) are cropped and verified by a VLM. 
For vehicles and signs, the VLM confirms presence; for humans, it also disambiguates cyclist vs.\ pedestrian. 
Confirmed regions are added as new masks, modifying only pixels still labeled as background.

\subsection{Risk‑Aware Deterministic Triage}

Instead of weighted scoring, we use a deterministic rule requiring concordant evidence for rejection. 
Rejection is destructive—deleted pixels cannot be recovered—so relying on any single negative signal would compound false positives across agents. 
Let $\mathcal{N}=\{\texttt{invalid},\texttt{bad},\texttt{fail}\}$ denote negative signals.

A mask is \textbf{rejected} if at least two signals in $\mathcal{N}$ are present. 
A \texttt{background} verdict counts as two signals and rejects immediately.

A mask is \textbf{accepted} if the BBox agent outputs \texttt{valid}, the quality agent outputs \texttt{good}, and the consistency check outputs \texttt{pass}.

A mask is routed to \textbf{refinement} if it is valid and of good quality but fails the consistency check and the correction agent recommends refinement.

All remaining cases—exactly one dissenting judge—are sent to \textbf{human review}. 
Review masks are retained but flagged, ensuring that no single erroneous judgment silently deletes valid labels.

\subsection{Interpretability and Reproducibility}

All prompts, thresholds, and decision rules are fixed before evaluation. 
Every triage outcome can be traced to explicit agent outputs, and no learned fusion weights or continuous scores are used, ensuring reproducibility across datasets and models.

\subsection{Downstream Segmentation}

We train CLFTv2 on seven annotation variants derived from a 1{,}000-frame Qwen2.5-VL-72B inference run on the ZOD validation subset.
Rejected pixels are mapped to \texttt{ignore}.
The full downstream comparison—including clean-frame baselines, LLaVA-34B, consensus voting, and the complete 6{,}454-frame dataset—is reserved for future experiments (Table~\ref{tab:downstream}).

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} reports vehicle, sign, and combined human-class IoU (cyclist + pedestrian) for all seven variants.
Three findings stand out.

\textbf{Swin filtering outperforms VLM triage for dominant classes.}
Swin-only filtering achieves 33.6 mIoU versus 28.8 for the full Swin + VLM triage, a 4.8-point gap driven by sign and vehicle classes.
The BBox agent's crop-based classification introduces false rejections on small, thin signs where pixel-level Swin agreement is the more reliable signal.

\textbf{Discovery consistently adds value.}
Adding Swin-confirmed discovery objects boosts mIoU by 2.1 points over both its base configurations: raw SAM (26.4 $\to$ 28.5) and Swin-only (33.6 $\to$ 34.2).
The gain is strongest on vehicles (+4.1 points over raw SAM), where Swin-detected candidate regions are large and SAM tends to undersegment.
Sign IoU drops slightly under Swin + discovery ($-$2.4 points vs.\ Swin-only), consistent with connected-component discovery masks being coarser than SAM proposals for frequently occurring structured objects.

\textbf{VLM triage uniquely benefits the rare human class.}
The full pipeline (triage + discovery) achieves 0.148 human IoU—the highest across all variants and 2.9 points above Swin + discovery (0.119).
Both configurations add the same 28 human-class discovery objects (21 cyclists, 7 pedestrians); the gap is attributable to VLM triage removing false vehicle and sign masks that otherwise compete for model capacity on the rare human class.
This confirms that VLM semantic reasoning adds unique value for rare-class recovery, while Swin pixel-level agreement is the stronger signal for dominant classes.

The best-performing single annotation set is \textbf{Swin + discovery} (34.2 mIoU), suggesting the primary contribution of the VLM in our pipeline is confirmation of discovery candidates rather than triage rejection.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This work introduced a modular multi-agent framework for validating, refining, and augmenting SAM-based semantic segmentation annotations in autonomous driving. 
By combining object-scale multimodal crops with specialized visual–LLM agents and a domain-specific Swin Transformer, the system provides interpretable, risk-aware triage in which destructive rejection requires agreement between independent judges. 
This prevents false-positive decisions from compounding while preserving transparency and reproducibility.

A key insight is that a dataset-adapted segmentation model offers a stronger and more stable quality signal than a general-purpose VLM for dominant-class triage, while the VLM contributes most strongly in two specific roles: (1) confirming Swin-discovered candidates, where a 45.5\% confirmation rate filters noisy region proposals into reliable new annotations; and (2) removing majority-class annotation noise that otherwise suppresses rare-class learning—the full pipeline achieves the best human-class IoU (0.148) despite lower overall mIoU than Swin-only filtering.
Class-aware thresholds further ensure that small-object masks are not over-rejected due to lower spatial resolution.

Beyond filtering erroneous masks, the Swin-based discovery module recovers objects missed by SAM, enabling a bidirectional correction process that addresses both false positives and false negatives. 
The clean/flagged frame partition from our earlier annotation campaign provides large-scale weak supervision and supports downstream experiments in which filtered versions of discarded frames are returned to the training pool.

The architecture is backend-agnostic and supports local deployment, making it suitable for privacy-sensitive workflows. 
Cross-dataset evaluation on iSEAuto validates generalization across sensor configurations and recording conditions.
Future work includes adaptive prompting, multi-agent debate mechanisms, and extensions to panoptic and temporal segmentation. 
Overall, the framework offers a reproducible foundation for scalable annotation quality control in autonomous-driving datasets.
