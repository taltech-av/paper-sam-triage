\begin{abstract}
The Segment Anything Model (SAM) makes large-scale pseudo-labeling feasible for autonomous-driving perception, but its masks drift at boundaries, hallucinate regions, and miss small objects—errors concentrated in safety-critical cyclist and pedestrian classes.
Vision–language models (VLMs) are increasingly proposed to filter such labels, yet it is unclear when their reasoning helps versus when a cheaper, domain-adapted signal suffices.

We answer this through a semi-supervised pipeline on the Zenseact Open Dataset (ZOD): 2{,}319 curated frames train a domain-adapted Swin Transformer that scores SAM mask quality; a zero-shot VLM provides independent object-level verification.
Both signals score masks from 4{,}135 unannotated flagged frames; a Swin-driven discovery module additionally recovers objects SAM missed.
We evaluate with two open-weight VLMs (LLaVA-1.6-34B and Qwen2.5-VL-72B), measuring downstream effect by training a camera–LiDAR fusion model on every annotation variant.

The domain-adapted Swin subsumes the VLM at every stage: Swin + discovery without VLM confirmation (50.6 mIoU) outperforms VLM-confirmed discovery (49.4 mIoU) and the curated SAM baseline (44.4 mIoU), while VLM-only triage falls below unfiltered SAM.
All decisions trace to explicit outputs with no learned fusion weights, enabling full reproducibility without re-running VLM inference.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Pixel-level annotations for autonomous driving are expensive, yet the Segment Anything Model (SAM) \cite{kirillov2023sam} has made large-scale pseudo-labeling feasible.
The problem is reliability: SAM masks drift at boundaries, hallucinate in texturally uniform regions, and miss small traffic participants—errors that cluster around safety-critical cyclists, pedestrians, and signs.
Geometric filtering approaches \cite{zou2019confidence,lee2013pseudo,jaritz2020xmuda} cannot address these failures: a mask with a plausible shape but the wrong semantic class passes every geometric check and corrupts downstream training.
VLMs \cite{liu2024llava,wang2024qwen2vl} offer richer semantic priors, but their role in SAM mask refinement—especially over camera–LiDAR inputs and small per-mask crops—has not been systematically studied.

We address this with a semi-supervised pipeline on the Zenseact Open Dataset (ZOD) \cite{alibeigi2023zod}.
A clean partition of 2{,}319 curated frames trains a domain-adapted Swin Transformer \cite{liu2021swin} that scores each SAM mask by per-pixel class agreement; a zero-shot VLM provides independent object-level verification on tight crops.
Rejection requires both signals to concur, preventing any single erroneous judge from silently discarding valid masks.
A Swin-driven discovery module recovers objects SAM missed, addressing both false positives and false negatives.
Downstream quality is measured by training CLFTv2 \cite{gu2024clft}, a camera–LiDAR fusion model, on every annotation variant and evaluating transfer to the iSEAuto dataset.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{a controlled comparison} of domain-adapted segmentation versus VLM verification across all pipeline stages, yielding a clear negative result: VLM confirmation is net negative in both rejection triage and discovery when a domain-adapted model is available,
    \item \textbf{a risk-aware concordance triage rule} in which destructive rejection requires two independent negative signals, with class-aware thresholds that prevent over-rejection of small traffic participants,
    \item \textbf{a Swin-driven discovery module} that recovers objects missed by SAM—adding false-negative corrections without requiring VLM confirmation,
    \item and \textbf{a downstream evaluation} measuring how each annotation variant affects CLFTv2 camera–LiDAR fusion performance across weather conditions and a second dataset, rather than mask-level agreement alone.
\end{itemize}





\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

\subsection{Semantic Segmentation and Pseudo-Labeling in Autonomous Driving}

Semantic segmentation underpins scene understanding in autonomous driving, with camera-based architectures such as FCN, PSPNet, and DeepLab \cite{long2015fcn,zhao2017psp,chen2018deeplab} establishing dense prediction baselines and later work extending these to camera--LiDAR fusion through feature-level alignment \cite{valada2019selfsupervised,jaritz2019multimodal}.
Despite strong performance on established benchmarks, these models depend on pixel-accurate supervision that is expensive to collect at scale.

Pseudo-labeling and self-training methods \cite{lee2013pseudo,zou2019confidence} reduce labeling cost by using model predictions as supervision, while cross-modal consistency constraints \cite{jaritz2020xmuda} and LiDAR-projection checks \cite{behley2019semantickitti,caesar2020nuscenes} leverage geometric redundancy to filter erroneous labels.
These approaches improve annotation efficiency but rely on fixed thresholds and cannot detect semantic errors—a mask with geometrically plausible shape but misassigned class label passes all geometric checks and corrupts training.
Long-tail classes such as cyclists and pedestrians, which are underrepresented in both labels and model predictions, are particularly susceptible to this failure mode.

\subsection{Foundation Models for Scalable Annotation}

The Segment Anything Model (SAM) \cite{kirillov2023sam} has fundamentally changed the economics of mask generation: class-agnostic proposals can be produced for every object in a scene without per-class prompts or labeled training data.
This has motivated large-scale pseudo-annotation pipelines for autonomous driving, where SAM proposals are matched to semantic categories via overlaid sensor data or downstream classifiers.
However, SAM's class-agnostic design means it cannot verify the semantic correctness of its own output.
Boundary drift occurs where object edges are ambiguous in RGB alone; hallucinated regions arise in texturally uniform areas such as sky and vegetation; and small objects are frequently missed when their image footprint falls below SAM's effective resolution.
Post-hoc semantic validation of SAM proposals is therefore a necessary complement to any SAM-based annotation pipeline.

\subsection{Vision–Language Models and Multi-Signal Filtering}

Vision–language models (VLMs) combine image encoders with autoregressive language decoders, enabling structured reasoning over visual content \cite{liu2024llava,ye2023mplug,wang2024qwen2vl}.
The LLM-as-judge paradigm \cite{zheng2023judging} has established that language models can reliably evaluate the quality of other models' outputs when given structured prompts and constrained output formats, providing a scalable alternative to human review for certain quality-assessment tasks.
In the annotation context, this suggests VLMs can serve as semantic judges over mask proposals—verifying object identity and flagging implausible regions—without requiring fine-tuning on task-specific labels.

A complementary line of work studies ensemble and multi-signal filtering for label noise reduction \cite{malach2017decoupling,han2018co}: requiring independent classifiers to agree before rejecting a sample prevents any single erroneous judge from silently discarding valid training examples.
This principle directly motivates our triage rule: rejection requires concordance between at least two independent negative signals—Swin quality and VLM verdict—so that disagreement escalates the mask rather than deleting it.
Prior work has not applied such concordance-based filtering to SAM mask validation over camera--LiDAR inputs, nor compared pixel-level segmentation and zero-shot VLM signals as independent quality judges in a single annotation pipeline.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

The system refines SAM-generated masks using a set of specialized visual–LLM agents and a domain-specific segmentation model. 
Each agent receives a multimodal input bundle consisting of an RGB crop, a mask overlay, a LiDAR depth projection, and structured metadata, and returns a categorical decision. 
All outputs feed into a deterministic triage rule designed for safety‑critical annotation workflows.

\subsection{Multimodal Input Construction}

For each SAM proposal, we extract a tight crop around the bounding box and generate three aligned channels: the RGB crop, the mask overlay, and a projected LiDAR depth map. 
Metadata describing geometric properties and LiDAR support is provided as structured text. 
Object‑scale cropping is essential: small traffic participants often occupy only 10–30 pixels, and full‑frame VLM queries fail to resolve them reliably. 
We therefore upscale each crop to at least 224 pixels and query one mask at a time, using the full frame only as optional context.

\subsection{Visual–LLM Agents}

The system decomposes mask assessment into three VLM agents (BBox, Failure‑Mode, and Correction), one Swin‑based quality agent, and one deterministic geometric check.
Per-mask execution order is: Consistency Check, BBox Agent, Swin agreement, then Correction on the refine path only.

\paragraph{Consistency Check}
A deterministic LiDAR‑support test run first, before any VLM call: the fraction of mask pixels with projected LiDAR returns must exceed $\tau{=}0.1$.
This check reliably detects masks leaking into sky or void regions at zero inference cost.

\paragraph{Bounding‑Box Agent}
Performs blind forced‑choice classification over a fixed vocabulary of object classes and distractors.
The agent is not told the expected class to avoid agreement bias.
Outputs are: \texttt{valid} (matches expected class), \texttt{background} (vegetation, building, sky, snow), or \texttt{invalid}.
The agent sees only the crop; providing the full frame causes scene‑level bias.

\paragraph{Quality Agent}
Evaluates mask correctness using a Swin Transformer \cite{liu2021swin} trained on clean ZOD frames.
A single forward pass produces a per‑pixel class map for the entire frame; per-mask agreement~$\alpha$ is computed from that shared prediction without an additional forward pass.
Because Swin achieves high mIoU on large objects but lower mIoU on cyclists and pedestrians, we use class‑specific thresholds: $\tau_q^{\text{large}}{=}0.30$, $\tau_q^{\text{small}}{=}0.15$.

\paragraph{Correction Agent}
Called only on the refine path (\texttt{bbox=valid}, $\alpha{\geq}\tau_q$, \texttt{consistency=fail}): determines whether the mask can be improved and proposes a single refinement step (\texttt{refine} or \texttt{no\_refine}).

\paragraph{Failure‑Mode Agent}
Classifies the dominant error type (\texttt{boundary\_drift}, \texttt{hallucination}, \texttt{occlusion\_miss}, \texttt{fragmentation}).
Diagnostic only; does not affect triage outcomes.

All VLM agents return constrained categorical outputs to ensure reproducibility.

\subsection{Swin‑Based Object Discovery}

Triage removes false positives but cannot detect objects SAM missed. 
We therefore introduce a discovery step that compares the Swin class map to the SAM annotation at $384{\times}384$ resolution. 
Pixels where Swin predicts a non‑background class but SAM predicts background form candidate regions. 
Connected components below a minimum size are discarded; the largest remaining components (up to $K_{\text{disc}}$ per frame) are cropped and verified by a VLM. 
For vehicles and signs, the VLM confirms presence; for humans, it also disambiguates cyclist vs.\ pedestrian. 
Confirmed regions are added as new masks, modifying only pixels still labeled as background.

\subsection{Risk‑Aware Deterministic Triage}

Instead of weighted scoring, we use a deterministic rule requiring concordant evidence for rejection. 
Rejection is destructive—deleted pixels cannot be recovered—so relying on any single negative signal would compound false positives across agents. 
Let $\mathcal{N}=\{\texttt{invalid},\texttt{bad},\texttt{fail}\}$ denote negative signals.

A mask is \textbf{rejected} if at least two signals in $\mathcal{N}$ are present. 
A \texttt{background} verdict counts as two signals and rejects immediately.

A mask is \textbf{accepted} if the BBox agent outputs \texttt{valid}, the quality agent outputs \texttt{good}, and the consistency check outputs \texttt{pass}.

A mask is routed to \textbf{refinement} if it is valid and of good quality but fails the consistency check and the correction agent recommends refinement.

All remaining cases—exactly one dissenting judge—are sent to \textbf{human review}. 
Review masks are retained but flagged, ensuring that no single erroneous judgment silently deletes valid labels.

\subsection{Interpretability and Reproducibility}

All prompts, thresholds, and decision rules are fixed before evaluation. 
Every triage outcome can be traced to explicit agent outputs, and no learned fusion weights or continuous scores are used, ensuring reproducibility across datasets and models.


\section{Experiments and Results}
\label{sec:experiments}

We evaluate the proposed multi-agent annotation system on the Zenseact Open Dataset (ZOD) \cite{alibeigi2023zod} using SAM-generated mask proposals from our upstream pipeline \cite{gu2024clft}. 
All agents return categorical decisions, and all triage outcomes follow the deterministic rules in Section~\ref{sec:methodology}. 
No continuous scores or weighted combinations are used.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

Each candidate frame in our previous annotation campaign was manually inspected and either accepted or discarded, producing a \emph{clean} and a \emph{flagged} partition. 
Table~\ref{tab:dataset} summarizes the dataset. 
Flagged frames contain more proposals per frame, consistent with crowded scenes being harder for SAM and more likely to fail inspection. 
This partition provides large-scale weak supervision for evaluating triage behavior, while a stratified subset of proposals is labeled at mask level for agent-level evaluation.

\paragraph{Train / Validation / Test Splits}
Both partitions are split independently using the same stratified procedure.
Frames are grouped into ten strata defined by weather condition (day-fair, day-rain, night-fair, night-rain, snow) crossed with rare-class presence (cyclist or pedestrian pixels $\geq 50$), so every split mirrors the overall weather and class-frequency distribution.
Within each stratum, 10\,\% of frames are held out for validation and the remainder is allocated between test and training.
Because night-rain and snow conditions are under-represented in ZOD, we enforce a minimum of 40 frames per weather category in the test set, increasing the effective test ratio for small conditions only.
Per-weather test subsets are used for all downstream evaluations to isolate weather-specific mIoU.

\paragraph{VLM Configuration}
We evaluate two open-weight VLMs served via Ollama on a single A100 GPU: Qwen2.5-VL-72B \cite{bai2025qwen25vl} and LLaVA-1.6-34B \cite{liu2024llava}.
The Swin Quality Agent runs once per frame and is shared across both models; only the BBox, Correction, and Failure-Mode agents differ.
All triage-rule ablations are performed offline using stored agent outputs.

\subsection{Downstream Segmentation}

We train CLFTv2-Base (RGB--LiDAR fusion) on all seven annotation variants for both VLMs, using the flagged partition (4{,}135 frames, train split from Table~\ref{tab:splits}).
A curated SAM baseline is trained on the clean partition (2{,}319 frames) using the same model.
Rejected pixels are mapped to \texttt{ignore} in all VLM-annotated variants.
Evaluation uses the five per-weather test subsets; results are reported as mIoU averaged over vehicle, sign, and human (cyclist + pedestrian combined).
We additionally include a modality ablation (RGB, LiDAR, Fusion) on the best-performing annotation variant to quantify the contribution of each sensor stream independently of annotation quality.

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} reports vehicle, sign, and combined human-class IoU (cyclist + pedestrian) for all variants.
Four findings stand out.

\textbf{Swin filtering outperforms VLM triage for dominant classes.}
Swin-only filtering achieves 45.7 mIoU versus 40.5 for the full Swin + VLM triage, a 5.2-point gap driven by sign and vehicle classes (sign: 39.2 vs.\ 34.3; vehicle: 69.1 vs.\ 64.6).
The BBox agent's crop-based classification introduces false rejections on small, thin signs where pixel-level Swin agreement is the more reliable signal.
VLM-only triage (37.6 mIoU) falls below even the unfiltered raw SAM baseline (38.7), confirming that the BBox agent is an unreliable standalone rejection judge.

\textbf{Discovery consistently adds value; VLM confirmation in discovery is not needed.}
Adding discovery objects boosts mIoU over both base configurations: raw SAM (38.7 $\to$ 43.5) and Swin-only (45.7 $\to$ 50.6).
The gain is strongest on the human class ($+$11.5 IoU over Swin-only), where the discovery module recovers missed pedestrians and cyclists.
Critically, Swin + disc.\ without VLM confirmation (50.6 mIoU; vehicle 65.9, sign 45.8, human 40.3) outperforms VLM-confirmed discovery (49.4 mIoU; vehicle 70.5, sign 40.5, human 37.2).
VLM confirmation in discovery hurts sign IoU by 5.3 points and human IoU by 3.1 points, indicating that the VLM rejects valid sign and pedestrian candidates detected by Swin.

\textbf{VLM confirmation is net negative at every stage.}
In rejection triage, VLM + Swin (40.5) underperforms Swin-only (45.7).
In discovery confirmation, VLM-confirmed (49.4) underperforms no-VLM (50.6).
VLM-only triage (37.6) falls below raw SAM (38.7).
Across all ablations, adding VLM judgment to Swin decisions reduces mIoU—a consistent negative result on the utility of general-purpose VLMs in this pipeline.

\textbf{Pseudo-labels with Swin + discovery outperform the curated SAM baseline.}
Table~\ref{tab:downstream} shows that Swin + disc.\ (no VLM) trained on 4{,}135 pseudo-labeled frames (50.6 mIoU) outperforms the curated SAM baseline trained on 2{,}319 clean frames (44.4 mIoU) across every class, including the safety-critical human class (40.3 vs.\ 31.0 IoU).
This indicates that annotation volume combined with Swin-based triage can compensate for the label quality gap, and that VLM verification is unnecessary to achieve this result.

\textbf{When VLM adds value.}
The negative finding above is conditioned on the availability of a domain-adapted model.
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} also shows that Raw SAM + VLM-confirmed discovery (43.5 mIoU) outperforms unfiltered raw SAM (38.7) by 4.8 points—a gain attributable solely to the VLM, since no Swin filtering is applied in that variant.
The VLM does recover real missed objects and provides a meaningful improvement over the SAM baseline.
It becomes redundant only once a domain-adapted quality signal is available.
The Swin Quality Agent was trained on 2{,}319 clean ZOD frames with direct knowledge of the sensor configuration, weather distribution, and class geometry of this specific dataset; this in-distribution advantage is what Swin exploits and what a general-purpose VLM—trained on internet images—cannot match.
In a zero-shot setting where no domain-adapted model exists, such as when annotating a newly deployed sensor configuration with no labeled reference data, VLM-based discovery represents the most accessible path to recovering missed objects.
The practical implication is that annotation effort is better allocated toward producing a small clean-partition ground truth—which simultaneously serves downstream evaluation and Swin training—than toward querying a general-purpose VLM indefinitely.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This work introduced a modular multi-agent framework for validating, refining, and augmenting SAM-based semantic segmentation annotations in autonomous driving. 
By combining object-scale multimodal crops with specialized visual–LLM agents and a domain-specific Swin Transformer, the system provides interpretable, risk-aware triage in which destructive rejection requires agreement between independent judges. 
This prevents false-positive decisions from compounding while preserving transparency and reproducibility.

A key insight is that a dataset-adapted segmentation model offers a stronger and more stable quality signal than a general-purpose VLM at every stage of the pipeline.
In rejection triage, Swin-only filtering (45.7 mIoU) outperforms both VLM-only (37.6) and the full Swin + VLM triage (40.5).
In discovery, accepting all Swin-detected candidates without VLM confirmation (50.6 mIoU; 40.3 human IoU) outperforms VLM-confirmed discovery (49.4 mIoU; 37.2 human IoU), as the VLM systematically rejects valid sign and pedestrian proposals.
Class-aware thresholds further ensure that small-object masks are not over-rejected due to lower spatial resolution.

This negative result is conditioned on the availability of domain-adapted supervision.
Raw SAM augmented with VLM-confirmed discovery alone achieves 43.5 mIoU—a 4.8-point gain over unfiltered SAM (38.7)—demonstrating that VLM discovery does recover genuine missed objects.
The VLM becomes redundant only because the Swin Quality Agent, trained on 2{,}319 in-domain frames, provides a strictly superior filtering signal.
The practical implication for practitioners is clear: a modest investment in a clean-partition ground truth enables training of a domain-adapted model that outperforms indefinite VLM querying, both in annotation quality and inference cost.
In zero-shot settings with no domain-labeled data, VLM-based discovery remains a valuable bootstrapping tool.

Beyond filtering erroneous masks, the Swin-based discovery module recovers objects missed by SAM without requiring VLM confirmation, enabling a bidirectional correction process that addresses both false positives and false negatives.
The resulting pseudo-labels trained on 4{,}135 flagged frames surpass the curated SAM baseline trained on 2{,}319 clean frames across all classes, including the safety-critical human class (40.3 vs.\ 31.0 IoU).

The architecture is backend-agnostic and supports local deployment, making it suitable for privacy-sensitive workflows.
Future work includes validation on additional datasets and sensor configurations, adaptive discovery thresholds, and extensions to panoptic and temporal segmentation.
Overall, the framework offers a reproducible foundation for scalable annotation quality control in autonomous-driving datasets.
