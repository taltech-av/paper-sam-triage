\begin{abstract}
The Segment Anything Model (SAM) makes large-scale pseudo-labeling feasible for autonomous-driving perception, but its masks drift at boundaries, hallucinate regions, and miss small objects, with errors concentrated in safety-critical cyclist and pedestrian classes.
Vision–language models (VLMs) are increasingly proposed to filter such labels, yet it is unclear when their reasoning helps versus when a cheaper, domain-adapted signal suffices.

We answer this through a semi-supervised pipeline on the Zenseact Open Dataset (ZOD): 2{,}319 curated frames train a domain-adapted Swin Transformer that scores SAM mask quality; a zero-shot VLM provides independent object-level verification.
Both signals score masks from 4{,}135 unannotated flagged frames; a Swin-driven discovery module additionally recovers objects SAM missed.
We evaluate with two open-weight VLMs (LLaVA-1.6-34B and Qwen2.5-VL-72B) using identical fixed prompts for both, measuring downstream effect by training a camera–LiDAR fusion model on every annotation variant.

All variants are evaluated against a single human-curated reference on a common test set, with paired bootstrap confidence intervals.
Under this fixed-prompt, zero-shot setting, the domain-adapted Swin subsumes the VLM: Swin-only filtering reaches 47.6 mIoU, significantly exceeding both the curated SAM baseline trained on the clean partition (44.4 mIoU; $\Delta{=}{+}3.3$, 95\% CI $[+1.5,+5.5]$) and unfiltered SAM pseudo-labels (43.2 mIoU), while adding VLM verdicts to the triage rule yields no significant further gain.
Swin-driven discovery recovers thousands of objects SAM missed; VLM-confirmed discovery still trails Swin-only filtering (best case 45.1 mIoU), but requiring agreement between both VLMs before adding a candidate recovers annotation quality to parity with the curated baseline (44.7 vs.\ 44.4), whereas a single VLM's large, unselective confirmed set approaches the cost of adding every unconfirmed candidate.
Applying the same prompts to Qwen2.5-VL-72B reveals that prompt-response calibration, not model capability, determines annotation behaviour: Qwen accepts 93.3\% of masks as valid versus LLaVA's 62.1\%, yet confirms only 17.8\% of discovery candidates versus 82.5\%, and these behavioural differences propagate directly to downstream training.
All decisions trace to explicit outputs with no learned fusion weights, enabling full reproducibility without re-running VLM inference.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Pixel-level annotations for autonomous driving are expensive, yet the Segment Anything Model (SAM) \cite{kirillov2023sam} has made large-scale pseudo-labeling feasible.
The challenge is reliability: SAM masks drift at boundaries, hallucinate in texturally uniform regions, and miss small traffic participants, with errors concentrated in safety-critical cyclists, pedestrians, and signs.
Geometric filtering approaches \cite{zou2019confidence,lee2013pseudo,jaritz2020xmuda} cannot detect such semantic failures: a mask with a plausible shape but the wrong class label passes every geometric check and degrades downstream training.
Vision–language models (VLMs) \cite{liu2024llava,wang2024qwen2vl} offer richer semantic priors, but their effectiveness for SAM mask refinement, especially on small object crops and multimodal camera–LiDAR inputs, remains unclear.

We study this problem using a semi-supervised pipeline on the Zenseact Open Dataset (ZOD) \cite{alibeigi2023zod}.
A clean partition of 2{,}319 curated frames is used to train a domain-adapted Swin Transformer \cite{liu2021swin} that provides per-pixel class agreement scores for every SAM mask.
A zero-shot VLM supplies an independent object-level verdict on tight crops.
Rejection requires concordance between multiple signals (Swin agreement, VLM verdict, and a deterministic LiDAR consistency check), reducing the risk that any single erroneous judge silently removes valid labels.
A Swin-driven discovery module identifies objects missed by SAM, addressing both false positives and false negatives.
Downstream impact is measured by training CLFTv2 \cite{gu2024clft}, a camera–LiDAR fusion model, on each annotation variant and evaluating performance across weather conditions and a second dataset.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{a controlled comparison} of domain-adapted segmentation and zero-shot VLM verification under identical fixed prompts, showing when each signal is reliable and when it is not,
    \item \textbf{a prompt-portability analysis} demonstrating that VLM acceptance and discovery rates vary dramatically across models under the same prompts, and that this behavioural calibration, not general model capability, determines downstream annotation quality,
    \item \textbf{a Swin-driven discovery mechanism} that recovers objects missed by SAM, together with a downstream analysis showing that discovery quality tracks candidate-set volume and precision, and that requiring two-VLM agreement before confirming a candidate recovers annotation quality to parity with a curated baseline,
    \item and \textbf{a downstream study} under a single curated evaluation reference with paired bootstrap confidence intervals, quantifying how each annotation variant affects camera–LiDAR fusion performance beyond mask agreement.
\end{itemize}





\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

\subsection{Semantic Segmentation and Pseudo-Labeling in Autonomous Driving}

Semantic segmentation underpins scene understanding in autonomous driving, with camera-based architectures such as FCN, PSPNet, and DeepLab \cite{long2015fcn,zhao2017psp,chen2018deeplab} establishing dense prediction baselines and later work extending these to camera--LiDAR fusion through feature-level alignment \cite{valada2019selfsupervised,jaritz2019multimodal}. 
Despite strong performance on established benchmarks, these models depend on pixel-accurate supervision that is expensive to collect at scale.

Pseudo-labeling and self-training methods \cite{lee2013pseudo,zou2019confidence} reduce labeling cost by using model predictions as supervision, while cross-modal consistency constraints \cite{jaritz2020xmuda} and LiDAR-projection checks \cite{behley2019semantickitti,caesar2020nuscenes} leverage geometric redundancy to filter erroneous labels. 
These approaches improve annotation efficiency but rely on fixed thresholds and cannot detect semantic errors: a mask with geometrically plausible shape but an incorrect class label passes all geometric checks and degrades downstream training. 
Rare classes such as cyclists and pedestrians, which occupy small pixel regions and appear infrequently, remain particularly challenging for pseudo-labeling pipelines.

\subsection{Foundation Models for Scalable Annotation}

The Segment Anything Model (SAM) \cite{kirillov2023sam} has changed the economics of mask generation by enabling class-agnostic proposals without per-class prompts or labeled training data. 
This has motivated large-scale pseudo-annotation pipelines for autonomous driving, where SAM proposals are matched to semantic categories using sensor overlays or downstream classifiers. 
However, SAM's class-agnostic design means it cannot verify the semantic correctness of its own output. 
Boundary drift arises where object edges are ambiguous in RGB; hallucinated regions appear in texturally uniform areas such as sky and vegetation; and small objects are frequently missed when their footprint falls below SAM's effective resolution. 
Post-hoc semantic validation is therefore a necessary complement to SAM-based annotation.

\subsection{Vision--Language Models and Multi-Signal Filtering}

Vision--language models (VLMs) combine image encoders with autoregressive language decoders, enabling structured reasoning over visual content \cite{liu2024llava,ye2023mplug,wang2024qwen2vl}. 
The LLM-as-judge paradigm \cite{zheng2023judging} shows that language models can evaluate the quality of other models' outputs when given structured prompts and constrained output formats, providing a scalable alternative to human review in certain settings. 
In annotation pipelines, this suggests VLMs may serve as semantic judges over mask proposals, verifying object identity and flagging implausible regions, without requiring fine-tuning on task-specific labels.

A complementary line of work studies ensemble and multi-signal filtering for label-noise reduction \cite{malach2017decoupling,han2018co}. 
Requiring agreement between independent classifiers before rejecting a sample reduces the risk that any single erroneous judge silently discards valid training examples. 
This principle motivates our concordance-based triage rule: rejection requires at least two negative signals among Swin agreement, VLM verdict, and LiDAR consistency, so that disagreement escalates the mask rather than deleting it. 
Prior work has not applied such concordance-based filtering to SAM mask validation over camera--LiDAR inputs, nor compared pixel-level segmentation and zero-shot VLM signals as independent quality judges within a unified annotation pipeline.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

The system refines SAM-generated masks using a set of specialized visual--LLM agents and a domain-adapted segmentation model. 
Most agents receive a multimodal input bundle consisting of an RGB crop, a mask overlay, a LiDAR depth projection, and structured metadata, and return a categorical decision. 
The BBox Agent is an exception and receives only the RGB crop to reduce scene-level bias. 
All agent outputs feed into a deterministic triage rule designed for safety-critical annotation workflows.

\subsection{Multimodal Input Construction}

For each SAM proposal, we extract a tight crop around the bounding box and generate three aligned channels: the RGB crop, the mask overlay, and a projected LiDAR depth map. 
Metadata describing geometric properties and LiDAR support is provided as structured text. 
Object-scale cropping is essential: small traffic participants often occupy only 10--30 pixels, and full-frame VLM queries struggle to resolve them reliably. 
Each crop is upscaled to at least $224{\times}224$ pixels and processed independently; the full frame is used only as optional context.

\subsection{Visual--LLM Agents}

The system decomposes mask assessment into three VLM agents (BBox, Failure--Mode, and Correction), one Swin-based quality agent, and one deterministic geometric check. 
Per-mask execution order is: Consistency Check, BBox Agent, Swin agreement, then Correction on the refine path only.

All VLM agents use a single fixed prompt per role, applied identically to both LLaVA-1.6-34B and Qwen2.5-VL-72B without any model-specific calibration.
This reflects the realistic deployment constraint of large-scale annotation pipelines, where per-model prompt tuning is infeasible, and it enables a direct test of how well fixed prompts transfer across VLMs of different architectures and capability levels.

\paragraph{Consistency Check}
A deterministic LiDAR-support test is run first, before any VLM call: the fraction of mask pixels with projected LiDAR returns must exceed $\tau{=}0.1$. 
This check reliably detects masks leaking into sky or void regions at zero inference cost.

\paragraph{Bounding-Box Agent}
Performs blind forced-choice classification over a fixed vocabulary of object classes and distractors. 
The agent is not told the expected class to avoid agreement bias. 
Traffic lights and pole-mounted road lighting are included in the vocabulary and mapped to the \texttt{sign} class following ZOD annotation conventions. 
Outputs are: \texttt{valid}, \texttt{background}, or \texttt{invalid}. 
The agent sees only the RGB crop; overlays and depth channels are withheld to reduce the risk of classifying surrounding context rather than the marked object.

\paragraph{Quality Agent}
Evaluates mask correctness using a Swin Transformer \cite{liu2021swin} trained on clean ZOD frames. 
A single forward pass produces a per-pixel class map for the entire frame; per-mask agreement~$\alpha$ is computed from that shared prediction without an additional forward pass. 
Because Swin achieves higher mIoU on large objects than on cyclists and pedestrians, we use class-specific thresholds: $\tau_q^{\text{large}}{=}0.30$ and $\tau_q^{\text{small}}{=}0.15$. 
For ablation studies, all agents are called on every mask to enable direct signal comparison; bypass thresholds ($0.70$ for large classes, $0.40$ for small classes) are used only for deployment efficiency and are not applied during evaluation.

\paragraph{Correction Agent}
Called only on the refine path (\texttt{bbox=valid}, $\alpha{\geq}\tau_q$, \texttt{consistency=fail}). 
Determines whether the mask can be improved and proposes a single refinement step (\texttt{refine} or \texttt{no\_refine}).

\paragraph{Failure-Mode Agent}
Classifies the dominant error type (\texttt{boundary\_drift}, \texttt{hallucination}, \texttt{occlusion\_miss}, \texttt{fragmentation}). 
Diagnostic only; does not affect triage outcomes.

All VLM agents return constrained categorical outputs to ensure reproducibility.

\subsection{Swin-Based Object Discovery}

Triage removes false positives but cannot detect objects SAM missed. 
We therefore introduce a discovery step that compares the Swin class map to the SAM annotation at $384{\times}384$ resolution. 
Pixels where Swin predicts a non-background class but SAM predicts background form candidate regions. 
Connected components below a minimum size are discarded; the largest remaining components (up to $K_{\text{disc}}$ per frame) are cropped and verified by a VLM. 
For vehicles and signs, the VLM confirms presence; for humans, it also disambiguates cyclist vs.\ pedestrian. 
Confirmed regions are added as new masks, modifying only pixels still labeled as background.

\subsection{Risk-Aware Deterministic Triage}

Instead of weighted scoring, we use a deterministic rule requiring concordant evidence for rejection. 
Rejection is destructive: deleted pixels cannot be recovered, so relying on any single negative signal would compound false positives across agents. 
Let $\mathcal{N}=\{\texttt{invalid},\texttt{bad},\texttt{fail}\}$ denote negative signals.

A mask is \textbf{rejected} if at least two signals in $\mathcal{N}$ are present. 
A \texttt{background} verdict counts as two signals and rejects immediately. 
To avoid over-rejection of small objects, when \texttt{bbox=invalid} but the Quality Agent scores the mask as \texttt{good}, the \texttt{invalid} verdict is downgraded to a single negative signal. 
The mask is then routed to human review when consistency passes, or rejected only when consistency additionally fails.

A mask is \textbf{accepted} if the BBox agent outputs \texttt{valid}, the Quality Agent outputs \texttt{good}, and the Consistency Check outputs \texttt{pass}.

A mask is routed to \textbf{refinement} if it is valid and of good quality but fails the Consistency Check and the Correction Agent recommends refinement.

All remaining cases, with exactly one dissenting judge, are sent to \textbf{human review}. 
Review masks are retained but flagged, ensuring that no single erroneous judgment silently deletes valid labels.

\subsection{Interpretability and Reproducibility}

All prompts, thresholds, and decision rules are fixed before evaluation. 
Every triage outcome can be traced to explicit agent outputs, and no learned fusion weights or continuous scores are used, supporting reproducibility across datasets and models.

\section{Experiments and Results}
\label{sec:experiments}

We evaluate the proposed multi-agent annotation system on the Zenseact Open Dataset (ZOD) \cite{alibeigi2023zod} using SAM-generated mask proposals from our upstream annotation pipeline (released alongside this work; see Reproducibility below).
All agents return categorical decisions, and all triage outcomes follow the deterministic rules in Section~\ref{sec:methodology}. 
No continuous scores or weighted combinations are used.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

Each candidate frame in our previous annotation campaign was manually inspected and either accepted or discarded, producing a \emph{clean} and a \emph{flagged} partition. 
Table~\ref{tab:dataset} summarizes the dataset. 
Flagged frames contain more proposals per frame, consistent with crowded scenes being harder for SAM and more likely to fail inspection. 
This partition provides large-scale weak supervision for evaluating triage behavior, while a stratified subset of proposals is labeled at mask level for agent-level evaluation.

\paragraph{Train / Validation / Test Splits}
Both partitions are split independently using the same stratified procedure.
Frames are grouped into ten strata defined by weather condition (day-fair, day-rain, night-fair, night-rain, snow) crossed with rare-class presence (cyclist or pedestrian pixels $\geq 50$), so every split mirrors the overall weather and class-frequency distribution.
Within each stratum, 10\,\% of frames are held out for validation and the remainder is allocated between test and training.
Because night-rain and snow conditions are under-represented in ZOD, we enforce a minimum of 40 frames per weather category in the test set, increasing the effective test ratio for small conditions only.
All downstream evaluations use the per-weather test subsets of the \emph{clean} partition (565 frames total), scored against the manually curated annotations; Section~\ref{sec:experiments} details this protocol.

\paragraph{VLM Configuration}
We evaluate two open-weight VLMs served via Ollama on a single A100 GPU: LLaVA-1.6-34B \cite{liu2024llava} and Qwen2.5-VL-72B \cite{bai2025qwen25vl}.

\paragraph{Computational Cost}
Table~\ref{tab:timing} reports inference time extracted from per-mask and per-frame timings logged during annotation.
The BBox agent dominates cost: a single crop verification call takes 3.56s (LLaVA) or 14.15s (Qwen) on average, and is issued once per mask, so per-mask latency scales almost linearly with model size.
The Correction agent, triggered only for the 1.5--1.8\% of masks that fail the LiDAR consistency check, adds a comparable per-call cost but touches few masks overall.
The Swin Quality Agent runs once per frame at negligible cost ($<$0.01s/mask), confirming that domain-adapted CNN triage is essentially free relative to VLM inference.
Annotating the full flagged partition (4{,}135 frames, 90{,}761 masks) on a single A100 with 4 concurrent frame workers took approximately 33 GPU-hours with LLaVA-1.6-34B versus 123 GPU-hours with Qwen2.5-VL-72B, a $3.7\times$ cost increase that is not matched by a proportional accuracy gain (Table~\ref{tab:agent_performance}).
Estimated VRAM footprint (quantized weights plus a bounded KV cache at the deployed parallelism) is $\sim$22GB for LLaVA-1.6-34B versus $\sim$51GB for Qwen2.5-VL-72B, comfortably within the single A100-80GB used for both models.

\subsection{Downstream Segmentation}

We train CLFTv2-Base (RGB--LiDAR fusion) on all annotation variants.
VLM-dependent variants use the flagged partition (4{,}135 frames, train split from Table~\ref{tab:splits}); a curated SAM baseline uses the clean partition (2{,}319 frames) to provide a quality-controlled reference at a smaller annotation volume.
Rejected pixels are mapped to \texttt{ignore} in all VLM-annotated variants.

\paragraph{Evaluation Protocol}
Comparing annotation variants requires a reference that is independent of every variant's own training labels: scoring a model against the same pseudo-labels it was trained on measures label self-consistency rather than segmentation quality, and inflates precisely the variants with the most idiosyncratic labels.
We therefore evaluate every trained model on the same held-out frames, the five per-weather test subsets of the clean partition (565 frames), against the manually curated annotations, the only human-verified reference available.
Results are reported as mIoU averaged over vehicle, sign, and human (cyclist + pedestrian combined), where the overall mIoU is the mean of the five per-weather mIoUs.
Uncertainty is quantified with a paired bootstrap: test frames are resampled with replacement 10{,}000 times within each weather stratum, with identical resample indices for all variants, yielding 95\% confidence intervals for each variant and for pairwise mIoU differences.
One caveat is inherent to this protocol: the curated reference is itself SAM-derived, so objects that SAM missed are absent from it, and a model that correctly segments such objects is penalised.
Discovery-related results below should be read with this conservative bias in mind.
We additionally include a modality ablation (RGB, LiDAR, Fusion) on the Swin + disc.\ variant to quantify each sensor stream's contribution independently of annotation quality.

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} reports vehicle, sign, and combined human-class IoU (cyclist + pedestrian) for all variants.
Several observations emerge from these results.

\textbf{Swin-filtered pseudo-labels surpass the curated baseline.}
Swin-only filtering achieves 47.6 mIoU, the best result of any variant, exceeding the curated SAM baseline trained on 2{,}319 manually reviewed frames (44.4; paired $\Delta{=}{+}3.3$, 95\% CI $[+1.5,+5.5]$, $P(\Delta{\leq}0){=}0.0004$) and unfiltered raw SAM (43.2; $\Delta{=}{+}4.5$, CI $[+3.0,+6.2]$).
Annotation volume combined with a domain-adapted quality signal therefore compensates for the label-quality gap of pseudo-labels: filtering alone, with no VLM involvement, converts 4{,}135 flagged frames into better training data than 2{,}319 hand-curated ones.

\textbf{VLM triage adds no significant gain over Swin.}
Full triage (Swin + VLM + consistency) reaches 44.5 mIoU with LLaVA and 43.9 with Qwen, both \emph{below} Swin-only filtering ($\Delta{=}{-}3.2$, CI $[-5.1,-1.5]$ for LLaVA), and VLM-only triage without the Swin signal is statistically indistinguishable from full triage (LLaVA: 43.7 vs.\ 44.5, $\Delta{=}{+}0.9$, CI $[-1.2,+2.9]$).
Crop-based VLM rejection removes masks that pixel-level Swin agreement retains, and the removals hurt more than they help under the fixed-prompt configuration studied here.

\textbf{Discovery regions carry a volume-dependent annotation cost, distinct from mask geometry.}
An earlier version of this analysis painted discovery candidates as filled bounding-box rectangles rather than their true connected-component shape; retraining every discovery variant on the exact object masks changes the no-VLM result substantially (21.1 $\to$ 39.5 mIoU) while leaving the VLM-confirmed variants nearly unchanged (Qwen: 46.0 $\to$ 45.1; LLaVA: 41.0 $\to$ 40.5), confirming that rectangle noise scales with the number of unconfirmed candidates and contributes little once a VLM has already trimmed the set.
With this confound removed, discovery still reduces mIoU relative to Swin-only in every configuration: Swin-only (0 regions, 47.6) $>$ Qwen-confirmed (9{,}878 regions, 45.1; $\Delta{=}{+}2.6$, CI $[+1.1,+4.4]$) $>$ LLaVA-confirmed (45{,}882 regions, 40.5; $\Delta{=}{+}7.2$, CI $[+5.6,+9.1]$) $\approx$ unconfirmed (all 55{,}630 candidates, 39.5; $\Delta{=}{+}8.2$, CI $[+6.5,+9.9]$).
Because the curated evaluation reference is itself SAM-derived, it cannot credit genuine recoveries (see Evaluation Protocol), so part of this cost is an artifact of the reference rather than the annotations; the remainder tracks candidate-set precision, larger, less selective confirmed sets (LLaVA's 45{,}882) approach the unconfirmed case (39.5 vs.\ 40.5, CI $[-0.0,+1.9]$, not clearly separable), while smaller, more selective sets (Qwen's 9{,}878) retain most of Swin-only's advantage.

\textbf{Cross-model consensus recovers discovery to curated-baseline parity.}
Requiring both VLMs to independently confirm a discovery candidate, the same concordance principle used for triage rejection, yields a smaller set (8{,}703 objects) than either VLM alone and closes most of the gap: Swin filtering with both-confirmed discovery reaches 44.7 mIoU, statistically indistinguishable from the curated baseline (44.4; $\Delta{=}{+}0.3$, CI $[-1.6,+2.0]$) and from Qwen's own selective confirmation (45.1; $\Delta{=}{-}0.4$, CI $[-1.5,+0.6]$), while significantly exceeding LLaVA's confirmed set (40.5; $\Delta{=}{+}4.2$, CI $[+2.4,+5.8]$).
Agreement between independent VLMs thus functions as a precision filter for discovery candidates, though it remains below Swin-only filtering with no discovery at all (47.6; $\Delta{=}{+}3.0$, CI $[+1.3,+5.1]$).
Applying the more permissive union-reject triage rule (Section~\ref{sec:methodology} ablations) to the same both-confirmed discovery set instead reaches only 42.6, below either single-VLM triage (LLaVA 44.5, Qwen 43.9), reinforcing that Swin-based filtering, not VLM agreement, is what drives triage quality.

\textbf{Discovery inverts the VLM ranking.}
Under identical prompts, Qwen confirms far fewer discovery candidates than LLaVA (17.8\% vs.\ 82.5\%, Table~\ref{tab:agent_behavior}), which Section~\ref{sec:experiments} initially suggests is excessive scepticism.
Downstream, that scepticism is protective: Qwen's Swin + disc.\ variant scores 45.1 versus LLaVA's 40.5 ($\Delta{=}{+}4.6$, CI $[+3.3,+5.8]$).
Prompt-response calibration thus propagates directly to downstream performance, and the direction of the effect depends on whether the elicited behaviour (acceptance or scepticism) matches the reliability of the candidate stream, not on model capability or recency.

\textbf{The triage limitation fixes show no significant downstream effect at this scale.}
Section~\ref{sec:experiments} identifies two triage weaknesses from qualitative inspection: unilateral rejection on a bare \texttt{background} verdict, and a LiDAR support threshold that is not class-aware.
Applying both fixes (\texttt{background\_protected} and \texttt{lidar\_class\_aware}) rescues 1{,}002 masks (914{,}277 pixels: 892 signs, 66 cyclists, 37 pedestrians, 7 vehicles) from rejection under offline replay, matching the classes and mechanism of the qualitative examples in Fig.~\ref{fig:limitations}.
Retraining LLaVA's Swin + disc.\ variant with the fixed triage rule, holding the discovery set constant, gives 39.1 mIoU versus 40.5 for the unfixed rule ($\Delta{=}{-}1.2$, CI $[-2.3,+0.1]$), not a significant difference.
The rescued masks are a small fraction of the 90{,}761-mask training set, and their effect may be swamped by the same discovery-volume cost identified above; isolating the triage fix from a no-discovery baseline is left to future work.

\textbf{Prompt-response calibration dominates model capability.}
Table~\ref{tab:agent_behavior} compares per-VLM agent outputs on the same 90{,}761 masks using identical prompts.
The BBox verdicts diverge sharply: LLaVA returns \texttt{valid} on 62.1\% of crops while Qwen returns \texttt{valid} on 93.3\%, suggesting the fixed forced-choice prompt elicits a near-unconditional acceptance response from Qwen rather than the selective judgement it produces in LLaVA.
The miscalibration is class-specific: sign rejection drops from 26.6\% to 5.3\% and cyclist rejection from 47.4\% to 8.1\%, so Qwen retains many small-object masks that LLaVA rejects.
The pattern inverts for discovery: Qwen confirms only 17.8\% of Swin-proposed candidates (9{,}878 objects, 490 human-class) against LLaVA's 82.5\% (45{,}882 objects, 6{,}415 human-class).
Neither behaviour is uniformly better: Qwen's permissive triage and LLaVA's permissive discovery each admit noise, and as Table~\ref{tab:ablation} shows, the downstream consequences track which candidate stream the permissiveness is applied to, with Qwen's conservative discovery confirmation yielding the higher downstream mIoU.
These results show that identical prompts can produce qualitatively different annotation behaviours across VLMs, and that downstream outcomes track prompt-response calibration more closely than general model capability.

\textbf{Within-model prompt sensitivity.}
The cross-model divergence above could in principle reflect stable model dispositions rather than prompt effects.
A development-stage revision of the prompt set provides a direct within-model test: an earlier Qwen2.5-VL-72B run with the original prompts covers 1{,}000 of the same flagged frames (13{,}612 masks), and all VLM-independent signals are bit-identical across the two runs.
Table~\ref{tab:prompt_sensitivity} compares the two prompt versions on this overlap subset, with triage outcomes recomputed under the final deterministic rule so that differences reflect prompt and response-protocol changes only.
The revision, wording intended to improve small-object handling plus a stricter response-parsing protocol, flipped 25.9\% of Qwen's BBox verdicts and raised its valid rate from 64.4\% to 85.2\%; under the original prompts, Qwen's verdict distribution had been close to LLaVA's under the revised prompts (64.4\% vs.\ 63.7\% valid).
The vehicle discovery prompt was not revised and its confirmation rate is unchanged (54.3\% vs.\ 54.4\%), ruling out run-to-run sampling noise, while the revised sign and human discovery prompts shifted confirmation by $+$11.7 and $+$9.7 points.
Qwen's near-unconditional acceptance is therefore not an innate disposition but a model-specific response to a prompt revision that left LLaVA selective, and the within-model prompt effect (25.9\% of verdicts) is of the same order as the cross-model disagreement under identical prompts (40.6--42.9\% on the same subset).
Both findings point to the same practical requirement: any change of model \emph{or} prompt in a VLM annotation pipeline requires behavioural re-validation.

\subsection{Cross-Dataset Generalisation}

To assess whether annotation quality affects the transferability of learned representations, we fine-tune CLFTv2-Base CrossFusion on the iSEAuto dataset starting from three initialisations: ImageNet weights only (scratch), and ZOD-trained checkpoints from raw SAM and Swin + disc.\ (no VLM).
All runs use a reset learning rate; the comparison focuses on \emph{convergence speed}: a checkpoint encoding higher-quality semantic features should reach a given validation mIoU in fewer epochs.
iSEAuto contains vehicle and human classes only (no sign); mIoU is averaged over those two classes.

Figure~\ref{fig:iseauto_convergence} shows the full convergence trajectory (5-epoch smoothed mIoU, sampled every 5 epochs from epoch 10 to 90).
The Swin + disc.\ (no VLM) initialisation separates from the other two at epoch 20 (52.3 vs.\ 49.8 for scratch, smoothed), while the raw SAM checkpoint follows the ImageNet-initialisation curve throughout, showing no benefit from domain exposure alone.
By epoch 30 all three methods have converged to the same trajectory ($\approx$55 mIoU), and by epoch 90 all reach approximately the same final level ($\approx$67 mIoU).
This transfer result is notable because the same Swin + disc.\ checkpoint scores \emph{lowest} against the curated ZOD reference (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}): representations shaped by Swin-filtered labels with dense discovery supervision adapt faster to a new domain even though the discovery regions depress mIoU against a SAM-derived reference.
The dissociation is consistent with the evaluation-protocol caveat of Section~\ref{sec:experiments}, discovery supervision may encode real objects the reference cannot credit, but it may equally reflect a generic benefit of denser foreground supervision; distinguishing the two requires human-verified evaluation labels.

\subsection{Qualitative Analysis}

Fig.~\ref{fig:qualitative} walks through five situations the pipeline actually ran into on the flagged partition (LLaVA-1.6-34B), showing not just what each agent decided but why.

\textbf{(a) Hallucination.} Sometimes SAM proposes a mask where the labeled object simply isn't there, and the BBox agent catches it by returning \texttt{background}. One mask claims a \texttt{vehicle} inside a dense hedge; the other claims a \texttt{sign} on a gantry's bare steel support lattice; the real sign panel is a few pixels away, just outside the mask. Both are correctly rejected.

\textbf{(b) Consistency failure.} A parked bicycle is a real object: the BBox agent says \texttt{valid} and Swin agrees (\texttt{quality=good}, 82\% agreement), yet only 9.1\% of its mask carries a LiDAR return, short of the 10\% threshold, so the Consistency Check fails. The bottom crop shows why: a bicycle's thin wire frame reflects far fewer LiDAR beams than a solid object of the same size, so most beams pass straight through the mask and hit the wall behind it instead (bright, dense returns on the wall; sparse returns inside the green outline). Since this pattern is indistinguishable from a genuine sky leak, the pipeline can't just accept the mask, but it also should not delete a real bicycle, so it sends the case to the Correction Agent to refine instead of rejecting.

\textbf{(c) Depth-support rejection.} A pedestrian-shaped statue on a plinth fools the BBox VLM into calling it \texttt{valid}, but the other two signals disagree with it and with each other's certainty. Swin's own dense prediction sees no person at all (0\% agreement with \texttt{human}; it reads the statue's rigid, single-material, post-mounted shape as sign or background instead), and only 8.4\% of the mask has LiDAR support. Two votes against one are enough to reject the mask, overriding the VLM's mistake; this is the concordance rule doing its job, catching an error that a single judge would have missed. The bottom crop is a simpler case: SAM has merged a real car's roofline with a large blurred artifact into one mask, and here all three agents agree it is bad, so there is no disagreement to resolve.

\textbf{(d) Agent disagreement (human review).} Three separate road signs get merged into one SAM mask. The BBox agent sees an oddly-shaped compound object and calls it \texttt{invalid}, but the Quality Agent recognizes the pixels as genuinely sign-like (\texttt{quality=good}) and disagrees. Rather than trusting either judge alone, the downgrade rule from Section~\ref{sec:methodology} sends the mask to a human instead of deleting real sign pixels. The bottom crop shows the same pattern on a bike rack: several parked bicycles are merged into one \texttt{cyclist} mask, nobody is riding so BBox again says \texttt{invalid}, but Swin scores it \texttt{good} (92\% agreement; ZOD labels parked bikes as cyclists too), so it is again escalated to a human rather than discarded.

\textbf{(e) Discovery.} SAM missed both of these people entirely: a cyclist carrying a child, and a pedestrian standing by a tree, so neither ever had a mask to judge in the first place. Instead, they were found by watching where Swin's own dense, per-pixel prediction disagrees with SAM: the cyan overlay is Swin confidently predicting \texttt{human} pixels that SAM left as background. Once enough of that disagreement clusters together (the orange box), it is cropped out and shown to the VLM, which confirms the two objects as \texttt{cyclist} and \texttt{pedestrian}. In the bottom crop, the recovered mask traces the pedestrian's outline so tightly it could pass for a hand-drawn label, showing how precisely a missed object can be localized before a VLM ever sees it.

\textbf{Limitations observed.} Curating these examples surfaced two systematic weaknesses that the aggregate metrics of Table~\ref{tab:ablation} do not expose, illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:limitations}. First, a \texttt{background} verdict is treated as two negative signals and triggers immediate, irreversible rejection with no human-review fallback, unlike the \texttt{invalid}+\texttt{good} downgrade rule that protects panel (d) of Fig.~\ref{fig:qualitative}. This proved risky in practice: several masks over real, correctly-shaped objects in our candidate pool, including a legible road sign at night (Fig.~\ref{fig:limitations}b) and a clearly visible bus (Fig.~\ref{fig:limitations}a), were rejected purely because the BBox agent misread a dark or motion-blurred crop as empty, even though Swin's independent quality score was high for the sign case (0.605 agreement, above threshold). A single VLM misjudgment on a real object is therefore sufficient to silently discard a valid label, undermining the safety guarantee the concordance rule otherwise provides.
Second, the three-signal design assumes Swin, the VLM, and LiDAR consistency fail independently, but several rejected masks show correlated failure instead: sparse or dot-matrix objects, an LED matrix road sign (Fig.~\ref{fig:limitations}c) and a pedestrian captured only from the waist up at close range (Fig.~\ref{fig:limitations}d), drove both Swin agreement and LiDAR support to near zero simultaneously, even though the BBox agent correctly judged them \texttt{valid}. Because rejection requires only two of three signals, a shared physical cause, low solid-surface fraction within the mask, can defeat the concordance requirement rather than being caught by it, and correctly proposed, safety-relevant sign and human masks are lost as a result. We did not correct these cases in the reported ablations; we flag them here as a concrete direction for future work, for example exempting the \texttt{background} verdict from unilateral rejection, or making the LiDAR support threshold class- or geometry-aware.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This work evaluates a multi-agent refinement pipeline for SAM-based annotation in autonomous driving, combining a domain-adapted Swin Transformer with fixed-prompt visual--LLM agents and deterministic decision rules. Across experiments on ZOD, the Swin Quality Agent, trained on a small clean partition, provides a stable signal for both mask triage and object discovery, enabling refinement with a single forward pass per frame and supporting fully reproducible offline ablations.

Under a single curated evaluation reference with paired bootstrap confidence intervals, Swin-only filtering is the strongest annotation strategy: pseudo-labels from 4{,}135 flagged frames filtered by the Swin agreement signal outperform a curated SAM baseline trained on 2{,}319 clean frames ($\Delta{=}{+}3.3$ mIoU, 95\% CI $[+1.5,+5.5]$), while adding VLM verdicts to the triage rule yields no significant further gain under the fixed-prompt configuration studied here.
Annotation volume combined with a cheap domain-adapted quality signal therefore compensates for initial label noise without any VLM inference.
Swin-driven discovery recovers thousands of objects SAM missed, and its downstream cost tracks confirmed candidate-set volume and precision rather than mask geometry: a large, unselective confirmed set trails Swin-only filtering nearly as much as adding every unconfirmed candidate, but requiring both VLMs to agree before confirming a candidate, the same concordance principle used for triage, recovers annotation quality to parity with the curated baseline.
Because the curated reference is itself SAM-derived and cannot credit genuine recoveries, some of this remaining cost is an artifact of evaluation rather than annotation quality; resolving the two apart requires human-verified evaluation labels, which we identify as the most important next step.

Applying identical prompts to Qwen2.5-VL-72B reveals that prompt-response calibration determines annotation behaviour more than model capability: the same prompt elicits near-unconditional acceptance from Qwen in triage and strong scepticism in discovery, producing systematically different annotation distributions without any change to the pipeline, and these differences propagate to downstream training. This finding has a practical implication: deploying a VLM annotation pipeline with a new model requires behavioural validation of prompt responses, not just a capability benchmark.

Cross-dataset transfer to iSEAuto shows that the Swin + disc.\ checkpoint leads by 2.5 mIoU at epoch 20 (smoothed) and converges to the same final level as scratch by epoch 30, while the raw SAM checkpoint is indistinguishable from ImageNet initialisation throughout: domain exposure alone is insufficient, and the filtered, densely supervised labels encode transferable features even where they do not raise mIoU against the SAM-derived reference. The architecture is backend-agnostic and suitable for local deployment. Future work includes human-verified evaluation labels, evaluation on additional datasets, adaptive discovery thresholds, and extensions to panoptic or temporal segmentation.
