\title{Quantifying Effects of VLM Backend Substitution in Driving-Scene Annotation}

\author{\authorname{Toomas Tahves\sup{1}\orcidAuthor{0009-0008-0050-2146}, Mauro Bellone\sup{2,3}\orcidAuthor{0000-0003-3692-0688} and Raivo Sell\sup{1}\orcidAuthor{0000-0003-1409-0206}} \affiliation{\sup{1}Department of Mechanical and Industrial Engineering, Tallinn University of Technology, Estonia} \affiliation{\sup{2}FinEst Centre for Smart Cities, Tallinn University of Technology, Estonia} \affiliation{\sup{3}Department of Computer Science and Engineering, Universitas Mercatorum, Rome, Italy} \email{\{toomas.tahves, mauro.bellone, raivo.sell\}@taltech.ee} }

\keywords{Multi-Agent Systems, Vision-Language Models, Prompt Engineering, Annotation Pipelines, Semantic Segmentation, Autonomous Driving.}

\abstract{Multi-agent systems built on vision-language models (VLMs) are increasingly used to audit machine-generated annotations, each agent given a role, a fixed prompt, and a constrained output vocabulary.
When such a backend is replaced, that behaviour changes is not in question; what a practitioner needs is a measurement of where it changes and by how much, and on a deployed pipeline over a complex corpus that is rarely measured at all.
We give a protocol for measuring it and apply it to a SAM mask-verification pipeline on the Zenseact Open Dataset: two open-weight backends, LLaVA-1.6-34B and Qwen2.5-VL-72B, over the same 4{,}110 driving frames and 90{,}176 mask proposals under byte-identical prompts, every non-VLM signal held bit-identical, the comparison paired at mask level, the triage rule recomputed offline under one revision for both runs, and each response's provenance recorded so a serving fault cannot be read as model scepticism.
The protocol localises the change instead of summarising it.
The backends agree on only 57.0\,\% of mask verdicts, and neither is uniformly more selective: relative to LLaVA, Qwen rejects far fewer signs (10.8\,\% vs.\ 26.0\,\%) and cyclists, yet more vehicles (16.3\,\% vs.\ 5.1\,\%) and pedestrians, so substitution changes \emph{which} classes lose labels rather than how many.
The effect is agent-specific and can vanish in aggregate: on object discovery the two confirm at almost the same pooled rate, 82.4\,\% against 83.9\,\%, while the whole divergence sits in the one prompt requiring a three-way discrimination rather than a presence check, where confirmation falls from 85.9\,\% to 45.3\,\%, a 41-point deficit on the two safety-critical classes.
Portability must therefore be validated per-agent and per-class on identical inputs, with response provenance reported next to every rate.}

\section{\uppercase{Introduction}}
\label{sec:introduction}

Delegating judgement to a language model has become a standard component of data pipelines.
The LLM-as-judge pattern~\cite{zheng2023judging} assigns a model a narrow evaluative role, constrains its output to a small vocabulary, and treats its verdict as a signal to be fused with others.
Vision language models extend the pattern to image data~\cite{liu2024llava,wang2024qwen2vl}, which makes it attractive for annotation auditing: a mask proposal, a crop, a fixed question, a categorical answer.

Systems built this way inherit an engineering convenience that is rarely examined.
Because each agent is defined by a role and a prompt rather than by weights, the backend appears to be a swappable part: a practitioner who has validated a pipeline on one open model expects to move to a larger or cheaper one by changing a configuration entry, and per-model prompt tuning is in any case infeasible at annotation scale.
That two models behave differently is not in question.
What is missing is a way to measure \emph{how} differently and \emph{where}, on a working pipeline over a corpus complex enough for the answer to matter: substitution is normally assessed by a single acceptance or confirmation rate per model, a statistic that cannot say which agent moved, which classes moved, or whether a rate shifted because the model judged differently or because the server failed to answer.

We build that measurement into a system where the substitution can be isolated: five agents assess Segment Anything Model (SAM)~\cite{kirillov2023sam} mask proposals on driving scenes, of which three are VLM agents with fixed prompts and two --- a segmentation quality agent and a deterministic geometric check --- are computed once and shared.
Only the VLM agents change when the backend changes, so running two open-weight backends over the same 4{,}110 frames produces two annotation sets whose every difference traces to the VLM, and we verify the non-VLM inputs were bit-identical before comparing.

Our contributions are as follows.
We describe an annotation system whose agent roles, output contracts, and default-substitution policy are explicit enough that cross-backend differences can be attributed rather than merely observed, together with the driving corpus it runs over and the partition and split protocol it rests on.
We report a controlled portability study over 90{,}176 mask proposals and 55{,}256 discovery candidates showing that fixed prompts transfer neither uniformly across agents nor uniformly across classes, and that a per-model aggregate rate --- the statistic such comparisons usually report --- would have scored the two backends as near-equivalent on the discovery agent while concealing a 41-point deficit on cyclists and pedestrians.
We separate model-side from server-side non-answering, which we found necessary after a serving fault made a degraded backend look like a sceptical one, and we quantify the deployment cost of the larger model against the annotation distribution it actually produces.

Whether any of these agents \emph{should} be deployed --- each signal scored against human verdicts, with downstream segmentation effect --- is a separate question, and one this comparison cannot settle.
Ours is narrower and prior to it: given that such a system is built, what changes when its backend does?

\section{\uppercase{Related Work}}

\textbf{Language models as delegated judges.} The LLM-as-judge paradigm~\cite{zheng2023judging,wu2023llmjudge} shows that language models can evaluate other systems' outputs given structured prompts and constrained formats, and it has been applied to data annotation directly~\cite{gilardi2023chatgptannotator,li2023llmannotator,li2023llmdata}.
Agent frameworks compose several such roles into pipelines with explicit delegation~\cite{xi2023agents,schick2023toolformer,wang2023voyager}, an organising principle also used for embodied decision-making~\cite{ahn2022saycan,huang2023inner,brohan2023rt2}.
This literature largely evaluates a composed system as a whole, on the assumption that a role specification is portable across the models that might fill it.

\textbf{Prompt sensitivity and model substitution.} That assumption is weakly supported: prompt phrasing and format are known to move model behaviour substantially, and work on instruction form~\cite{wei2022chain} shows a small change in instruction changes what a model does, not merely how well it does it.
What is missing is a controlled substitution study at deployment scale --- two backends, identical prompts, identical non-model inputs, resolved per agent and per class rather than at the level of a pipeline's aggregate output.
Work on foundation-model capability surfaces~\cite{bommasani2021opportunities} anticipates that substitution is not neutral without measuring where the non-neutrality lands in a working system.

\textbf{Annotation auditing over SAM proposals.} SAM~\cite{kirillov2023sam} produces class-agnostic masks without per-class prompts, which makes large-scale pseudo-annotation practical but leaves semantic correctness unverified.
Filtering on confidence or cross-modal geometry~\cite{zou2019confidence,jaritz2020xmuda} cannot detect a plausibly-shaped mask carrying the wrong class, which motivates a semantic judge, and ensemble-style label-noise methods~\cite{malach2017decoupling,han2018co} motivate requiring agreement before acting on a rejection.
Our system combines both; this paper asks what happens to it when the semantic judge is replaced.

\section{\uppercase{Methodology}}
\label{sec:methodology}

This section describes the system under test, the corpus it is run over, and the three controls that make a backend substitution attributable.
Only the VLM agents change between the two runs; every other signal is computed once and shared, which is what allows a verdict difference to be charged to the model rather than to the pipeline around it.

\subsection{Agent Roles and Output Contracts}

Five agents assess each mask (Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}, Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}).
Each returns a categorical value from a closed vocabulary; no agent returns a score, and no learned weights combine them.

\paragraph{Consistency Check (deterministic)} Runs first, before any model call.
The fraction of mask pixels carrying projected LiDAR returns must exceed $\tau{=}0.1$.
Detects leaks into sky and void at zero inference cost, and is identical across backends.

\paragraph{Quality Agent (segmentation)} A Swin Transformer~\cite{liu2021swin} trained on the clean partition emits a per-pixel class map once per frame; per-mask agreement $\alpha$ is read from that shared map with no extra forward pass.
Thresholds are class-specific, $\tau_q^{\text{large}}{=}0.30$ and $\tau_q^{\text{small}}{=}0.15$.
Also identical across backends.

\paragraph{BBox Agent (VLM)} Blind forced-choice classification over a fixed vocabulary of classes and distractors, on a tight RGB crop upscaled to at least $224{\times}224$~px.
Returns \texttt{valid}, \texttt{background}, or \texttt{invalid}.
The agent is not told the expected class, which removes agreement bias, and receives only RGB --- overlays and depth are withheld so it cannot classify context instead of the marked object.
This is the agent issued once per mask, and it dominates cost.

\paragraph{Correction Agent (VLM)} Invoked only on the refine path (\texttt{bbox=valid}, $\alpha{\geq}\tau_q$, \texttt{consistency=fail}).
Returns \texttt{refine} or \texttt{no\_refine}.
It differs from the other VLM agents twice over: its prompt asks for a judgement about a hypothetical action rather than a description of visible content, and it is the only one sent three images instead of one.

\paragraph{Failure-Mode Agent (VLM)} Classifies the dominant error type (\texttt{boundary\_drift}, \texttt{hallucination}, \texttt{occlusion\_miss}, \texttt{fragmentation}).
Diagnostic only; its output never reaches the triage rule.

A separate discovery path compares the Quality Agent's class map to the SAM annotation at $384{\times}384$, forms connected components over pixels where the two disagree, and issues one class-specific VLM prompt per candidate: presence confirmation for vehicles and signs, and a three-way discrimination for human candidates, which must be resolved into cyclist or pedestrian.
That asymmetry between the three discovery prompts is what Section~\ref{sec:results} finds to matter.

\subsection{Triage Rule and the Safe-Default Policy}

Rejection is destructive, so the rule requires concordance.
With $\mathcal{N}=\{\texttt{invalid},\texttt{bad},\texttt{fail}\}$ the negative signals, a mask is rejected on two or more signals in $\mathcal{N}$, with a \texttt{background} verdict counting as two; accepted on three positives; routed to refinement when it is valid and of good quality but fails consistency; and otherwise flagged for human review, so a single dissenting judge escalates a mask rather than deleting it.
An \texttt{invalid} verdict paired with \texttt{quality=good} is downgraded to one negative signal, which protects small objects the VLM misreads.
Only rejection is destructive --- acceptance, refinement and review all write the mask unchanged --- so the two flagged outcomes are reported as one \emph{retained, flagged} category throughout.

One implementation detail matters for every rate reported later.
When a response cannot be parsed, the agent substitutes a \textsc{safe\_default}, which for the BBox agent is \texttt{valid}.
This is the conservative choice for an annotation pipeline --- an unparseable reply must not delete a label --- but it makes a model's apparent permissiveness partly an artifact of its parse failure rate.
Because every substituted verdict is \texttt{valid}, any run's valid rate is a weighted mean of its answered rate and 100\,\%, giving
\begin{equation}
\text{valid}_{\text{answered}} \;\leq\; \text{valid}_{\text{as-recorded}}
\label{eq:default_bound}
\end{equation}
for \emph{any} run, whatever its default share.
We use this bound below to establish a direction without needing the unrecoverable half of the measurement.

\subsection{Corpus}
\label{sec:corpus}

The system runs over the Zenseact Open Dataset (ZOD)~\cite{alibeigi2023zod}.
A prior annotation campaign generated SAM masks for every candidate frame and inspected each one, accepting or discarding the frame as a whole.
That inspection is what defines the two partitions this work uses (Table~\ref{tab:dataset}): a \emph{clean} partition of 2{,}319 frames whose annotations passed, which trains the Quality Agent and is never annotated by the system, and a \emph{flagged} partition of 4{,}135 frames whose annotations did not, which is what the agents are run over.
Of those, 25 could not be annotated cleanly and are excluded, leaving the 4{,}110 frames and 90{,}176 mask proposals reported throughout; the excluded set is not distinguishable from a random draw of the same size by mask count (permutation test, $p{=}0.30$).

The two partitions differ in exactly the way the inspection implies.
Flagged frames carry 21.9 masks each against the clean partition's 15.5, consistent with crowded scenes being harder for SAM and likelier to fail inspection, and the flagged partition is denser in object pixels in every weather condition (Table~\ref{tab:dataset_distributions}).
This matters for reading the results below: the corpus the two backends are compared on is deliberately the difficult half, so verdict rates measured here are not estimates of what either model would return on an average driving frame.

Both partitions are split independently under one procedure.
Frames are grouped into ten strata, weather condition (day-fair, day-rain, night-fair, night-rain, snow) crossed with rare-class presence (cyclist or pedestrian pixels $\geq50$), so each split mirrors the overall weather and class-frequency distribution; 10\,\% of each stratum is held out for validation and the remainder divided between test and training.
Because night-rain and snow are under-represented in ZOD, a floor of 40 frames per weather category is enforced in the test set, which raises the effective test ratio for those conditions only.
Table~\ref{tab:splits} gives the resulting sizes.
The splits are not exercised by this paper, which trains nothing --- both runs annotate the same frames and are compared to each other --- but they are reported here because they define the partition boundaries any downstream use of these annotations must respect.

\subsection{Comparison Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

Two open-weight backends are served via Ollama on a single A100-80GB: LLaVA-1.6-34B~\cite{liu2024llava} and Qwen2.5-VL-72B~\cite{bai2025qwen25vl}.
Prompts are byte-identical across the two, with no per-model calibration, so what is measured is portability of a fixed prompt set rather than either model's ceiling.
Three controls make the comparison attributable.

\textbf{Identical inputs, verified.} All figures are computed on the 4{,}110 frames and 90{,}176 masks both runs completed.
The comparison is paired at mask level, and we verify that the two runs saw the same mask identifiers, bit-identical Swin agreement and LiDAR support values, and the same number of proposed discovery candidates per frame.
Triage outcomes are recomputed offline under one rule for both runs, so a rule revision between campaigns cannot masquerade as a model difference.

\textbf{Verdicts reported as-recorded, with provenance.} BBox rates include masks whose verdict is a \textsc{safe\_default} substitution, because that is the verdict the pipeline acted on and wrote into the annotations.
For Qwen we additionally report the default share and the rate over answered masks.
The equivalent decomposition is not available for LLaVA: that run predates the per-agent parse telemetry and stored only the parsed verdict, so its defaulted masks cannot be distinguished offline from confidently valid ones.
Inequality~\eqref{eq:default_bound} is what lets us state a direction regardless.

\textbf{Response provenance separated from model judgement.} Both runs store each discovery response verbatim, so we re-derive the outcome for both with a single parser and separate four cases that a boolean \texttt{confirmed} flag collapses into one: the model named a class the prompt offered, the model declined (\texttt{other}, a genuine negative), the model replied outside the offered vocabulary, or no content was returned at all.
Confirmation rates are then taken over answered candidates only.
On the instrumented rerun the two causes separate cleanly across the 55{,}256 discovery candidates: LLaVA's non-answers are entirely model-side (1.7\,\% replied outside the offered vocabulary, 0.0\,\% content-free) and Qwen's are nil on both counts, the health monitor described below having cleared the content-free replies that the earlier campaign had pooled into declines.
This separation was not an aesthetic choice.
An earlier campaign reported a strikingly sceptical discovery profile for the larger model which, on inspection, was a degraded server returning empty replies that the parser folded into declines.
Pooling those cases is what made a serving fault look like a behavioural trait, and we now report the provenance decomposition next to every rate.

\textbf{The fault, and what it cost.} On this serving stack the larger backend intermittently stops emitting a stop token and returns content-free replies, at a rate that rises with sustained call volume rather than with elapsed time.
The run reported here is an instrumented rerun guarded by a health monitor that tracks unparseable replies in a sliding window and aborts when it saturates, discarding the in-flight frame rather than writing it dirty, then resuming after a model reload.
Retry and reload traffic from those episodes is included in the deployment cost reported in Section~\ref{sec:results}, which is therefore a deployed rather than a fault-free figure, and the 25 frames excluded from the corpus are the residue the monitor could not clear.
The smaller backend shows no such degradation on the same frames, so this is a property of the deployment and not of the images --- which is precisely why a verdict rate reported without provenance is not safe to read as model behaviour.

\section{\uppercase{Results}}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{What the Agents Actually Decide}

Fig.~\ref{fig:qualitative} shows the five decision paths the architecture produces, with the agent outputs behind each.
\textbf{(a) Hallucination:} SAM proposes a mask where the object is not present and the BBox agent returns \texttt{background} --- a \texttt{vehicle} inside a dense hedge, and a \texttt{sign} on a gantry's bare support lattice with the real panel a few pixels outside the mask.
Both are rejected correctly.
\textbf{(b) Consistency failure:} the mask covers a parked bicycle, BBox returns \texttt{valid} and Swin agrees (\texttt{good}, 82\,\%), but only 9.1\,\% of the mask carries a LiDAR return, below $\tau{=}0.1$: a thin frame reflects few beams, and most pass through and return from the wall behind.
\textbf{(c) Depth-support rejection:} a pedestrian-shaped statue draws \texttt{valid} from the VLM, while Swin sees no person (0\,\% agreement) and LiDAR support is 8.4\,\% --- two negatives against one positive, the concordance rule catching an error a single judge would have missed.
\textbf{(d) Agent disagreement:} three road signs merged into one mask draw \texttt{invalid} from BBox but \texttt{good} from Swin, so the downgrade rule escalates to a human rather than deleting real sign pixels.
\textbf{(e) Discovery:} SAM missed a cyclist and a pedestrian entirely; both are recovered where Swin's dense prediction disagrees with SAM, and the VLM confirms the class of each resulting component.

These are the paths both backends share by construction.
What the rest of this section measures is that running the same prompts through a different backend does not preserve which path a given mask takes.

\subsection{The Two Backends Disagree on Four Crops in Ten}

Table~\ref{tab:agent_behavior} gives the per-agent comparison.
The headline is negative for portability: under identical prompts the two backends return the same BBox verdict on only 57.0\,\% of masks.
Whatever a fixed prompt is transferring, it is not a shared decision boundary.

Qwen is the more permissive mask judge.
It returns \texttt{valid} on 70.9\,\% of crops against LLaVA's 62.1\,\% as-recorded, and since Qwen's answered-only rate of 67.2\,\% already exceeds LLaVA's as-recorded rate, inequality~\eqref{eq:default_bound} places the gap on answered masks at \emph{at least} 5.1 points in that direction.
LLaVA's unmeasured defaults can only widen it, so the direction holds without re-running the model and only the magnitude remains a lower bound.

The more consequential finding is that this is not a uniform relaxation.
Relative to LLaVA, Qwen rejects far fewer signs (10.8\,\% vs.\ 26.0\,\%) and cyclists (33.3\,\% vs.\ 46.5\,\%), yet substantially \emph{more} vehicles (16.3\,\% vs.\ 5.1\,\%) and pedestrians (35.4\,\% vs.\ 16.9\,\%).
The verdict profile is redistributed across classes rather than shifted along a single permissiveness axis, so neither model is uniformly more selective and a single aggregate acceptance rate does not predict which classes a backend will protect.
For a safety-critical annotation task this is the difference that matters: substituting the backend changes \emph{which} classes lose labels, and does so in a direction that the aggregate conceals.

\input{tables/agent_performance}

Table~\ref{tab:agent_performance} scores each backend against the Quality Agent's verdict.
That verdict is not ground truth and the table should not be read as one backend being more correct than the other --- it is a second automated judge, and an agreement statistic between two such judges cannot say which is right.
What it is good for is that it is bit-identical for both runs, so it measures how far each backend's decision boundary sits from one fixed reference.
LLaVA is the balanced judge (precision 72.0, recall 71.1, F1 71.5) and Qwen the higher-recall, lower-precision one (69.0, 77.8, F1 73.1), consistent with its verdict distribution.
Restricting Qwen to answered masks raises precision to 75.6 and accuracy to 67.5, which quantifies how much of its apparent permissiveness was the default substitution rather than the model, and is exactly the correction that becomes impossible when provenance is not stored.

\subsection{Portability Fails Per-Agent, Not Per-Model}

The three discovery prompts separate the finding sharply.
Given the same 55{,}256 candidates, the two backends confirm at nearly the same rate over answered candidates --- 82.4\,\% for Qwen against 83.9\,\% for LLaVA --- and Qwen is in fact marginally \emph{more} permissive on the vehicle prompt (87.6\,\% vs.\ 83.0\,\%) and the sign prompt (89.3\,\% vs.\ 84.2\,\%).
A study reporting one confirmation rate per model would stop here and record the two as near-equivalent on this agent.

The entire divergence sits in the third prompt.
Where a human candidate must be disambiguated into cyclist or pedestrian, Qwen confirms 45.3\,\% of candidates against LLaVA's 85.9\,\%, yielding 3{,}506 human objects against 6{,}376 from an identical candidate stream.
That is a 41-point deficit concentrated on the two classes the pipeline can least afford to lose, produced by the only prompt of the three carrying a three-way discrimination rather than a presence check.
The failure is a property of the \emph{task the prompt encodes}, not of the model's competence in aggregate --- the same backend is more permissive than its counterpart on both of the presence-check prompts.

A third agent fails outright, but in a way this design cannot attribute.
The Correction Agent is reached on 1{,}449 masks under Qwen and returns an unusable reply on every one of them, so all fall back to \texttt{no\_refine}; under LLaVA it is reached on 1{,}311 and answers 1{,}304 with \texttt{refine}.
Two things separate this agent from the two that do transfer --- it is the only one asking for a judgement about a hypothetical action rather than a description of visible content, and the only one sent three images rather than one --- and the two are perfectly confounded here, so we cannot say whether this is a prompt effect or a context-length failure on the larger model's image tokens.
We therefore report it as an unattributed null and exclude it from the portability comparison.
This is also why Table~\ref{tab:agent_behavior} pools the two non-destructive triage outcomes: were the refine path reported separately, a failing agent would enter the cross-model rates as a behavioural difference, which is precisely the error this paper argues against.

Taken together the two agents this design can attribute fail in two different modes under one prompt set: the BBox agent transfers with a class-redistributed boundary, and the discovery agent transfers on two prompts and collapses on the third.
No per-model summary statistic distinguishes these cases, which is our central practical claim: portability must be validated per agent and per class, on the same inputs, or it is not validated.

\subsection{What the Larger Backend Costs, and What It Buys}

Table~\ref{tab:timing} reports deployment cost.
The BBox agent dominates, at 3.54\,s per call for LLaVA against 11.39\,s for Qwen, issued once per mask, so per-mask latency scales roughly with model size.
Annotating the 4{,}110 frames took 132.3 GPU-hours with LLaVA against 426.9 with Qwen, a $3.2\times$ increase, or 33.1 against 106.7 wall-clock hours at four concurrent frame workers.
The Qwen figure is a deployed rather than a fault-free cost: it includes the retry and reload traffic of the monitor-triggered restarts described in Section~\ref{sec:protocol}.
Estimated VRAM is $\sim$22\,GB against $\sim$51\,GB, both within a single A100-80GB.

Against that premium, the larger backend's verdicts are less selective rather than more, its discovery confirmation rate is marginally lower in aggregate while differing by 41 points on one prompt, and a third agent returns nothing usable at all.
The $3.2\times$ cost buys a different annotation distribution, not a better one on any axis measured here.
Whether either backend's triage is worth its cost at all is a question only a human reference can settle; the narrower point available from this comparison alone is that the cost does not purchase portability.

\section{\uppercase{Conclusion}}

We treated the VLM backend of a multi-agent annotation system as the experimental variable, holding prompts and every non-model input fixed across 4{,}110 frames and 90{,}176 mask proposals.
What that measurement shows is not that the two differ but how: the difference is neither uniform across agents nor summarisable by one rate per model, which is what makes it worth localising rather than reporting.
The two backends agree on 57.0\,\% of mask verdicts; one redistributes its rejections across classes rather than shifting selectivity, protecting signs and cyclists while rejecting more vehicles and pedestrians; the discovery agent transfers on both presence-check prompts and collapses by 41 points on the one prompt requiring a three-way discrimination, precisely on the safety-critical classes.
A third agent fails outright on one backend, but its prompt and its image payload both differ from the others, so we report that failure without attributing it.
A single acceptance rate per model, the statistic such comparisons usually report, would have registered the discovery agent as portable.

Two practices follow for anyone building this way.
Validate portability per agent and per class on identical inputs, since a pipeline-level output comparison cannot localise a failure and an aggregate rate can hide a reversal.
And record response provenance alongside every verdict --- whether a value was answered, declined, unparseable, or empty --- because a safe-default policy silently converts serving faults into apparent model behaviour, which in our case produced a plausible and entirely spurious account of a model's character until the raw responses were re-parsed.
The $3.2\times$ inference premium of the larger backend bought a different annotation distribution rather than a better one, and it did not buy portability.
