\begin{abstract}
Multi-agent systems built on vision--language models (VLMs) are increasingly used to audit machine
generated annotations, with each agent given a role, a fixed prompt, and a constrained output
vocabulary. Such a design implicitly assumes the backend is a replaceable component: swap the model,
keep the prompts, and expect the same behaviour at a different cost. We test that assumption
directly. In an annotation system where five agents assess Segment Anything Model proposals over
driving scenes, we run two open-weight backends --- LLaVA-1.6-34B and Qwen2.5-VL-72B --- across
4{,}110 frames and 90{,}176 mask proposals under byte-identical prompts and verified-identical
non-VLM inputs, so any behavioural difference is attributable to the model alone. Portability fails,
and it fails unevenly. The two backends return the same verdict on only 57.0\,\% of crops. Neither
is uniformly more selective: relative to LLaVA, Qwen rejects far fewer signs (10.8\,\% vs.\
26.0\,\%) and cyclists, yet substantially more vehicles (16.3\,\% vs.\ 5.1\,\%) and pedestrians. On
object discovery the two agree in aggregate, 82.4\,\% against 83.9\,\%, while the entire divergence
concentrates in the single prompt requiring a three-way discrimination rather than a presence check,
where confirmation collapses from 85.9\,\% to 45.3\,\% --- a 41-point deficit on the two
safety-critical classes that one aggregate rate per model would have hidden entirely. One agent's
prompt fails to transfer outright, returning no content at all. We argue that portability validation
must be per-agent and per-class, report response provenance next to every rate so a degraded server
cannot be mistaken for a sceptical model, and quantify the $3.2\times$ deployment cost the larger
backend carries for a different annotation distribution rather than a better one.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Delegating judgement to a language model has become a standard component of data pipelines. The
LLM-as-judge pattern~\cite{zheng2023judging} assigns a model a narrow evaluative role, constrains
its output to a small vocabulary, and treats its verdict as a signal to be fused with others. Vision
language models extend the pattern to image data~\cite{liu2024llava,wang2024qwen2vl}, which makes it
attractive for annotation auditing: a mask proposal, a crop, a fixed question, a categorical answer.

Systems built this way inherit an engineering convenience that is rarely examined. Because each
agent is defined by a role and a prompt rather than by weights, the backend appears to be a
swappable part: a practitioner who has validated a pipeline on one open model expects to move to a
larger or cheaper one by changing a configuration entry, and per-model prompt tuning is in any case
infeasible at annotation scale. The assumption behind this convenience is that a fixed prompt
elicits comparable behaviour from comparable models, and that where it does not, the difference
appears as a shift in selectivity that a single acceptance rate would reveal.

We test that assumption in a system where it can be isolated: five agents assess Segment Anything
Model (SAM)~\cite{kirillov2023sam} mask proposals on driving scenes, of which three are VLM agents
with fixed prompts and two --- a segmentation quality agent and a deterministic geometric check ---
are computed once and shared. Only the VLM agents change when the backend changes, so running two
open-weight backends over the same 4{,}110 frames produces two annotation sets whose every
difference traces to the VLM, and we verify the non-VLM inputs were bit-identical before comparing.

Our contributions are as follows. We describe an annotation system whose agent roles, output
contracts, and default-substitution policy are explicit enough that cross-backend differences can be
attributed rather than merely observed. We report a controlled portability study over 90{,}176 mask
proposals and 55{,}256 discovery candidates showing that fixed prompts transfer neither uniformly
across agents nor uniformly across classes, and that a per-model aggregate rate --- the statistic
such comparisons usually report --- would have scored the two backends as near-equivalent on the
discovery agent while concealing a 41-point deficit on cyclists and pedestrians. We separate
model-side from server-side non-answering, which we found necessary after a serving fault made a
degraded backend look like a sceptical one, and we quantify the deployment cost of the larger model
against the annotation distribution it actually produces.

Whether any of these agents \emph{should} be deployed --- each signal scored against human verdicts,
with downstream segmentation effect --- is a separate study~\cite{audit}; prompts and the complete
result set are in an accompanying technical report~\cite{techreport}. Here the question is narrower
and prior to both: given that such a system is built, what changes when its backend does?

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Language models as delegated judges.} The LLM-as-judge
paradigm~\cite{zheng2023judging,wu2023llmjudge} shows that language models can evaluate other
systems' outputs given structured prompts and constrained formats, and it has been applied to data
annotation directly~\cite{gilardi2023chatgptannotator,li2023llmannotator,li2023llmdata}. Agent
frameworks compose several such roles into pipelines with explicit
delegation~\cite{xi2023agents,schick2023toolformer,wang2023voyager}, an organising principle also
used for embodied decision-making~\cite{ahn2022saycan,huang2023inner,brohan2023rt2}. This literature
largely evaluates a composed system as a whole, on the assumption that a role specification is
portable across the models that might fill it.

\textbf{Prompt sensitivity and model substitution.} That assumption is weakly supported: prompt
phrasing and format are known to move model behaviour substantially, and work on
instruction form~\cite{wei2022chain} shows a small change in instruction changes what a model does,
not merely how well it does it. What is missing is a controlled substitution study at deployment
scale --- two backends, identical prompts, identical non-model inputs, resolved per agent and per
class rather than at the level of a pipeline's aggregate output. Work on foundation-model capability
surfaces~\cite{bommasani2021opportunities} anticipates that substitution is not neutral without
measuring where the non-neutrality lands in a working system.

\textbf{Annotation auditing over SAM proposals.} SAM~\cite{kirillov2023sam} produces class-agnostic
masks without per-class prompts, which makes large-scale pseudo-annotation practical but leaves
semantic correctness unverified. Filtering on confidence or cross-modal
geometry~\cite{zou2019confidence,jaritz2020xmuda} cannot detect a plausibly-shaped mask carrying the
wrong class, which motivates a semantic judge, and ensemble-style label-noise
methods~\cite{malach2017decoupling,han2018co} motivate requiring agreement before acting on a
rejection. Our system combines both; this paper asks what happens to it when the semantic judge is
replaced.

\section{The Multi-Agent Annotation System}
\label{sec:system}

\subsection{Agent Roles and Output Contracts}

Five agents assess each mask (Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}, Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}). Each returns a
categorical value from a closed vocabulary; no agent returns a score, and no learned weights combine
them.

\paragraph{Consistency Check (deterministic)} Runs first, before any model call. The fraction of
mask pixels carrying projected LiDAR returns must exceed $\tau{=}0.1$. Detects leaks into sky and
void at zero inference cost, and is identical across backends.

\paragraph{Quality Agent (segmentation)} A Swin Transformer~\cite{liu2021swin} trained on the clean
partition emits a per-pixel class map once per frame; per-mask agreement $\alpha$ is read from that
shared map with no extra forward pass. Thresholds are class-specific,
$\tau_q^{\text{large}}{=}0.30$ and $\tau_q^{\text{small}}{=}0.15$. Also identical across backends.

\paragraph{BBox Agent (VLM)} Blind forced-choice classification over a fixed vocabulary of classes
and distractors, on a tight RGB crop upscaled to at least $224{\times}224$~px. Returns
\texttt{valid}, \texttt{background}, or \texttt{invalid}. The agent is not told the expected class,
which removes agreement bias, and receives only RGB --- overlays and depth are withheld so it cannot
classify context instead of the marked object. This is the agent issued once per mask, and it
dominates cost.

\paragraph{Correction Agent (VLM)} Invoked only on the refine path
(\texttt{bbox=valid}, $\alpha{\geq}\tau_q$, \texttt{consistency=fail}). Returns \texttt{refine} or
\texttt{no\_refine}. Unlike the others its prompt asks for a judgement about a hypothetical
corrective action rather than a description of visible content.

\paragraph{Failure-Mode Agent (VLM)} Classifies the dominant error type (\texttt{boundary\_drift},
\texttt{hallucination}, \texttt{occlusion\_miss}, \texttt{fragmentation}). Diagnostic only; its
output never reaches the triage rule.

A separate discovery path compares the Quality Agent's class map to the SAM annotation at
$384{\times}384$, forms connected components over pixels where the two disagree, and issues one
class-specific VLM prompt per candidate: presence confirmation for vehicles and signs, and a
three-way discrimination for human candidates, which must be resolved into cyclist or pedestrian.
That asymmetry between the three discovery prompts is what Section~\ref{sec:results} finds to matter.

\subsection{Triage Rule and the Safe-Default Policy}

Rejection is destructive, so the rule requires concordance. With
$\mathcal{N}=\{\texttt{invalid},\texttt{bad},\texttt{fail}\}$ the negative signals, a mask is
rejected on two or more signals in $\mathcal{N}$, with a \texttt{background} verdict counting as
two; accepted on three positives; routed to refinement when it is valid and of good quality but
fails consistency; and otherwise flagged for human review, so a single dissenting judge escalates a
mask rather than deleting it. An \texttt{invalid} verdict paired with \texttt{quality=good} is
downgraded to one negative signal, which protects small objects the VLM misreads.

One implementation detail matters for every rate reported later. When a response cannot be parsed,
the agent substitutes a \textsc{safe\_default}, which for the BBox agent is \texttt{valid}. This is
the conservative choice for an annotation pipeline --- an unparseable reply must not delete a label
--- but it makes a model's apparent permissiveness partly an artifact of its parse failure rate.
Because every substituted verdict is \texttt{valid}, any run's valid rate is a weighted mean of its
answered rate and 100\,\%, giving
\begin{equation}
\text{valid}_{\text{answered}} \;\leq\; \text{valid}_{\text{as-recorded}}
\label{eq:default_bound}
\end{equation}
for \emph{any} run, whatever its default share. We use this bound below to establish a direction
without needing the unrecoverable half of the measurement.

\section{Comparison Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

Two open-weight backends are served via Ollama on a single A100-80GB: LLaVA-1.6-34B~\cite{liu2024llava}
and Qwen2.5-VL-72B~\cite{bai2025qwen25vl}. Prompts are byte-identical across the two, with no
per-model calibration, so what is measured is portability of a fixed prompt set rather than either
model's ceiling. Three controls make the comparison attributable.

\textbf{Identical inputs, verified.} All figures are computed on the 4{,}110 frames and 90{,}176
masks both runs completed. The comparison is paired at mask level, and we verify that the two runs
saw the same mask identifiers, bit-identical Swin agreement and LiDAR support values, and the same
number of proposed discovery candidates per frame. Triage outcomes are recomputed offline under one
rule for both runs, so a rule revision between campaigns cannot masquerade as a model difference.

\textbf{Verdicts reported as-recorded, with provenance.} BBox rates include masks whose verdict is a
\textsc{safe\_default} substitution, because that is the verdict the pipeline acted on and wrote into
the annotations. For Qwen we additionally report the default share and the rate over answered masks.
The equivalent decomposition is not available for LLaVA: that run predates the per-agent parse
telemetry and stored only the parsed verdict, so its defaulted masks cannot be distinguished offline
from confidently valid ones. Inequality~\eqref{eq:default_bound} is what lets us state a direction
regardless.

\textbf{Response provenance separated from model judgement.} Both runs store each discovery response
verbatim, so we re-derive the outcome for both with a single parser and separate four cases that a
boolean \texttt{confirmed} flag collapses into one: the model named a class the prompt offered, the
model declined (\texttt{other}, a genuine negative), the model replied outside the offered
vocabulary, or no content was returned at all. Confirmation rates are then taken over answered
candidates only. This separation was not an aesthetic choice. An earlier campaign reported a
strikingly sceptical discovery profile for the larger model which, on inspection, was a degraded
server returning empty replies that the parser folded into declines. Pooling those cases is what made
a serving fault look like a behavioural trait, and we now report the provenance decomposition next to
every rate.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{What the Agents Actually Decide}

Fig.~\ref{fig:qualitative} shows the five decision paths the architecture produces, with the agent
outputs behind each. \textbf{(a) Hallucination:} SAM proposes a mask where the object is not
present and the BBox agent returns \texttt{background} --- a \texttt{vehicle} inside a dense hedge,
and a \texttt{sign} on a gantry's bare support lattice with the real panel a few pixels outside the
mask. Both are rejected correctly. \textbf{(b) Consistency failure:} the mask covers a parked
bicycle, BBox returns \texttt{valid} and Swin agrees (\texttt{good}, 82\,\%), but only 9.1\,\% of
the mask carries a LiDAR return, below $\tau{=}0.1$: a thin frame reflects few beams, and most pass
through and return from the wall behind. \textbf{(c) Depth-support rejection:} a pedestrian-shaped
statue draws \texttt{valid} from the VLM, while Swin sees no person (0\,\% agreement) and LiDAR
support is 8.4\,\% --- two negatives against one positive, the concordance rule catching an error a
single judge would have missed. \textbf{(d) Agent disagreement:} three road signs merged into one
mask draw \texttt{invalid} from BBox but \texttt{good} from Swin, so the downgrade rule escalates to
a human rather than deleting real sign pixels. \textbf{(e) Discovery:} SAM missed a cyclist and a
pedestrian entirely; both are recovered where Swin's dense prediction disagrees with SAM, and the
VLM confirms the class of each resulting component.

These are the paths both backends share by construction. What the rest of this section measures is
that running the same prompts through a different backend does not preserve which path a given mask
takes.

\subsection{The Two Backends Disagree on Four Crops in Ten}

Table~\ref{tab:agent_behavior} gives the per-agent comparison. The headline is negative for
portability: under identical prompts the two backends return the same BBox verdict on only 57.0\,\%
of masks. Whatever a fixed prompt is transferring, it is not a shared decision boundary.

Qwen is the more permissive mask judge. It returns \texttt{valid} on 70.9\,\% of crops against
LLaVA's 62.1\,\% as-recorded, and since Qwen's answered-only rate of 67.2\,\% already exceeds
LLaVA's as-recorded rate, inequality~\eqref{eq:default_bound} places the gap on answered masks at
\emph{at least} 5.1 points in that direction. LLaVA's unmeasured defaults can only widen it, so the
direction holds without re-running the model and only the magnitude remains a lower bound.

The more consequential finding is that this is not a uniform relaxation. Relative to LLaVA, Qwen
rejects far fewer signs (10.8\,\% vs.\ 26.0\,\%) and cyclists (33.3\,\% vs.\ 46.5\,\%), yet
substantially \emph{more} vehicles (16.3\,\% vs.\ 5.1\,\%) and pedestrians (35.4\,\% vs.\ 16.9\,\%).
The verdict profile is redistributed across classes rather than shifted along a single
permissiveness axis, so neither model is uniformly more selective and a single aggregate acceptance
rate does not predict which classes a backend will protect. For a safety-critical annotation task
this is the difference that matters: substituting the backend changes \emph{which} classes lose
labels, and does so in a direction that the aggregate conceals.

\input{tables/agent_performance}

Table~\ref{tab:agent_performance} scores each backend against the Quality Agent's verdict, which is
a noisy reference rather than ground truth but is identical for both runs and therefore ranks them
on one fixed yardstick. LLaVA is the balanced judge (precision 72.0, recall 71.1, F1 71.5) and Qwen
the higher-recall, lower-precision one (69.0, 77.8, F1 73.1), consistent with its verdict
distribution. Restricting Qwen to answered masks raises precision to 75.6 and accuracy to 67.5,
which quantifies how much of its apparent permissiveness was the default substitution rather than
the model, and is exactly the correction that becomes impossible when provenance is not stored.

\subsection{Portability Fails Per-Agent, Not Per-Model}

The three discovery prompts separate the finding sharply. Given the same 55{,}256 candidates, the
two backends confirm at nearly the same rate over answered candidates --- 82.4\,\% for Qwen against
83.9\,\% for LLaVA --- and Qwen is in fact marginally \emph{more} permissive on the vehicle prompt
(87.6\,\% vs.\ 83.0\,\%) and the sign prompt (89.3\,\% vs.\ 84.2\,\%). A study reporting one
confirmation rate per model would stop here and record the two as near-equivalent on this agent.

The entire divergence sits in the third prompt. Where a human candidate must be disambiguated into
cyclist or pedestrian, Qwen confirms 45.3\,\% of candidates against LLaVA's 85.9\,\%, yielding
3{,}506 human objects against 6{,}376 from an identical candidate stream. That is a 41-point deficit
concentrated on the two classes the pipeline can least afford to lose, produced by the only prompt of
the three carrying a three-way discrimination rather than a presence check. The failure is a property
of the \emph{task the prompt encodes}, not of the model's competence in aggregate --- the same
backend is more permissive than its counterpart on both of the presence-check prompts.

The Correction Agent fails more starkly still, and in the third possible way. Its prompt is the only
one asking for a judgement about a hypothetical corrective action rather than a description of
visible content, and under Qwen it elicits no usable content at all: every call falls back to
\texttt{no\_refine} and the refine path never fires, against 1.4\,\% of masks routed to refinement
under LLaVA (Table~\ref{tab:agent_behavior}). We report this as a null result and make no refine-path
claim for Qwen. The explanation we can offer --- that a counterfactual instruction transfers worse
than a descriptive one --- is plausible and untested.

Taken together the three agents fail in three different modes under one prompt set: the BBox agent
transfers with a class-redistributed boundary, the discovery agent transfers on two prompts and
collapses on the third, and the Correction agent does not transfer at all. No per-model summary
statistic distinguishes these cases, which is our central practical claim: portability must be
validated per agent and per class, on the same inputs, or it is not validated.

\subsection{What the Larger Backend Costs, and What It Buys}

Table~\ref{tab:timing} reports deployment cost. The BBox agent dominates, at 3.54\,s per call for
LLaVA against 11.39\,s for Qwen, issued once per mask, so per-mask latency scales roughly with model
size. Annotating the 4{,}110 frames took 132.3 GPU-hours with LLaVA against 426.9 with Qwen, a
$3.2\times$ increase, or 33.1 against 106.7 wall-clock hours at four concurrent frame workers. The
Qwen figure is a deployed rather than a fault-free cost: it includes retry and reload traffic from
the monitor-triggered restarts described in Section~\ref{sec:protocol}. Estimated VRAM is
$\sim$22\,GB against $\sim$51\,GB, both within a single A100-80GB.

Against that premium, the larger backend's verdicts are less selective rather than more, its
discovery confirmation rate is marginally lower in aggregate while differing by 41 points on one
prompt, and one of its three agent prompts does not function. The $3.2\times$ cost buys a different
annotation distribution, not a better one on any axis measured here. Whether either backend's triage
is worth its cost against a human reference is the question of~\cite{audit}; the narrower point
available from this comparison alone is that the cost does not purchase portability.

\subsection{Two Weaknesses of the Rule Itself}

Inspecting individual triage paths exposed two failure modes that belong to the rule rather than to
either backend, both visible in Fig.~\ref{fig:limitations}. First, a \texttt{background} verdict
counts as two negatives and therefore rejects unilaterally, with no escalation path, so a single VLM
misreading of a dark or motion-blurred crop is sufficient to discard a valid label --- we observe
this on a legible night road sign whose independent Swin score was well above threshold
(Fig.~\ref{fig:limitations}b, 0.605 agreement), and on a bus large enough to fill much of the crop
(Fig.~\ref{fig:limitations}a). Second, the rule assumes the three signals fail independently, and
for sparse or dot-matrix objects they do not: an LED matrix sign
(Fig.~\ref{fig:limitations}c) and a near-field pedestrian (Fig.~\ref{fig:limitations}d) each drive
Swin agreement and LiDAR support to near zero at once through one shared physical cause, a low
solid-surface fraction, defeating a two-of-three concordance requirement rather than being caught by
it --- and in both cases the BBox agent had judged the object \texttt{valid}. Both are correctable
--- exempting \texttt{background} from unilateral rejection when Swin agreement is high, and making
the LiDAR threshold class-aware --- and~\cite{audit} evaluates the repair against human verdicts.

\section{Conclusion}

We treated the VLM backend of a multi-agent annotation system as the experimental variable, holding
prompts and every non-model input fixed across 4{,}110 frames and 90{,}176 mask proposals. Fixed
prompts do not transfer between open backends, and the failure is neither uniform nor summarisable
per model. The two backends agree on 57.0\,\% of mask verdicts; one redistributes its rejections
across classes rather than shifting selectivity, protecting signs and cyclists while rejecting more
vehicles and pedestrians; the discovery agent transfers on both presence-check prompts and collapses
by 41 points on the one prompt requiring a three-way discrimination, precisely on the safety-critical
classes; and the Correction Agent's counterfactual prompt does not transfer at all. A single
acceptance rate per model, the statistic such comparisons usually report, would have registered the
discovery agent as portable.

Two practices follow for anyone building this way. Validate portability per agent and per class on
identical inputs, since a pipeline-level output comparison cannot localise a failure and an aggregate
rate can hide a reversal. And record response provenance alongside every verdict --- whether a value
was answered, declined, unparseable, or empty --- because a safe-default policy silently converts
serving faults into apparent model behaviour, which in our case produced a plausible and entirely
spurious account of a model's character until the raw responses were re-parsed. The $3.2\times$
inference premium of the larger backend bought a different annotation distribution rather than a
better one, and it did not buy portability.
