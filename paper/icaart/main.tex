\title{VLM Substitution in Autonomous Driving Data: Per-Class Behavioral Shifts in Scene Annotation}

\author{\authorname{Toomas Tahves\sup{1}\orcidAuthor{0009-0008-0050-2146}, Mauro Bellone\sup{2,3}\orcidAuthor{0000-0003-3692-0688} and Raivo Sell\sup{1}\orcidAuthor{0000-0003-1409-0206}} \affiliation{\sup{1}Department of Mechanical and Industrial Engineering, Tallinn University of Technology, Estonia} \affiliation{\sup{2}FinEst Centre for Smart Cities, Tallinn University of Technology, Estonia} \affiliation{\sup{3}Department of Computer Science and Engineering, Universitas Mercatorum, Rome, Italy} \email{\{toomas.tahves, mauro.bellone, raivo.sell\}@taltech.ee} }

\keywords{Multi-Agent Systems, Vision-Language Models, Prompt Engineering, Annotation Pipelines, Semantic Segmentation, Autonomous Driving.}

\abstract{
Vision–language models (VLMs) are now used as multi‑agent judges in large annotation pipelines, where each agent follows a fixed role, a fixed prompt, and a small output vocabulary.
Practitioners often swap VLM backends, but there is no reliable way to measure where the behaviour changes inside a deployed system.
We present a simple and controlled method that measures these changes per agent and per class, and we apply it to a SAM‑based mask‑verification pipeline on the Zenseact Open Dataset.
Two open‑weight VLMs, LLaVA‑1.6‑34B and Qwen2.5‑VL‑72B, are run over the same 4{,}110 driving frames and 90{,}176 mask proposals with identical prompts and identical non‑VLM signals.
All comparisons are paired at mask level, the triage rule is recomputed once for both runs, and every response is stored so serving faults can be separated from model judgement.
The two backends agree on only 57.0\% of BBox verdicts, and the differences are not uniform.
Compared to LLaVA, Qwen rejects far fewer signs and cyclists, but rejects more vehicles and pedestrians, which changes which classes lose labels rather than the overall rejection rate.
In the final annotations, 18.8\% of masks, or 7.5 million pixels, are kept by one backend and removed by the other.
The largest gap appears in the discovery agent for human classes, where the confirmation rate drops from 85.9\% to 45.3\% when the backend is changed.
This shows that prompt portability cannot be assumed, even between strong open‑weight VLMs.
A deployed system must validate each agent and each class on identical inputs, and it must report response provenance next to every rate.
}

\section{\uppercase{Introduction}}
\label{sec:introduction}

Delegating judgement to a language model has become a standard part of modern data pipelines.
The LLM-as-judge pattern~\cite{zheng2023judging} gives a model a narrow evaluative role, limits its output to a small vocabulary, and treats its verdict as one signal among several.
Vision–language models extend this idea to image data~\cite{liu2024llava,wang2024qwen2vl}, which makes them appealing for annotation auditing: a mask proposal, a crop, a fixed question, and a categorical answer.

Pipelines built this way inherit an engineering assumption that is rarely checked.
Because each agent is defined by a role and a prompt rather than by learned weights, the backend looks like a swappable component.
A practitioner who has validated a pipeline on one open model often expects to switch to a larger or cheaper one by changing a configuration entry.
Prompt tuning per model is not realistic at annotation scale.
That different models behave differently is obvious.
What is missing is a way to measure how differently and where, inside a working pipeline and on a corpus large enough for the answer to matter.
Substitution is usually reported as a single acceptance or confirmation rate per model, which cannot show which agent changed, which classes moved, or whether a shift came from the model or from a serving failure.

We build that measurement into a system where substitution can be isolated cleanly.
Four agents assess Segment Anything Model (SAM)~\cite{kirillov2023sam} mask proposals on driving scenes, and a fifth recovers objects the proposals missed.
Three of them use VLMs with fixed prompts.
The other two, a segmentation quality model and a deterministic geometric check, are computed once and shared.
Only the VLM agents change when the backend changes.
Running two open-weight backends over the same 4{,}110 frames produces two annotation sets whose differences can be traced directly to the VLM.
We verify that all non-VLM inputs are bit-identical before comparing the outputs.

Our contributions are as follows.
We describe an annotation system where the agent roles, output formats, and fallback behaviour are defined precisely enough that differences between backends can be traced to the model rather than guessed.
We present a controlled portability study over 90{,}176 mask proposals and 55{,}256 discovery candidates, showing that fixed prompts do not transfer uniformly across agents or classes.
We show that the overall acceptance rate makes the two backends look similar on the discovery agent, even though one of them confirms cyclists and pedestrians 41 percentage points less often.
We separate model-side and server-side non-answering, because serving faults can produce empty replies that would otherwise be mistaken for model behaviour.
We also quantify the deployment cost of the larger model against the annotation distribution it actually produces.

Whether any of these agents should be deployed is a separate question.
That requires scoring each signal against human judgement and measuring downstream segmentation effects.
Our focus is narrower and comes before that: once such a system exists, what changes when its backend does.

\section{\uppercase{Related Work}}

Language models have increasingly been used as delegated judges in data pipelines.  
The LLM-as-judge pattern~\cite{zheng2023judging,wu2023llmjudge} shows that a model can evaluate another system’s output when given a structured prompt and a constrained response format, and this idea has already been applied to data annotation~\cite{gilardi2023chatgptannotator,li2023llmannotator,li2023llmdata}.  
Agent frameworks extend this approach by combining several such roles into pipelines with explicit delegation~\cite{xi2023agents,schick2023toolformer,wang2023voyager}, an idea that also appears in embodied decision-making~\cite{ahn2022saycan,huang2023inner,brohan2023rt2}.  
Most of this work evaluates the composed system as a whole and assumes that a role specification can be transferred across different models without changing behaviour.

That assumption is weakly supported.  
Prompt phrasing and format are known to shift model behaviour, and work on instruction form~\cite{wei2022chain} shows that even small changes in wording can alter what a model does.  
What is missing is a controlled substitution study at deployment scale, where two backends are run with identical prompts and identical non-model inputs, and the comparison is resolved per agent and per class rather than through a single aggregate rate.  
Research on foundation-model capability surfaces~\cite{bommasani2021opportunities} suggests that substitution is not neutral, but does not measure where these differences appear inside a working system.

Annotation auditing over SAM proposals introduces its own challenges.  
SAM~\cite{kirillov2023sam} produces class-agnostic masks without per-class prompts, which makes large-scale pseudo-annotation feasible but leaves semantic correctness unchecked.  
Filtering on confidence or cross-modal geometry~\cite{zou2019confidence,jaritz2020xmuda} cannot detect a plausible mask that carries the wrong class, which motivates the use of a semantic judge.  
Ensemble-style label-noise methods~\cite{malach2017decoupling,han2018co} further motivate requiring agreement before acting on a rejection.  
Our system combines these ideas, and this paper examines what happens when the semantic judge itself is replaced.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

This section describes the system we evaluate, the dataset it runs on, and the controls that make it possible to attribute differences directly to the VLM backend.
Only the VLM agents change between the two runs; every other signal is computed once and reused, which ensures that any change in verdicts comes from the model rather than from the surrounding pipeline.

\subsection{Agent roles and output contracts}

Four agents act on a mask (Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}): three see every mask, and the fourth is reached only on the refine path.
Each agent returns a categorical value from a fixed vocabulary. None of them produce scores, and no learned weights combine their outputs.

\paragraph{Consistency check (deterministic)}  
This check runs first. It measures the fraction of mask pixels that contain projected LiDAR returns, which must exceed $\tau = 0.1$. It catches masks that leak into sky or void regions at essentially zero cost. The result is identical across backends.

\paragraph{Quality agent (segmentation)}  
This agent asks whether the pixels inside a mask actually belong to the class the mask claims, which is the question a crop-level judgement cannot answer.
A Swin Transformer \cite{liu2021swin} taking camera and LiDAR input, trained on the clean partition, predicts a dense class map once per frame at $384 \times 384$; every mask in that frame is then scored against the same map, so per-mask scoring costs no additional forward pass.
The agreement $\alpha$ is the fraction of a mask's pixels that the class map assigns to the mask's own class. It falls when a mask bleeds off the object into road or background, and approaches zero when the mask covers a different object altogether.
The agent returns \texttt{good} when $\alpha \geq \tau_q$ and \texttt{bad} otherwise.
Thresholds are class-specific, $\tau_q^{\text{large}} = 0.30$ for vehicles and signs and $\tau_q^{\text{small}} = 0.15$ for cyclists and pedestrians, because the segmentation model is markedly weaker on small objects and a single threshold would mark correctly drawn pedestrian masks as \texttt{bad}.
The class map does not separate cyclists from pedestrians, which share one human class, so for those masks $\alpha$ measures membership in the merged class and the distinction is left to the VLM.
Being a fixed model with fixed thresholds, this agent is identical across backends.

\paragraph{BBox agent (VLM)}  
This is the agent whose backend we substitute, and the only one that judges what the masked object \emph{is} rather than how well the mask fits it.
It is deliberately independent of the segmentation model, so that a mask can only be rejected when a learned segmenter and a language model fail on it in the same place.
It receives a tight RGB crop upscaled to at least $224 \times 224$ pixels, with no full frame, no overlay, and no depth. Context is withheld because it lets the model label the scene instead of the marked region, so that a crop taken on a motorway reads as \texttt{vehicle} whatever the mask actually covers.
The prompt asks which class the object belongs to and offers a closed vocabulary of twelve words: the four annotation classes plus \texttt{traffic\_light}; four concrete background surfaces (\texttt{vegetation}, \texttt{building}, \texttt{sky}, \texttt{snow}); and three weak options (\texttt{road}, \texttt{other}, \texttt{unclear}).
The expected class is never named. A presence question invites agreement and accepts noise blobs, whereas a forced choice makes the model commit to an identity that can disagree with the annotation.
The answer maps to one of three verdicts. Naming the annotated class returns \texttt{valid}, with \texttt{traffic\_light} accepted for masks labelled sign because ZOD groups them.
Naming one of the four background surfaces returns \texttt{background}: committing to a concrete surface is positive evidence that the mask covers no object at all, which is why this verdict alone is strong enough to reject.
Every other answer, whether a different object class or a weak option, returns \texttt{invalid}, a single negative that needs corroboration; \texttt{road} is the dominant surface in every crop and the most common wrong answer on small objects, so it must not reject on its own.
This agent is invoked once per mask and dominates inference cost.

\paragraph{Correction agent (VLM)}  
This agent exists for the one combination the other signals cannot resolve between them: the crop shows the right object and its pixels agree with the class map, yet too few of those pixels carry LiDAR returns.
It is reached only on that path, where \texttt{bbox=valid}, $\alpha \geq \tau_q$, and \texttt{consistency=fail}, so it arbitrates between two signals that disagree rather than judging the mask from scratch.
It is shown three images of the same region --- the RGB crop, the crop with the mask highlighted, and the projected LiDAR depth --- together with a metadata block giving the class, pixel count, bounding box, aspect ratio, and LiDAR support ratio.
This is the largest payload in the pipeline, and unlike the BBox agent it is told which class to expect, because the question is no longer what the object is.
The question is whether a simple geometric adjustment, such as tightening the boundary or removing a leaked region, would clearly improve the mask; the agent answers \texttt{refine} or \texttt{no\_refine}.
Two properties separate this prompt from the others. It asks for a judgement about a hypothetical action rather than about visible content, and the action is one the pipeline never carries out: a \texttt{refine} verdict routes the mask to refinement, which leaves the pixels unchanged and marks the mask for later attention.
Its safe default is \texttt{no\_refine}, so an unusable reply leaves the mask exactly as it stands. Section~\ref{sec:results} shows that this is the agent that fails outright on one backend.

A separate discovery path compares the Quality Agent’s class map to the SAM annotation at $384 \times 384$ resolution. It extracts connected components where the two disagree and issues one class‑specific VLM prompt per candidate region. Vehicles and signs use a presence‑confirmation prompt, while human candidates use a three‑way discrimination that must resolve into cyclist or pedestrian. This asymmetry between the three discovery prompts becomes important in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\subsection{Triage rule, outcomes, and safe defaults}
\label{sec:triage}

No agent decides the fate of a mask. Three signals do, through a fixed function of the BBox verdict, the Quality agent's \texttt{good} or \texttt{bad}, and the deterministic consistency check. It produces three outcomes, and only the first changes any pixels.

\paragraph{Reject} Rejection is destructive, so it requires agreement.
Let $\mathcal{N} = \{\texttt{invalid}, \texttt{bad}, \texttt{fail}\}$ be the negative verdicts; a mask is rejected once its negatives reach two. Each verdict in $\mathcal{N}$ counts one, with two exceptions. A \texttt{background} verdict counts two on its own, because naming a concrete surface is positive evidence of error rather than absence of confirmation. An \texttt{invalid} verdict counts two when consistency passes and the Quality agent does not return \texttt{good}, since neither remaining signal then vouches for the mask; against \texttt{quality=good} it stays a single negative, which protects small objects the VLM may misread. A mask is also rejected before any agent sees it if its geometry is degenerate: aspect ratio outside $[0.12, 4.5]$, or fewer pixels than the class minimum (30 for vehicles, 15 otherwise). This pre-filter is deterministic and removes the same masks in both runs.

\paragraph{Accept} All three signals are positive: \texttt{valid}, \texttt{good}, and \texttt{pass}. The mask is written unchanged.

\paragraph{Retained, flagged} Everything else, which in practice means a single dissenting signal. The mask is written unchanged and marked for attention, so one disagreeing judge escalates a mask instead of deleting it.

The rule separates two retained cases internally, and we pool them: a mask that is \texttt{valid} and \texttt{good} but fails consistency goes to the Correction agent, where \texttt{refine} marks it for refinement and anything else for human review. Pooling is lossless for the annotations, since only rejection zeroes pixels and both cases write byte-identical labels, and it is necessary for a cross-model comparison, because the agent that separates them answers on one backend and fails on the other (Section~\ref{sec:results}); the split would measure that failure rather than the models.

One implementation detail affects all reported rates. When a response cannot be parsed, the agent substitutes a \texttt{safe\_default}. For the BBox agent, this default is \texttt{valid}. This prevents an unparseable reply from deleting a label, but it also inflates the apparent permissiveness of a model. Since every substituted verdict is \texttt{valid}, the recorded valid rate is a weighted average of the answered rate and 100 percent:



\begin{equation}
\label{eq:default_bound}
\text{valid}_{\text{answered}} \leq \text{valid}_{\text{as-recorded}}
\end{equation}



This inequality holds for any run, regardless of its default share. We use it later to establish the direction of differences even when one run cannot be decomposed into answered and defaulted cases.

\subsection{Dataset}
\label{sec:corpus}

The system runs on the Zenseact Open Dataset (ZOD) \cite{alibeigi2023zod}.  
A previous annotation work produced SAM masks for all candidate frames and inspected each frame as a whole. This inspection defines two partitions (Table~\ref{tab:dataset}): a clean partition of 2,319 frames whose annotations passed, and a flagged partition of 4,135 frames whose annotations did not. The clean partition trains the Quality Agent and is not re‑annotated. Twenty‑five flagged frames could not be annotated cleanly and are excluded, leaving 4,110 frames and 90,176 mask proposals. The excluded frames are statistically indistinguishable from a random draw of the same size (permutation test, $p = 0.30$).

The flagged partition contains denser scenes. It has 21.9 masks per frame compared to 15.5 in the clean partition, which matches the intuition that crowded scenes are harder for SAM and more likely to fail inspection. Object‑pixel density is higher in every weather condition (Table~\ref{tab:dataset_distributions}). This matters for interpreting results: the comparison is intentionally run on the difficult half of the dataset, so the verdict rates here should not be taken as estimates for typical driving scenes.

Both partitions are split using the same procedure. Frames are grouped into ten strata defined by weather condition (day‑fair, day‑rain, night‑fair, night‑rain, snow) crossed with rare‑class presence (cyclist or pedestrian pixels $\geq 50$). Each split mirrors the overall distribution of weather and rare classes. Ten percent of each stratum is held out for validation, and the rest is divided into training and test sets. Because night‑rain and snow are under‑represented, a minimum of 40 frames per weather category is enforced in the test set, which increases the effective test ratio for those conditions. Table~\ref{tab:splits} shows the resulting sizes.

These splits are not used directly in this paper, since both backends annotate the same frames and are compared to each other. They are reported because they define the boundaries that any downstream use of these annotations must respect.

\subsection{Comparison}
\label{sec:protocol}

We compare two open‑weight VLM backends served through Ollama on a single A100‑80GB: LLaVA‑1.6‑34B \cite{liu2024llava} and Qwen2.5‑VL‑72B \cite{bai2025qwen25vl}.  
Prompts are identical at the byte level, and no per‑model tuning is applied. The goal is to measure how portable a fixed prompt set is, not to measure the best possible performance of either model.

Three controls make the comparison attributable.

\paragraph{Identical inputs, verified}  
All results are computed on the 4,110 frames and 90,176 masks that both runs completed. The comparison is paired at mask level. We verify that both runs saw the same mask identifiers, the same Swin agreement values, the same LiDAR support values, and the same number of discovery candidates per frame. Triage outcomes are recomputed offline under one rule for both runs, so a rule change cannot appear as a model difference.

\paragraph{Verdicts reported as recorded, with provenance}  
BBox rates include masks whose verdict is a \texttt{safe\_default}, because that is the verdict the pipeline acted on. For Qwen we also report the default share and the answered‑only rate. LLaVA’s run predates per‑agent parse telemetry, so its defaulted masks cannot be separated from confidently valid ones. Inequality allows us to reason about direction even without full decomposition.

\paragraph{Response provenance separated from model behaviour}  
Both runs store discovery responses verbatim. We re‑parse them with a single parser and separate four cases that a simple confirmed flag would collapse: the model named an offered class, the model declined (\texttt{other}), the model replied outside the offered vocabulary, or the model returned no content. Confirmation rates are computed over answered candidates only.

In the instrumented rerun, LLaVA’s non‑answers are entirely model‑side (1.7 percent outside vocabulary, 0 percent empty). Qwen’s non‑answers are zero, because the health monitor described below filtered out content‑free replies that earlier campaigns had mistakenly treated as declines. This separation is essential: an earlier run produced a highly sceptical discovery profile for Qwen that turned out to be caused by a degraded server returning empty replies. Without provenance, that serving fault looked like a behavioural trait.

\paragraph{The fault and its cost}  
On this serving stack, the larger backend occasionally stops emitting a stop token and returns empty replies. The rate increases with sustained call volume. The run reported here uses a health monitor that tracks unparseable replies in a sliding window and aborts when the window saturates. The monitor discards the in‑flight frame, reloads the model, and resumes. Retry and reload traffic are included in the deployment cost reported in Section~\ref{sec:results}. The 25 excluded frames are those the monitor could not clear. The smaller backend shows no such degradation, which confirms that the fault is a property of the deployment rather than the images. This is why verdict rates without provenance should not be interpreted as model behaviour.

\subsection{What the agents actually decide}

Figure~\ref{fig:qualitative} illustrates the five decision paths produced by the system, together with the agent outputs behind each case.

(a) Hallucination. SAM proposes a mask where the object is not present, and the BBox agent returns \texttt{background}. Both examples are vehicle masks on roadside vegetation: a rectangular region on a clipped hedge, and a car-shaped region inside a denser one. The Quality Agent independently reports 0.0 percent agreement on both, so two signals concur and the masks are rejected.

(b) LiDAR support fails and the mask is kept. The mask covers an articulated bus crossing a wet junction. The BBox agent returns \texttt{valid} and the Quality Agent agrees on 76.1 percent of the mask pixels, but only 4.0 percent of them carry LiDAR returns, below the threshold $\tau = 0.1$. The lower panel shows the outline enclosing almost no points: the bus is distant and the road is wet. One negative signal cannot delete, so the mask goes to the Correction agent, which returns \texttt{refine}, and the pixels are retained unchanged.

(c) The same signal fails and the mask is deleted. A pedestrian-shaped statue receives \texttt{valid} from the BBox agent, which sees a human form in the crop, but the Quality Agent finds none of the class inside the mask (0.0 percent) and LiDAR support is 8.4 percent. Two negatives outweigh the single positive and the concordance rule rejects the mask. Panels (b) and (c) are the rule in one picture: the same geometric failure removes pixels only when a second signal agrees with it, which is why no agent can delete alone.

(d) The two judges disagree, in both directions. They read different evidence, which is what makes the disagreement informative: the BBox agent sees only the cropped image and names what is in it, while the Quality Agent reads the segmentation model's per-pixel prediction inside the mask. In the upper panel the mask covers a row of parked motorcycles annotated as \texttt{cyclist}. The segmentation model assigns the class to 98.8 percent of those pixels, yet the VLM answers \texttt{invalid} on a crop that holds several overlapping machines rather than one rider. The lower panel reverses the roles: the mask covers a single pedestrian at a crossing, the VLM answers \texttt{valid}, and the segmentation model recognises the class on only 5.3 percent of the pixels. Neither case deletes anything. A lone negative is one negative, so both masks are retained and flagged, and a real object survives a single wrong verdict from either judge.

(e) Discovery. SAM misses a cyclist and a pedestrian entirely. Both are recovered where the Quality Agent’s dense prediction disagrees with SAM, and the VLM confirms the class of each connected component.

These paths are shared by both backends. The rest of this section shows that running the same prompts through a different backend does not preserve which path a given mask follows.

\subsection{The two backends disagree on four crops in ten}

Table~\ref{tab:agent_behavior} gives the per-agent comparison. The main result is that portability fails: under identical prompts, the two backends agree on the BBox verdict for only 57.0 percent of masks. A fixed prompt does not define a shared decision boundary.

Qwen is the more permissive mask judge. It returns \texttt{valid} on 70.9 percent of crops, compared to LLaVA’s 62.1 percent as recorded. Qwen’s answered-only rate is 67.2 percent, already above LLaVA’s as-recorded rate. Using inequality~\eqref{eq:default_bound}, the gap on answered masks is at least 5.1 percentage points. LLaVA’s unmeasured defaults can only widen the gap.

More importantly, the difference is not uniform. Compared to LLaVA, Qwen rejects far fewer signs (10.8 percent vs.\ 26.0 percent) and cyclists (33.3 percent vs.\ 46.5 percent), but rejects more vehicles (16.3 percent vs.\ 5.1 percent) and pedestrians (35.4 percent vs.\ 16.9 percent). The decision boundary shifts differently for each class. A single acceptance rate hides this redistribution, and for safety-critical annotation tasks the class-level differences matter most.

These differences propagate into the labels. Recomputed under one rule, 40.3 percent of masks receive a different triage outcome under the two backends. Of those, 16,985 masks, or 18.8 percent of the corpus and 7.5 million object pixels, are retained by one backend and rejected by the other. Since only rejection removes pixels, this is the quantity inherited by any model trained on these annotations. Roughly one mask in five appears in one annotation set and disappears in the other, depending solely on which backend was configured.

Figure~\ref{fig:flips} shows four of these masks. The pixels, the Swin agreement, and the LiDAR support are identical in the two runs; the BBox verdict is the only thing that changes. Panels (a) and (b) are masks LLaVA retains and Qwen deletes, drawn from the two classes Qwen rejects more often; (c) and (d) reverse the direction, on the two classes LLaVA rejects more often. Neither backend is ground truth here, so the figure is not evidence about which verdict is right. What it shows is that one fixed prompt places the boundary in a different position for each class, and that the disagreement is large enough to be visible on individual objects rather than only in aggregate.

\subsection{Portability fails per-agent, not per-model}

The three discovery prompts make this clear. Given the same 55,256 candidates, the two backends confirm at nearly the same rate over answered candidates: 82.4 percent for Qwen and 83.9 percent for LLaVA. Qwen is slightly more permissive on the vehicle prompt (87.6 percent vs.\ 83.0 percent) and the sign prompt (89.3 percent vs.\ 84.2 percent). A study reporting only one confirmation rate per model would conclude that the two backends behave similarly.

The divergence appears entirely in the human prompt. When the candidate must be classified as cyclist or pedestrian, Qwen confirms only 45.3 percent of candidates, compared to LLaVA’s 85.9 percent. This produces 3,506 human objects under Qwen and 6,376 under LLaVA, from identical candidate regions. The 41-point gap is concentrated on the two classes the pipeline can least afford to lose. The failure arises from the task encoded by the prompt, not from the model’s general capability: the same backend is more permissive on the two presence-check prompts.

A third agent, the Correction Agent, fails completely, but in a way that cannot be attributed. Under Qwen it is reached on 1,449 masks and returns an unusable reply on all of them, so all fall back to \texttt{no\_refine}. Under LLaVA it is reached on 1,311 masks and answers 1,304 with \texttt{refine}. This agent differs from the others in two ways: it asks for a judgement about a hypothetical action, and it receives three images instead of one. These factors are confounded, so we cannot determine whether the failure is due to the prompt or to context length. We therefore exclude this agent from portability analysis.

Taken together, the two agents that can be attributed fail in different ways under the same prompt set. The BBox agent transfers with a class-redistributed boundary, and the discovery agent transfers on two prompts but collapses on the third. No per-model summary statistic captures these differences. Portability must be validated per agent and per class, on identical inputs, or it is not validated.

\subsection{What the larger backend costs, and what it buys}

Table~\ref{tab:timing} reports deployment cost. The BBox agent dominates runtime: 3.54 seconds per call for LLaVA and 11.39 seconds for Qwen. Since it is invoked once per mask, per-mask latency scales with model size. Annotating the 4,110 frames required 132.3 GPU-hours with LLaVA and 426.9 GPU-hours with Qwen, a factor of 3.2. Wall-clock time at four concurrent workers was 33.1 hours versus 106.7 hours. The Qwen figure includes retry and reload traffic caused by the health monitor described earlier. Estimated VRAM usage is about 22 GB for LLaVA and 51 GB for Qwen, both fitting within a single A100-80GB.

Against that cost, the larger backend is less selective, has slightly lower confirmation rates in aggregate, differs by 41 points on the human discovery prompt, and returns nothing usable for the Correction Agent. The 3.2 times increase in cost produces a different annotation distribution, not a better one on any metric measured here. Whether either backend’s triage is worth its cost requires a human reference. The narrower conclusion available from this comparison is that the additional cost does not buy portability.


\section{Conclusion}

We treated the VLM backend of a multi‑agent annotation system as the variable under study, while keeping prompts and all non‑model inputs fixed across 4,110 frames and 90,176 mask proposals.  
The goal was not simply to show that two backends differ, but to reveal how those differences appear inside a working pipeline. The results show that the differences are not uniform across agents and cannot be summarised by a single acceptance rate per model. This is precisely why localisation matters.

The two backends agree on 57.0 percent of mask‑level verdicts. One backend redistributes its rejections across classes rather than shifting selectivity uniformly, rejecting more vehicles and pedestrians while protecting signs and cyclists. The discovery agent behaves similarly on the two presence‑check prompts, yet diverges sharply on the prompt that requires a three‑way discrimination, with a 41‑point drop on the cyclist–pedestrian decision. A third agent fails completely on one backend, but its prompt and image payload differ from the others, so we report the failure without attributing it.

A single acceptance rate per model would have suggested that the discovery agent transfers cleanly, even though one of its prompts collapses. This is the practical point: portability must be validated per agent and per class, on identical inputs, or it is not validated. A pipeline‑level comparison cannot localise a failure, and an aggregate rate can hide a reversal.

Two practices follow. First, evaluate portability at the level of individual agents and classes rather than at the level of the pipeline. Second, store response provenance alongside every verdict, including whether the model answered, declined, replied outside the vocabulary, or returned no content. Without provenance, a safe‑default policy can silently convert serving faults into apparent model behaviour. In our case, this produced a plausible but entirely incorrect account of one model’s scepticism until the raw responses were re‑parsed.

The larger backend required roughly 3.2 times more inference time and produced a different annotation distribution rather than a better one. It did not buy portability.
