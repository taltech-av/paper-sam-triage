\begin{abstract}
Vision--language models (VLMs) are increasingly proposed to verify the masks the Segment Anything
Model (SAM) produces at pseudo-labeling scale, yet that verification is almost always validated by
agreement with another automated signal, which cannot say which of two disagreeing judges is right.
We build the missing reference: on the Zenseact Open Dataset a human judges every SAM proposal and
every discovery candidate in a frame in one blind pass, so one pass scores every automated signal
and attribution becomes a join rather than a question put to the labeller. Four results follow.
Because only 20.4\,\% of proposals are wrong, every rejection rule buys purity by deleting correct
labels, and F1 ranks such rules in the opposite order to their downstream effect; scored on the two
error directions separately, a free segmentation signal dominates zero-shot VLM triage at a
fraction of the cost, and is worth $+3.9$ mIoU downstream where layering either VLM backend on it
returns $-3.0$ and $-2.7$. Object discovery is measurably a boundary editor rather than an object
finder: 78.6\,\% of its candidates are rims on objects already segmented, 61.7\,\% of the pixels it
adds a human rejected, and it costs $9.4$ mIoU. Cross-model agreement is not a proxy for accuracy
--- scored against humans the two backends' ranking inverts by class, and their aggregate ordering
is set by the corpus class mix. Deleting the 5.3\,\% of object pixels a human rejected, at fixed
training-set size, is worth $+2.5$ mIoU: label purity, not verification and not volume, is the
binding constraint.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{P}{ixel-level} annotations for autonomous driving are expensive, and the Segment
Anything Model (SAM)~\cite{kirillov2023sam} has made large-scale pseudo-labeling feasible. The
challenge is reliability: SAM masks drift at boundaries, hallucinate in texturally uniform
regions, and miss small traffic participants, with errors concentrated in safety-critical
cyclists, pedestrians, and signs. Geometric filtering~\cite{zou2019confidence,lee2013pseudo,jaritz2020xmuda}
cannot detect such semantic failures: a mask with a plausible shape but the wrong class passes
every geometric check and degrades downstream training. Vision--language models
(VLMs)~\cite{liu2024llava,wang2024qwen2vl} offer richer semantic priors, and the
LLM-as-judge paradigm~\cite{zheng2023judging} suggests they can serve as semantic verifiers over
mask proposals.

The difficulty is how such verification is validated. A VLM judge is typically scored by how far it
agrees with the segmentation signal it is meant to complement, which measures concordance between
two automated judges rather than the accuracy of either: when they disagree, agreement statistics
cannot say which is right, and when they fail together they look consistent. Every design decision
that follows --- whether to add the stage, which model to serve, how to fuse the signals --- is then
made against a yardstick that cannot detect a shared error.

We supply the missing yardstick. On the Zenseact Open Dataset (ZOD)~\cite{alibeigi2023zod} we
construct a pipeline whose signals are deliberately independent --- a domain-adapted Swin
Transformer~\cite{liu2021swin} scoring mask quality, a zero-shot VLM giving an object-level verdict
on tight crops, a deterministic LiDAR support check, and a Swin-driven module proposing objects SAM
missed --- and then verify its entire flagged partition by hand, every proposal and every candidate
judged in one pass with the labeller never told which model produced what. Downstream effect is
measured by training CLFTv2~\cite{gu2024clft}, a camera--LiDAR fusion model, on each annotation
variant under a reference independent of every variant's own labels.

The result is not a better pipeline. It is a measurement of what the pipeline was worth, and the
measurement is largely negative in a way that is specific and actionable. Our contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A human-verified reference for SAM pseudo-labels} in which every mask proposal and
        every discovery candidate of the flagged partition is judged by a human, each region
        carrying the decision every automated signal made about it, so one pass scores all of them
        and attribution is performed in software rather than asked of the labeller.
  \item \textbf{An error-budget criterion for annotation rules}, separating masks wrongly kept from
        masks wrongly deleted, which ranks rules in the opposite order to pooled metrics such as F1
        and in the same order as their downstream effect.
  \item \textbf{A geometric decomposition of object discovery} showing the stage to be predominantly
        a boundary editor rather than an object finder, quantifying the pixel contamination that
        results, and identifying a filter that separates the candidate pool for free.
  \item \textbf{Evidence that cross-model agreement is not a proxy for accuracy}: scored against
        human verdicts rather than against each other, two open VLM backends invert their ranking by
        class, and their aggregate ordering is set by the class composition of the corpus.
  \item \textbf{A downstream study under a single admissible reference} with paired bootstrap
        intervals, including a controlled experiment holding training-set size fixed while only
        human cleaning varies, and \textbf{a cost accounting against human effort}.
\end{itemize}

A companion paper~\cite{companion} specifies the multi-agent system, the corpus construction, and
the behavioural comparison of the two backends against each other --- what each agent decides, and
how a fixed prompt transfers. That is the measurement this paper argues cannot settle accuracy; the
scoring against human verdicts is here. Except where a cross-model contrast is stated explicitly,
all VLM figures in this paper are LLaVA-1.6-34B.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

\textbf{Pseudo-labeling and geometric filtering.} Segmentation for
driving~\cite{long2015fcn,zhao2017psp,chen2018deeplab}, camera--LiDAR fusion
included~\cite{valada2019selfsupervised,jaritz2019multimodal}, depends on pixel-accurate supervision
that is expensive at scale. Pseudo-labeling and self-training~\cite{lee2013pseudo,zou2019confidence}
reduce that cost, and cross-modal consistency~\cite{jaritz2020xmuda} and LiDAR-projection
checks~\cite{behley2019semantickitti,caesar2020nuscenes} filter erroneous labels by geometric
redundancy, but a fixed threshold cannot detect a semantic error. SAM~\cite{kirillov2023sam}
produces class-agnostic proposals without per-class prompts and by the same design cannot verify
their semantic correctness, which makes post-hoc validation a necessary complement.

\textbf{VLM judges and multi-signal filtering.} The LLM-as-judge paradigm~\cite{zheng2023judging}
shows language models can evaluate other models' outputs under constrained formats, and VLMs extend
it to images~\cite{liu2024llava,ye2023mplug,wang2024qwen2vl}; a complementary line requires
agreement between independent classifiers before rejecting a
sample~\cite{malach2017decoupling,han2018co}, which motivates the concordance rule we evaluate. What
this literature does not supply is a human reference at the granularity the decisions are made at:
validation is typically model-against-model, and the quantity a practitioner needs --- how many
correct labels a rule destroys to remove one wrong one --- is not recoverable from an agreement
statistic.

\subsection{Signals}

The \textbf{consistency check} is deterministic and runs first: the fraction of mask pixels carrying
projected LiDAR returns must exceed $\tau{=}0.1$, which catches masks leaking into sky or void
before any model is called. The \textbf{quality agent} is a Swin Transformer~\cite{liu2021swin}
trained on 2{,}319 manually curated clean ZOD frames; it emits a per-pixel class map in one forward
pass per frame, so per-mask agreement $\alpha$ is read off a prediction the pipeline already has, at
no additional cost. Thresholds are class-specific, $\tau_q^{\text{large}}{=}0.30$ and
$\tau_q^{\text{small}}{=}0.15$, because Swin is stronger on large objects than on cyclists and
pedestrians. The \textbf{bbox agent} is the one signal that costs a model call per mask: a zero-shot
VLM is shown the mask's bounding box as a tight crop and asked to name what it contains from a
closed list carrying decoy categories, and the answer reduces to \texttt{valid}, \texttt{background},
or \texttt{invalid}. It is not told the expected class and is given neither the overlay nor the
depth channel, which is what keeps its verdict independent of the signal it is fused with rather
than correlated with it by construction.

\subsection{Deterministic Triage}
Rejection is destructive --- deleted pixels cannot be recovered --- so instead of weighted scoring
we require concordant evidence. With
$\mathcal{N}=\{\texttt{invalid},\texttt{bad},\texttt{fail}\}$ the negative signals, a mask is
\textbf{rejected} on at least two signals in $\mathcal{N}$ (a \texttt{background} verdict counts as
two), \textbf{accepted} on three positive signals, and otherwise retained but flagged for
\textbf{human review}, so no single erroneous judgment silently deletes a valid label;
\texttt{bbox=invalid} paired with \texttt{quality=good} is downgraded to one negative signal to
avoid over-rejecting small objects. Prompts, thresholds and rules are fixed before evaluation and
every outcome traces to explicit agent outputs, so all annotation variants are reproducible offline
from stored responses without re-running VLM inference.

\subsection{Object Discovery and Candidate Geometry}
\label{sec:geometry}
Triage removes false positives but cannot recover objects SAM missed. A discovery step compares
the Swin class map to the SAM annotation at $384{\times}384$; pixels where Swin predicts a
non-background class and SAM predicts background form candidates. Components below 20~px are
discarded and the largest remaining ones are cropped and confirmed by a class-specific VLM prompt.
Confirmed regions become new masks, modifying only pixels still labeled background.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \resizebox{\columnwidth}{!}{\input{diagrams/candidate_formation.tex}}
    \caption{How a discovery candidate is formed, and why its geometry is not incidental. The stage
    subtracts SAM's coverage from the Swin class map \emph{before} connected components are formed,
    so the same operation yields two different objects: on something the pipeline had already
    segmented all that survives is the rim around it (top), while on something SAM missed the
    candidate is the whole object as Swin sees it (bottom). This is the premise the columns of
    Table~\ref{tab:discovery_geometry} rest on, and it is why a rejected rim is paint applied beside
    a correct object rather than a harmless duplicate --- the rim never overlaps the mask it
    surrounds. Which of the two a candidate is can be decided from geometry alone, before any model
    call and without a human label. Components below 20\,px are discarded and the largest $K$ per
    frame are cropped and shown to the VLM.}
    \label{fig:candidate_formation}
\end{figure}

Because SAM's coverage is subtracted \emph{before} components are formed, this stage produces two
geometrically distinct things (Fig.~\ref{fig:candidate_formation}): a candidate on an object SAM
already found is only the rim left over, while one on an object SAM missed entirely is the whole
object as Swin sees it. These are different findings and a pooled precision hides the difference.
The reliable test asks whether the ring of SAM pixels around a candidate contains a mask of the
candidate's \emph{own class}; if it does, the candidate is a rim.\footnote{%
A cheaper ring-share threshold --- how much of the neighbourhood is SAM-covered at all --- is
one-sided in its errors: reliable on the fringe side (97.6\,\% of fringes do abut a same-class mask)
but leaky on the other, 33.3\,\% of what it calls standalone abutting one too. Where the distinction
carries an argument we use the class test.}
The vocabularies are reconciled first, since SAM proposals carry ZOD's four classes and candidates
Swin's three, in which cyclist and pedestrian are one \texttt{human} class; both sides are folded to
the coarser one --- the fold the downstream mIoU uses --- before any class is compared.

\section{The Human Reference}
\label{sec:human_verification}

Mask agreement and downstream mIoU are both indirect measures of annotation quality: the first
compares two automated signals to each other, the second passes the annotations through a training
run before anything is observed. We therefore verify the annotation set directly. Every SAM
proposal and every discovery candidate on all 4{,}110 flagged frames, 145{,}428 regions in total,
is presented to a human in a single pass, painted as pixels in the frame it belongs to, and the
human marks the errors. The job was built over the entire partition and the pass is closed at
\humancoverage.

\textbf{The realised sample estimates the partition.} Labelling proceeds in job order, which is not
stratified by scene content, so rather than assume the sample is random we test the realised subset
against the frames never reached. It is not distinguishable from a random draw of the same size on
weather condition (permutation test, $p{=}0.36$), on cyclist or pedestrian presence ($p{=}0.47$),
or on annotated pixel volume per frame ($p{=}0.47$), and its class composition matches to within
$0.9$ points on every class.

\textbf{One property does not average out, and we report it rather than leave it to be discovered.}
The standard is \emph{non-stationary}: the share of SAM proposals the labeller accepts falls
monotonically with labelling order, by 2.25 points per 100 frames ($r{=}-0.902$), and the second
half of the pass is steeper than the first rather than converging. It is a change of standard, not
of material --- scene composition, mask size and mean Swin agreement are flat across the pass, yet
within every fixed band of Swin agreement the acceptance rate still falls, from 95.9\,\% to
91.6\,\% on the most confident band and from 65.2\,\% to 41.8\,\% on the least. Two consequences
follow. Absolute rates pooled over the pass are a mixture over a moving standard, so wherever one
carries an argument we give it for the final 200 frames as well, the current calibration. And
\emph{paired comparisons are immune}, both sides being judged by the same person on the same region
at the same moment --- every ranking in this paper rests on one.

\textbf{Blindness is structural rather than procedural.} Mask proposals are extracted
deterministically, so every run scored the same proposals and a human verdict is run-independent.
Each region carries the decision every automated source made about it, so one pass scores all of
them and attribution is a join performed afterwards rather than a question put to the labeller.
This removes an order effect --- a labeller who knew which model kept a mask could not judge it
neutrally --- and makes the judgements reusable if further variants are added later. Redundancy is
one labeller per region by design, the task being a click-what-is-wrong toggle rather than an expert
adjudication, so no inter-annotator statistic is available and the drift above is the property of
the reference we report in its place. The verdicts then serve two purposes: a direct precision
measurement for each automated decision (Table~\ref{tab:human_agreement}), and a human-verified
annotation set that is itself trainable.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Setup}
A previous annotation campaign inspected every candidate frame and either accepted or discarded it,
producing a \emph{clean} partition of 2{,}319 frames, used to train the Swin quality agent, and a
\emph{flagged} partition of 4{,}110 frames, which is the object of study here. Splits are stratified
over weather condition crossed with rare-class presence, with a floor of 40 frames per weather
category in the test set; the companion paper~\cite{companion} gives the partition construction,
the split protocol and the per-weather class distributions in full. VLM inference is served via
Ollama on a single A100-80GB. Of 4{,}135 flagged frames, 25 could not be annotated cleanly and are
excluded, without measurable bias in the resulting set~\cite{companion}.

\paragraph{Evaluation protocol} Comparing annotation variants requires a reference independent of
every variant's own training labels: scoring a model against the pseudo-labels it was trained on
measures label self-consistency rather than segmentation quality, and inflates precisely the
variants with the most idiosyncratic labels. The human pass supplies that reference and dictates the
split. The 1{,}001 verified frames are the only ones carrying an admissible reference, so for the
ladder they are the test set and nothing else can be: every variant trains on the 2{,}642 flagged
frames with no human labels and is scored on all 1{,}001 that have them. Rejected pixels map to
\texttt{ignore}; mIoU averages vehicle, sign and human (cyclist $+$ pedestrian) as the mean of the
five per-weather mIoUs; uncertainty comes from a paired bootstrap resampling test frames 10{,}000
times within each weather stratum with identical indices for all variants.

\subsection{Most Proposals Are Already Correct, Which Bounds What Any Filter Can Win}
\label{sec:human_results}

Humans accept 79.6\,\% of the SAM proposals they are shown (Table~\ref{tab:manual_verification}),
68.8\,\% under the final calibration. A rejection rule therefore operates on a minority of
genuinely wrong masks, and every rule we measure buys purity by deleting correct labels as well.
Table~\ref{tab:human_agreement} makes the trade-off explicit by keeping the two error directions
apart. Keeping every proposal has, by construction, a 100\,\% false-accept rate and no false
rejects; the Swin threshold cuts false accepts to 23.5\,\% at the cost of deleting 26.7\,\% of the
correct masks; VLM triage sits at the opposite end, retaining 54.9\,\% (LLaVA) and 59.5\,\% (Qwen)
of the masks a human rejected while deleting 14.1\,\% and 11.4\,\% of the correct ones. The Swin
threshold is also the rule least disturbed by the drift: between the first and last 200 frames of
the pass the base rate fell by 16.7 points, yet its two error rates moved by 2.0 and 6.2.

\textbf{This is where a single accuracy figure misleads.} On F1 the ranking is the reverse of the
downstream ranking: keeping everything scores 88.6, above every triage rule, while the Swin
threshold scores 81.7. Keeping everything scores an F1 equal to the base rate by construction,
which is the cleanest demonstration that the metric rewards the rule that deletes nothing. The
asymmetry F1 pools is real and directional --- a retained wrong mask paints wrong pixels into a
training label, whereas a deleted correct mask only leaves those pixels unlabelled --- so
annotation rules should be ranked on the two error rates separately, and we do so throughout. The
same correction applies to the deterministic LiDAR check, whose apparently strong 86.8 F1 is an
artifact of passing nearly everything: it retains 93.6\,\% of the masks a human rejected, the worst
false-accept rate of any rule measured.

\textbf{Errors concentrate where the pipeline is weakest.} Humans reject 33.7\,\% of cyclist and
34.1\,\% of pedestrian proposals against 18.0\,\% of vehicles and 16.2\,\% of signs: the classes
with the worst SAM proposals are the safety-critical ones, and also the classes where the Swin
quality signal is least reliable.

\textbf{Cross-model agreement is not a proxy for accuracy.} The two backends agree with each other
on 57.0\,\% of mask verdicts~\cite{companion}, and an agreement statistic can say no more than that.
Scored against the human instead, Qwen2.5-VL-72B's triage is closer to the human verdict than
LLaVA-1.6-34B's by $+0.5$ to $+1.4$ F1 (paired frame-clustered bootstrap;
Table~\ref{tab:human_agreement}). Split by class the ordering inverts: Qwen leads on signs
(88.6 vs.\ 85.4) and cyclists (76.7 vs.\ 71.0) while LLaVA leads on pedestrians (83.2 vs.\ 76.4) and
vehicles (89.5 vs.\ 88.6). Signs are 50.5\,\% of the judged masks, so the aggregate ordering is set
by the class mix of this corpus rather than by a competence ordering that would transfer to another
one, and we do not report a cross-model winner without the class breakdown. The gap is a function of
the reference as well: it was $+1.7$ to $+2.9$ F1 over the first 450 frames and decays as the
labeller tightens, because Qwen's advantage sits in signs and signs are the class whose acceptance
rate fell most. An aggregate cross-model number is a statement about a corpus and a standard
together, not about either model --- which is the same failure of model-against-model validation
that motivates the reference, arriving one level up.

\textbf{Rule engineering does not move the operating point.} Qualitative inspection identifies two
triage weaknesses~\cite{companion}: unilateral rejection on a bare \texttt{background} verdict, and
a LiDAR threshold that is not class-aware. Applying both fixes rescues 1{,}002 masks (914{,}277~px,
892 of them signs) under offline replay, but scored against the human verdicts they buy a 1.7-point
reduction in masks wrongly deleted at the cost of a 1.8-point rise in masks wrongly kept --- a move
along the trade-off curve rather than off it, so we do not train them as a separate variant. Every
rule we measured within this fixed signal set sits within about a point of its neighbours on both
axes: what moves the operating point is which signals are consulted, not how their disagreement is
arbitrated.

\subsection{Downstream: the Free Signal Is the Only Filtering Stage That Helps}

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} reports the nested ladder, in which each step adds exactly one pipeline
stage so that any change is attributable to that stage. Every row the argument rests on is final;
the two full-pipeline rows are \pending{pending} on runs still in progress, and are not filled from

Applying the Swin agreement threshold to raw SAM proposals is worth $+3.9$ mIoU
(40.3 $\to$ 44.2, 95\,\% CI $[+2.5, +5.3]$, $P(\Delta\leq0)<10^{-4}$), and the gain is present in
all three classes and all five weather conditions. It is largest on signs ($+6.8$ IoU) and smallest
on vehicles ($+2.1$), matching where SAM's proposals are worst relative to their pixel volume. One
forward pass per frame, at $<$0.01\,s per mask, buys the entire measurable filtering effect in this
study. Breaking the ladder down by weather does not support the hypothesis that a filter mainly
removes low-visibility label noise: the Swin margin is $+3.8$ in day-fair against $+7.0$ in
night-fair and $+3.3$ in night-rain, so it neither widens monotonically with degradation nor stays
flat, and the adverse strata are small (29--146 frames).

\textbf{Does VLM triage add anything over Swin alone? No --- it subtracts.} Both triage variants
land significantly below the free signal they are layered on: 41.1 mIoU with LLaVA-1.6-34B ($-3.0$,
CI $[-4.5, -1.6]$) and 41.5 with Qwen2.5-VL-72B ($-2.7$, CI $[-4.1, -1.5]$), both with
$P(\Delta\geq0)<10^{-4}$ --- three quarters of the $+3.9$ the threshold had bought, given back.
Neither is distinguishable from doing nothing at all: against unfiltered raw SAM they are $+0.8$
(CI $[-1.0, +2.6]$) and $+1.2$ (CI $[-0.8, +2.9]$). Nor are the two backends distinguishable from
each other (paired CI $[-0.9, +1.4]$) despite a $3.2\times$ cost difference, so the backend
insensitivity reported at verdict level~\cite{companion} survives into the trained model.

The loss is heaviest where triage deletes most: sign IoU falls 37.8 $\to$ 35.4 (LLaVA) and 33.3
(Qwen) against 69.6 $\to$ 67.5 and 68.5 on vehicles, and both variants fall \emph{below} raw SAM in
night-rain (33.1 and 34.4 against 36.4) --- crops are least legible exactly where labels most need
protecting. A stage costing 109--352\,s of GPU time per frame had to beat 44.2 mIoU to have earned
it; it does not reliably beat 40.3.

\textbf{Discovery is strongly negative, and the human pass had already bounded it.} Adding all
Swin-detected discoveries to the Swin-filtered labels costs $-9.4$ mIoU (44.2 $\to$ 34.8, CI
$[-10.4, -8.4]$) --- more than twice the size of the filtering gain, and negative even against the
unfiltered raw-SAM baseline ($-5.5$, CI $[-6.8, -4.1]$). The damage is not confined to the classes
discovery adds: vehicle IoU falls 9.0 points and sign 12.6, so the added regions degrade the model
globally rather than trading one class against another. The deficit is also uniform across weather,
between 7 and 10 points in every condition, consistent with a mechanism --- coarse upsampled
regions painted into the labels --- that does not depend on visibility.

The discovery rows carry an evaluation caveat the others do not: an object that neither stage ever
proposed is absent from the human reference, so a model that segments one is penalised. This biases
the discovery comparison conservatively, in the direction the mask-level evidence already points,
and a $-9.4$ point deficit is far outside what a penalty of that kind can plausibly explain.

\subsection{Discovery Is a Boundary Editor Sold as an Object Finder}

An aggregate precision does not say what a wrong candidate \emph{was}, and the two possibilities
carry opposite implications. We therefore join every candidate's geometry to the human verdict on
it (Table~\ref{tab:discovery_geometry}), asking whether the ring
of SAM pixels around it contains a mask of the candidate's own class.

Four out of five candidates (78.6\,\%) are rims on an object the pipeline had already segmented,
and two thirds of those rims the human rejected: 52.0\,\% of all candidates are \emph{edge bleed},
a rim on a same-class mask judged not to be the object. Only 6.1\,\% are objects SAM missed
entirely and a human confirmed. Under the end-of-pass calibration those become 64.0\,\% and
1.8\,\%. The stage was designed and described as object recovery; measured against a human it is
predominantly boundary editing, and predominantly wrong when it edits. The mechanism is visible in
the per-class split: vehicles, which SAM finds reliably, yield the most boundary growth ---
39.3\,\% of their candidates and 42.5\,\% of their added pixels --- because on an object the
pipeline already has there is nothing left to propose but its edge.

This also explains why confirming candidates cannot rescue the stage. Standalone candidates are
27.3\,\% real before any model sees them, and only 7.4\,\% under the final calibration;
confirmation moves that by $+0.2$ points for LLaVA and $+1.4$ for Qwen, within noise of doing
nothing. On fringe candidates the signal inverts outright: the stratum neither model confirmed is
43.4\,\% real against 34.1\,\% for the stratum both confirmed. A gate uninformative on a third of
what it is shown and actively misleading on the rest cannot justify one VLM call per candidate, and
because the human judged the candidate region itself this measurement is free of the SAM-derived
reference bias affecting the downstream comparison.

\textbf{Discovery is not useless, and the aggregate hides what it recovers.} The same join isolates
841 candidates --- 0.85 per frame, $\approx$3{,}400 extrapolated --- that a human confirmed as
objects SAM never proposed, 686 touching no SAM mask at all and overwhelmingly signs (662 of 841,
10.7\,\% of sign candidates against 1.7\,\% of vehicle ones). What sinks the stage is the exchange
rate rather than an absence of finds: 0.85 confirmed recoveries per frame are bought with 9.35
human-rejected regions painted into the same labels, and the ladder scores the net.

\textbf{In pixels, which is what the loss sees, the contamination is severe.} Candidate counts and
painted pixels are different currencies: a rim on a bus and a rim on a distant sign are one
candidate each. We therefore re-derive what every candidate actually painted, reproducing the
pipeline's own operation --- the $384{\times}384$ component map upsampled to full resolution,
claiming only pixels triage left as background --- agreeing with the shipped annotation to 108\,px
per frame against some 10{,}800 added. Discovery adds 10.7\,M pixels to a label set already holding
28.5\,M object pixels, inflating the object-pixel budget by 37.5\,\%, and 61.7\,\% of what it adds
is material a human marked as not the object: 23.1\,\% of all object pixels in the resulting
labels. A contamination of that magnitude is a sufficient --- and measured rather than inferred ---
explanation for a 9.4-point deficit; what remains unproven is only that it is the \emph{operative}
one, which a geometry-gated variant would settle. Weighting by pixels moves the balance slightly in
discovery's favour, genuine recoveries being larger than average and rising from 6.1\,\% of
candidates to 9.3\,\% of added pixels while edge bleed falls from 52.0\,\% to 47.5\,\%.

\subsection{Human Cleaning Pays Out of Proportion to Its Size}

The ladder cannot answer whether human cleaning itself helps, because the human labels cover
1{,}001 frames and the automated variants 4{,}110, so a human row inside it would confound label
quality with annotation volume. Table~\ref{tab:cleaning} gives that question its own controlled
experiment, in which both arms train on the same 651 frames and differ in exactly one field:
whether the masks the human rejected are erased or kept.

The intervention is deliberately small --- deleting the rejected masks removes 5.3\,\% of the object
pixels, and adds nothing --- yet it is worth $+2.5$ mIoU (36.3 $\to$ 39.0, CI $[+0.7, +4.0]$,
$P(\Delta\leq0)=0.003$). For scale, as an order-of-magnitude remark rather than a paired comparison,
erasing one object pixel in twenty is worth about two thirds of what the Swin filter is worth on a
training set four times larger.

The lower block of Table~\ref{tab:cleaning} is the more informative half, because it reads each
class's IoU change against the share of that class the human deleted --- the dose that class
received. The three doses differ by a factor of six and the response follows them: vehicle, at a
3.5\,\% deletion rate, moves $+0.9$ IoU, while sign (12.2\,\%) moves $+3.4$ and the human classes
(20.9\,\%) $+2.4$. The heavily-cleaned classes gain three to four times what the lightly-cleaned one
does, so the aggregate is not a diffuse regularisation effect but a response localised to where the
deletions were. That sign gains slightly more than the human classes despite a smaller dose is
consistent with the corpus class composition rather than with a reversal, signs being 50.5\,\% of
judged masks; the direction and the ordering against dose are what this experiment supports, not the
exact per-class magnitudes. Label \emph{purity} rather than \emph{volume} is therefore the binding
constraint on this corpus, at this dose: the arms differ by 5.3\,\% of object pixels, and the result
does not extrapolate to an arm in which every mask was redrawn rather than deleted.

\subsection{Where Annotation Effort Should Go}
\label{sec:practice}

\textbf{Verification cost must be compared against human cost, not against zero.} Annotating the
flagged partition cost 132.3 GPU-hours with LLaVA-1.6-34B and 426.9 with Qwen2.5-VL-72B, or 109\,s
and 352\,s per frame. A human judging every proposal in a frame, presented as pixels and toggled in
one pass, takes 27\,s per frame at the median (37\,s trimmed mean), or $\approx$31--42 hours for the
entire partition. Per frame the large VLM spends $\approx$13$\times$ more machine time than the
human spends human time ($\approx$4$\times$ for the 34B model), while the human is by construction
the reference the VLM is scored against. Zero-shot mask verification at this model scale is not a
cheap approximation of human verification; on this corpus it is the expensive option.

\textbf{Keep the deterministic quality signal; do not deploy VLM triage as an automatic filter.}
It is the only automated stage measured with a positive downstream effect, $+3.9$ mIoU for one
forward pass per frame, it dominates VLM triage on the false-accept axis (23.5\,\% vs.\ 54.9\,\%),
and its operating point does not move as the reference tightens. Layering VLM triage on top costs
$-3.0$ and $-2.7$ mIoU and buys nothing measurable over not filtering at all.

\textbf{Gate discovery on geometry, which is free, rather than on a second VLM, which is not.}
The corrective is not a better confirmer but a cheaper filter applied earlier. Whether a candidate
is a rim is decidable from geometry alone --- does it abut a SAM mask of its own class --- with no
model call and no human label, so it applies to all 4{,}110 frames. Applied at proposal time it
costs nothing, it is the axis on which the candidate pool actually separates, and it leaves the
6.1\,\% of candidates that are genuine recoveries intact.

\textbf{Spend the freed budget on human verification, targeted by mask rather than by frame.}
Ranking masks by the free Swin signal reaches 44.0\,\% of all human-rejected masks within the top
20\,\% of masks, against 20.0\,\% for random order. Adding the large VLM as a \emph{ranker} rather
than a decision-maker raises that only to 47.1\,\%, and only at small budgets (27.0\,\% vs.\
21.7\,\% at a 10\,\% budget, nothing by 30\,\%), at one crop call per mask; and it must be a
tie-break rather than the primary key, since ordering by it first is \emph{worse} than the free
signals alone past a 20\,\% budget. Spending the budget on whole frames is far weaker --- 88\,\% of
frames hold at least one rejected mask, so frame-level ranking barely separates (25.2\,\% vs.\
19.9\,\% random at 20\,\%). Errors are spread thin across frames and concentrated within them, which
argues for mask-level review queues, and class priority follows the same evidence: the low-Swin
subset of the cyclist and pedestrian classes is 55\,\% of their masks while holding 81\,\% of their
errors.

\textbf{A confirm-or-reject stage cannot repair a boundary.} The structural limit behind all of the
above is that every automated stage here can only delete a mask or add a coarse one, with no
mechanism to redraw a boundary --- and boundary quality is what the human verdicts most often turn
on. Pipelines aimed at pseudo-label quality should therefore invest in correction mechanisms,
whether SAM re-prompting or human redrawing, rather than in further verification rules, whose
ceiling is set by the 20.4\,\% of proposals that are wrong in the first place.

\subsection{Limitations}

The reference drifts by twenty points of acceptance rate across the pass, which bounds what any
absolute rate measured against it can mean; every ranking we report is therefore paired, and
absolute rates are given at the final calibration alongside the pooled value. It is one annotator's
work, so a downstream gap says a model agrees more closely with that annotator on held-out frames,
not that it is closer to a neutral ground truth. An object neither SAM nor discovery ever proposed
is absent from it, so a model that segments one is penalised --- a bias running only against the
discovery variants, in the direction the candidate-level measurement already establishes. The
interface records a rejection without its reason, so we cannot report what share turn on boundary
quality. Finally, the two full-pipeline rows are pending, so the triage$\times$discovery interaction
is not separately measured; the triage comparison this paper turns on is closed at both levels.

\section{Conclusion}

We built a human reference over every SAM proposal and every discovery candidate of a flagged
partition, in one blind pass whose verdicts score every automated signal at once, and used it to
audit a multi-signal refinement pipeline. Because only 20.4\,\% of proposals are actually wrong,
verification is a purity--volume trade-off rather than a free improvement, and the metric normally
used to rank such rules orders them backwards: F1 rewards whichever rule deletes least, the
opposite of what a downstream loss rewards. Scored on the two error directions separately, a free
per-frame segmentation signal dominates zero-shot VLM triage on the direction that matters and is
worth $+3.9$ mIoU downstream, while layering either VLM backend on it costs $-3.0$ and $-2.7$ and
leaves labels indistinguishable from unfiltered SAM. Nor is the choice between those backends
settled by how far they agree: scored against the human their ranking inverts by class and their
aggregate ordering follows the corpus class mix, so an agreement statistic answers a question about
a corpus and a standard rather than about either model.

The object-discovery stage fails in a way its aggregate precision conceals. Joining candidate
geometry to human verdicts shows it recovers 841 genuine objects here, but they are 6.1\,\% of what
it proposes: 78.6\,\% are rims on objects already segmented and two thirds of those rims a human
rejected, 0.85 recoveries against 9.35 rejected regions per frame. In pixels the contamination is
sufficient on its own to explain the $-9.4$ mIoU it costs --- 61.7\,\% of what it adds is material a
human rejected, 23.1\,\% of all object pixels in the resulting labels. The property that separates
that candidate pool is geometric and free, which is the one clearly actionable fix this audit
produces.

Against all of this, deleting the 5.3\,\% of object pixels a human rejected, at fixed training-set
size, is worth $+2.5$ mIoU, with a per-class response tracking the per-class deletion dose. On this
corpus label purity is the binding constraint and human deletion is the cheapest way to buy it.
Future work follows from the limits reached rather than the successes: a protocol that pins the
labelling standard rather than more labelling under a drifting one; the geometry-gated discovery
variant, one training run that would show whether suppressing the contamination recovers the
deficit; and correction mechanisms that can redraw a boundary, for which confirmed boundary growth
at 10.8\,\% of object pixels already supplies a target.
