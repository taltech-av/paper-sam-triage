\begin{abstract}
The Segment Anything Model (SAM) makes large-scale pseudo-labeling feasible for driving
perception, and vision--language models (VLMs) are increasingly proposed to verify the masks it
produces. That verification is almost always validated by agreement with another automated signal,
which cannot say which of two disagreeing judges is right. We build the missing reference: on the
Zenseact Open Dataset a human judges every SAM proposal and every discovery candidate in a frame in
one blind pass, so a single labelling pass scores every automated signal and attribution becomes a
join rather than a question put to the labeller. Against it we report three results. Because only
20.4\,\% of proposals are actually wrong, every rejection rule buys purity by deleting correct
labels; pooled metrics such as F1 rank these rules in the opposite order to their downstream effect,
and scored on the two error directions separately a free segmentation signal dominates zero-shot VLM
triage at a fraction of the cost --- downstream that free signal is worth $+3.9$ mIoU, while
layering either VLM backend on it gives back $-3.0$ and $-2.7$. An object-discovery stage is
measurably a boundary editor rather
than an object finder: 78.6\,\% of its candidates are rims on objects already segmented, 61.7\,\% of
the pixels it adds were rejected by a human, and it costs $9.4$ mIoU downstream. And deleting the
5.3\,\% of object pixels a human rejected, at fixed training-set size, is worth $+2.5$ mIoU. On this
corpus label purity, not verification and not volume, is the binding constraint.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{P}{ixel-level} annotations for autonomous driving are expensive, and the Segment
Anything Model (SAM)~\cite{kirillov2023sam} has made large-scale pseudo-labeling feasible. The
challenge is reliability: SAM masks drift at boundaries, hallucinate in texturally uniform
regions, and miss small traffic participants, with errors concentrated in safety-critical
cyclists, pedestrians, and signs. Geometric filtering~\cite{zou2019confidence,lee2013pseudo,jaritz2020xmuda}
cannot detect such semantic failures: a mask with a plausible shape but the wrong class passes
every geometric check and degrades downstream training. Vision--language models
(VLMs)~\cite{liu2024llava,wang2024qwen2vl} offer richer semantic priors, and the
LLM-as-judge paradigm~\cite{zheng2023judging} suggests they can serve as semantic verifiers over
mask proposals.

The difficulty is how such verification is validated. A VLM judge is typically scored by how far
it agrees with the segmentation signal it is meant to complement. That measures concordance
between two automated judges rather than the accuracy of either: when they disagree, agreement
statistics cannot say which one is right, and when they fail together they look consistent. Every
design decision that follows --- whether to add the stage, which model to serve, how to fuse the
signals --- is then made against a yardstick that cannot detect a shared error.

We supply the missing yardstick. On the Zenseact Open Dataset (ZOD)~\cite{alibeigi2023zod} we
construct a pipeline whose signals are deliberately independent --- a domain-adapted Swin
Transformer~\cite{liu2021swin} scoring mask quality, a zero-shot VLM giving an object-level
verdict on tight crops, a deterministic LiDAR support check, and a Swin-driven module proposing
objects SAM missed --- and then verify its entire flagged partition by hand. Every SAM proposal
and every discovery candidate in a frame is judged directly, in one pass, with the labeller never
told which model produced what. Downstream effect is measured by training
CLFTv2~\cite{gu2024clft}, a camera--LiDAR fusion model, on each annotation variant under a
reference that is independent of every variant's own labels.

The result is not a better pipeline. It is a measurement of what the pipeline was worth, and the
measurement is largely negative in a way that is specific and actionable. Our contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A human-verified reference for SAM pseudo-labels} in which every mask proposal and
        every discovery candidate of the flagged partition is judged by a human, each region
        carrying the decision every automated signal made about it, so one labelling pass scores
        all of them and attribution is performed in software rather than asked of the labeller.
  \item \textbf{An error-budget criterion for annotation rules}, separating masks wrongly kept from
        masks wrongly deleted, which ranks rules in the opposite order to pooled metrics such as F1
        and in the same order as their downstream effect. Under it a free segmentation signal
        dominates zero-shot VLM triage on the direction that matters, and rule engineering within a
        fixed signal set moves the operating point far less than the choice of signals.
  \item \textbf{A geometric decomposition of object discovery}, joining candidate geometry to the
        human verdict, showing the stage to be predominantly a boundary editor rather than an object
        finder, quantifying the resulting pixel contamination, and identifying a filter that
        separates the candidate pool for free --- no model call and no human label.
  \item \textbf{A downstream study under a single admissible reference} with paired bootstrap
        intervals, including a controlled experiment holding training-set size fixed while only
        human cleaning varies, and \textbf{a cost accounting against human effort}.
\end{itemize}

A companion paper~\cite{companion} describes the multi-agent system in full and studies how fixed
prompts transfer across open VLM backends; that comparison is not repeated here. Except where a
cross-model contrast is stated explicitly, all VLM figures in this paper are LLaVA-1.6-34B.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

\textbf{Pseudo-labeling and geometric filtering.} Semantic segmentation underpins scene
understanding in driving~\cite{long2015fcn,zhao2017psp,chen2018deeplab}, with camera--LiDAR fusion
extending it through feature-level alignment~\cite{valada2019selfsupervised,jaritz2019multimodal},
but all of it depends on pixel-accurate supervision that is expensive at scale. Pseudo-labeling
and self-training~\cite{lee2013pseudo,zou2019confidence} reduce that cost, while cross-modal
consistency~\cite{jaritz2020xmuda} and LiDAR-projection
checks~\cite{behley2019semantickitti,caesar2020nuscenes} exploit geometric redundancy to filter
erroneous labels. These rely on fixed thresholds and cannot detect semantic errors. SAM
\cite{kirillov2023sam} changed the economics of mask generation by producing class-agnostic
proposals without per-class prompts, but by the same design it cannot verify the semantic
correctness of its own output, which makes post-hoc validation a necessary complement.

\textbf{VLM judges and multi-signal filtering.} VLMs combine image encoders with autoregressive
decoders and support structured reasoning over visual
content~\cite{liu2024llava,ye2023mplug,wang2024qwen2vl}; the LLM-as-judge
paradigm~\cite{zheng2023judging} shows language models can evaluate other models' outputs given
constrained output formats. A complementary line requires agreement between independent
classifiers before rejecting a sample, reducing the risk that a single erroneous judge discards
valid data~\cite{malach2017decoupling,han2018co}, which motivates the concordance rule we
evaluate. What this literature does not supply is a human reference at the granularity the
decisions are made at: validation is typically model-against-model, and the quantity a practitioner
needs --- how many correct labels a rule destroys to remove one wrong one --- is not recoverable
from an agreement statistic.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

The pipeline refines SAM masks with three independent signals and a deterministic rule. We describe
only what is needed to read the results. The full agent specification, the dataset construction,
and the verification-interface details are given in the accompanying technical
report~\cite{techreport}; the multi-agent system as a system, and how its behaviour changes with
the VLM backend, are the subject of a companion paper~\cite{companion}.

\subsection{Signals}

\paragraph{Consistency check} A deterministic LiDAR-support test runs first, before any VLM call:
the fraction of mask pixels carrying projected LiDAR returns must exceed $\tau{=}0.1$. This detects
masks leaking into sky or void at zero inference cost.

\paragraph{Quality agent} A Swin Transformer~\cite{liu2021swin} trained on 2{,}319 manually curated
clean ZOD frames produces a per-pixel class map in one forward pass per frame; per-mask agreement
$\alpha$ is read off that shared prediction at no additional cost. Because Swin is stronger on
large objects than on cyclists and pedestrians we use class-specific thresholds,
$\tau_q^{\text{large}}{=}0.30$ and $\tau_q^{\text{small}}{=}0.15$.

\paragraph{BBox agent} The one signal that costs a model call. A zero-shot VLM is shown the mask's
bounding box as a tight crop, enlarged to $224{\times}224$~px or more, and asked to name what it
contains from a closed list that includes decoy categories; the answer reduces to \texttt{valid},
\texttt{background}, or \texttt{invalid}. Withholding the expected class removes agreement bias, and
withholding the overlay and depth channels stops the model reporting on the scene around the object
instead of the object. Cropping to object scale is what makes the query answerable at all, since a
traffic participant may cover 10--30~px in the full frame.

\subsection{Deterministic Triage}
Rejection is destructive --- deleted pixels cannot be recovered --- so instead of weighted scoring
we require concordant evidence. With
$\mathcal{N}=\{\texttt{invalid},\texttt{bad},\texttt{fail}\}$ the negative signals, a mask is
\textbf{rejected} on at least two signals in $\mathcal{N}$ (a \texttt{background} verdict counts as
two), \textbf{accepted} on three positive signals, and otherwise retained but flagged for
\textbf{human review}, so no single erroneous judgment silently deletes a valid label;
\texttt{bbox=invalid} paired with \texttt{quality=good} is downgraded to one negative signal to
avoid over-rejecting small objects. All prompts, thresholds, and rules are fixed before evaluation
and every outcome traces to explicit agent outputs, so all annotation variants are reproducible
offline from stored responses without re-running VLM inference.

\subsection{Object Discovery and Candidate Geometry}
\label{sec:geometry}
Triage removes false positives but cannot recover objects SAM missed. A discovery step compares
the Swin class map to the SAM annotation at $384{\times}384$; pixels where Swin predicts a
non-background class and SAM predicts background form candidates. Components below 20~px are
discarded and the largest remaining ones are cropped and confirmed by a class-specific VLM prompt.
Confirmed regions become new masks, modifying only pixels still labeled background.

Because SAM's coverage is subtracted \emph{before} components are formed, this stage produces two
geometrically distinct things. A candidate on an object SAM already found is only the rim left
over; a candidate on an object SAM missed entirely is the whole object as Swin sees it. These are
different findings and a pooled precision hides the difference, so we separate them. The reliable
test asks whether the ring of SAM pixels around a candidate contains a mask of the candidate's
\emph{own class}: if it does, the candidate is a rim on an object the pipeline already had.\footnote{%
A cheaper ring-share threshold --- how much of the neighbourhood is SAM-covered at all --- is
one-sided in its errors. It is reliable on the fringe side (97.6\,\% of fringes do abut a
same-class mask) but leaky on the other: 33.3\,\% of what it calls standalone abuts a same-class
mask too. Where the distinction carries an argument we use the class test.}
The two vocabularies must be reconciled first, since SAM proposals carry ZOD's four classes while
candidates carry Swin's three, in which cyclist and pedestrian are one \texttt{human} class; both
sides are folded to the coarser one --- the same fold the downstream mIoU uses --- before any class
is compared.

\section{The Human Reference}
\label{sec:human_verification}

Mask agreement and downstream mIoU are both indirect measures of annotation quality: the first
compares two automated signals to each other, the second passes the annotations through a training
run before anything is observed. We therefore verify the annotation set directly. Every SAM
proposal and every discovery candidate on all 4{,}110 flagged frames, 145{,}428 regions in total,
is presented to a human in a single pass, painted as pixels in the frame it belongs to, and the
human marks the errors. The job was built over the entire partition and the pass is closed at
\humancoverage.

\textbf{The realised sample estimates the partition.} Labelling proceeds in job order, which is not
stratified by scene content, so rather than assume the sample is random we test the realised subset
against the frames never reached. It is not distinguishable from a random draw of the same size on
weather condition (permutation test, $p{=}0.36$), on cyclist or pedestrian presence ($p{=}0.47$),
or on annotated pixel volume per frame ($p{=}0.47$), and the class composition of the annotated
pixels matches to within $0.9$ points on every class. All intervals resample frames rather than
masks, since masks within a frame share a scene and a labeller's calibration.

\textbf{One property does not average out, and we report it rather than leave it to be discovered.}
The standard is \emph{non-stationary}: the share of SAM proposals the labeller accepts falls
monotonically with labelling order, by 2.25 points per 100 frames ($r{=}-0.902$), and the second
half of the pass is steeper than the first rather than converging. It is a change of standard, not
of material --- scene composition, mask size and mean Swin agreement are flat across the pass, yet
within every fixed band of Swin agreement the acceptance rate still falls, from 95.9\,\% to
91.6\,\% on the most confident band and from 65.2\,\% to 41.8\,\% on the least. Two consequences
follow and we apply both throughout. Absolute rates pooled over the pass are a mixture over a
moving standard, so wherever an absolute rate carries an argument we give it for the final 200
frames as well, which is the current calibration. \emph{Paired comparisons are immune}, because
both sides are judged by the same person on the same region at the same moment, and every ranking
in this paper rests on a paired comparison rather than on an absolute rate.

\textbf{Blindness is structural rather than procedural.} Mask proposals are extracted
deterministically, so every run scored the same proposals and a human verdict on a mask is
run-independent. Each region carries the decision every automated source made about it, so one pass
scores all of them and attribution is a join performed afterwards rather than a question put to the
labeller. This removes an order effect --- a labeller who knew which model kept a mask could not
judge it neutrally --- and makes the judgements reusable if further variants are added later.
Redundancy is one labeller per region by design, the task being a click-what-is-wrong toggle rather
than an expert adjudication, so no inter-annotator statistic is available and the drift above is
the property of the reference we report in its place.

The verdicts serve two purposes: they give a direct precision measurement for each automated
decision (Table~\ref{tab:human_agreement}), keeping a false accept and a false reject apart rather
than pooling them into one accuracy, and they define a human-verified annotation set that is itself
trainable.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Setup}
Each candidate frame in a previous annotation campaign was manually inspected and either accepted
or discarded, producing a \emph{clean} partition of 2{,}319 frames, used to train the Swin quality
agent, and a \emph{flagged} partition of 4{,}110 frames, which is the object of study here. Splits
are stratified over ten strata defined by weather condition (day-fair, day-rain, night-fair,
night-rain, snow) crossed with rare-class presence, with a floor of 40 frames per weather category
in the test set. VLM inference is served via Ollama on a single A100-80GB. Of 4{,}135 flagged
frames, 25 could not be annotated cleanly and are excluded; the excluded set is not distinguishable
from a random draw of the same size by mask count ($p{=}0.30$).

\paragraph{Evaluation protocol} Comparing annotation variants requires a reference independent of
every variant's own training labels: scoring a model against the pseudo-labels it was trained on
measures label self-consistency rather than segmentation quality, and inflates precisely the
variants with the most idiosyncratic labels. The human pass supplies that reference and dictates
the split. The 1{,}001 verified frames are the only frames carrying an admissible reference, so for
the annotation ladder they are the test set and nothing else can be: every variant trains on the
2{,}642 flagged frames that have no human labels and is scored on all 1{,}001 that do. Rejected
pixels map to \texttt{ignore}. mIoU averages vehicle, sign, and human (cyclist $+$ pedestrian),
taken as the mean of the five per-weather mIoUs. Uncertainty comes from a paired bootstrap: test
frames are resampled 10{,}000 times within each weather stratum with identical resample indices for
all variants, which is the statistic this design supports.

\subsection{Most Proposals Are Already Correct, Which Bounds What Any Filter Can Win}
\label{sec:human_results}

Humans accept 79.6\,\% of the SAM proposals they are shown (Table~\ref{tab:manual_verification}),
68.8\,\% under the final calibration. A rejection rule therefore operates on a minority of
genuinely wrong masks, and every rule we measure buys purity by deleting correct labels as well.
Table~\ref{tab:human_agreement} makes the trade-off explicit by keeping the two error directions
apart. Keeping every proposal has, by construction, a 100\,\% false-accept rate and no false
rejects; the Swin threshold cuts false accepts to 23.5\,\% at the cost of deleting 26.7\,\% of the
correct masks; VLM triage sits at the opposite end, retaining 54.9\,\% (LLaVA) and 59.5\,\% (Qwen)
of the masks a human rejected while deleting 14.1\,\% and 11.4\,\% of the correct ones. The Swin
threshold is also the rule least disturbed by the drift: between the first and last 200 frames of
the pass the base rate fell by 16.7 points, yet its two error rates moved by 2.0 and 6.2.

\textbf{This is where a single accuracy figure misleads.} On F1 the ranking is the reverse of the
downstream ranking: keeping everything scores 88.6, above every triage rule, while the Swin
threshold scores 81.7. Keeping everything scores an F1 equal to the base rate by construction,
which is the cleanest demonstration that the metric rewards the rule that deletes nothing. The
asymmetry F1 pools is real and directional --- a retained wrong mask paints wrong pixels into a
training label, whereas a deleted correct mask only leaves those pixels unlabelled --- so
annotation rules should be ranked on the two error rates separately, and we do so throughout. The
same correction applies to the deterministic LiDAR check, whose apparently strong 86.8 F1 is an
artifact of passing nearly everything: it retains 93.6\,\% of the masks a human rejected, the worst
false-accept rate of any rule measured.

\textbf{Errors concentrate where the pipeline is weakest.} Humans reject 33.7\,\% of cyclist and
34.1\,\% of pedestrian proposals against 18.0\,\% of vehicles and 16.2\,\% of signs: the classes
with the worst SAM proposals are the safety-critical ones, and also the classes where the Swin
quality signal is least reliable.

\textbf{Rule engineering does not move the operating point.} Qualitative inspection identifies two
triage weaknesses: unilateral rejection on a bare \texttt{background} verdict, and a LiDAR support
threshold that is not class-aware. Applying both fixes rescues 1{,}002 masks (914{,}277~px, 892 of
them signs) under offline replay, but scored against the human verdicts they buy a 1.7-point
reduction in masks wrongly deleted at the cost of a 1.8-point rise in masks wrongly kept --- a move
along the same trade-off curve rather than off it. We therefore do not train them as a separate
variant: every rule we measured within this fixed signal set sits within about a point of its
neighbours on both axes. What moves the operating point is which signals are consulted, not how
their disagreement is arbitrated.

\subsection{Downstream: the Free Signal Is the Only Filtering Stage That Helps}

Applying the Swin agreement threshold to raw SAM proposals is worth $+3.9$ mIoU
(40.3 $\to$ 44.2, 95\,\% CI $[+2.5, +5.3]$, $P(\Delta\leq0)<10^{-4}$), and the gain is present in
all three classes and all five weather conditions. It is largest on signs ($+6.8$ IoU) and smallest
on vehicles ($+2.1$), matching where SAM's proposals are worst relative to their pixel volume. One
forward pass per frame, at $<$0.01\,s per mask, buys the entire measurable filtering effect in this
study. Breaking the ladder down by weather does not support the hypothesis that a filter mainly
removes low-visibility label noise: the Swin margin is $+3.8$ in day-fair against $+7.0$ in
night-fair and $+3.3$ in night-rain, so it neither widens monotonically with degradation nor stays
flat, and the adverse strata are small (29--146 frames).

\textbf{Does VLM triage add anything over Swin alone? No --- it subtracts.} Both triage variants
land significantly below the free signal they are layered on: 41.1 mIoU with LLaVA-1.6-34B ($-3.0$,
CI $[-4.5, -1.6]$) and 41.5 with Qwen2.5-VL-72B ($-2.7$, CI $[-4.1, -1.5]$), both with
$P(\Delta\geq0)<10^{-4}$ --- three quarters of the $+3.9$ the threshold had bought, given back.
Neither is distinguishable from doing nothing at all: against unfiltered raw SAM they are $+0.8$
(CI $[-1.0, +2.6]$) and $+1.2$ (CI $[-0.8, +2.9]$). Nor are the two backends distinguishable from
each other (paired CI $[-0.9, +1.4]$) despite a $3.2\times$ cost difference, so the backend
insensitivity reported at verdict level~\cite{companion} survives into the trained model.

This is what the mask-level evidence predicted, and the loss is heaviest where triage deletes most:
sign IoU falls 37.8 $\to$ 35.4 (LLaVA) and 33.3 (Qwen) against 69.6 $\to$ 67.5 and 68.5 on
vehicles, and both variants fall \emph{below} raw SAM in night-rain (33.1 and 34.4 against 36.4) ---
crops are least legible exactly where labels most need protecting. A stage costing 109--352\,s of
GPU time per frame had to beat 44.2 mIoU to have earned it; it does not reliably beat 40.3.

\textbf{Discovery is strongly negative, and the human pass had already bounded it.} Adding all
Swin-detected discoveries to the Swin-filtered labels costs $-9.4$ mIoU (44.2 $\to$ 34.8, CI
$[-10.4, -8.4]$) --- more than twice the size of the filtering gain, and negative even against the
unfiltered raw-SAM baseline ($-5.5$, CI $[-6.8, -4.1]$). The damage is not confined to the classes
discovery adds: vehicle IoU falls 9.0 points and sign 12.6, so the added regions degrade the model
globally rather than trading one class against another. The deficit is also uniform across weather,
between 7 and 10 points in every condition, consistent with a mechanism --- coarse upsampled
regions painted into the labels --- that does not depend on visibility.

The discovery rows carry an evaluation caveat the others do not: an object that neither stage ever
proposed is absent from the human reference, so a model that segments one is penalised. This biases
the discovery comparison conservatively, in the direction the mask-level evidence already points,
and a $-9.4$ point deficit is far outside what a penalty of that kind can plausibly explain.

\subsection{Discovery Is a Boundary Editor Sold as an Object Finder}

An aggregate precision does not say what a wrong candidate \emph{was}, and the two possibilities
carry opposite implications. We therefore join every candidate's geometry to the human verdict on
it (Fig.~\ref{fig:candidate_geometry}, Table~\ref{tab:discovery_geometry}), asking whether the ring
of SAM pixels around it contains a mask of the candidate's own class.

Four out of five candidates (78.6\,\%) are rims on an object the pipeline had already segmented,
and two thirds of those rims the human rejected: 52.0\,\% of all candidates are \emph{edge bleed},
a rim on a same-class mask judged not to be the object. Only 6.1\,\% are objects SAM missed
entirely and a human confirmed. Under the end-of-pass calibration those become 64.0\,\% and
1.8\,\%. The stage was designed and described as object recovery; measured against a human it is
predominantly boundary editing, and predominantly wrong when it edits. The mechanism is visible in
the per-class split: vehicles, which SAM finds reliably, yield the most boundary growth ---
39.3\,\% of their candidates and 42.5\,\% of their added pixels --- because on an object the
pipeline already has there is nothing left to propose but its edge.

This also explains why confirming candidates cannot rescue the stage. Standalone candidates are
27.3\,\% real before any model sees them, and only 7.4\,\% under the final calibration;
confirmation moves that by $+0.2$ points for LLaVA and $+1.4$ for Qwen, within noise of doing
nothing. On fringe candidates the signal inverts outright: the stratum neither model confirmed is
43.4\,\% real against 34.1\,\% for the stratum both confirmed, so both models' confirmation lift is
negative. A gate uninformative on a third of what it is shown and actively misleading on the rest
cannot justify one VLM call per candidate. Because the human judged the candidate region itself,
this measurement is free of the SAM-derived reference bias affecting the downstream comparison.

\textbf{Discovery is not useless, and the aggregate hides what it recovers.} The same join isolates
841 candidates --- 0.85 per frame, $\approx$3{,}400 extrapolated over the candidate pool --- that a
human confirmed as objects SAM never proposed, 686 of them touching no SAM mask at all, and
overwhelmingly signs (662 of 841, 10.7\,\% of sign candidates against 1.7\,\% of vehicle ones). What
sinks the stage is therefore the exchange rate rather than an absence of finds: 0.85 confirmed
recoveries per frame are bought with 9.35 human-rejected regions painted into the same labels, an
11:1 ratio, and the downstream ladder scores the net.

\textbf{In pixels, which is what the loss sees, the contamination is severe.} Candidate counts and
painted pixels are different currencies: a rim on a bus and a rim on a distant sign are one
candidate each. We therefore re-derive what every candidate actually painted into the label,
reproducing the pipeline's own operation --- the $384{\times}384$ component map upsampled to full
resolution, claiming only pixels triage left as background --- agreeing with the shipped annotation
to 108\,px per frame against some 10{,}800 added. Discovery adds 10.7\,M pixels to a label set
already holding 28.5\,M object pixels, inflating the object-pixel budget by 37.5\,\%, and 61.7\,\%
of what it adds is material a human marked as not the object, which is 23.1\,\% of all object
pixels in the resulting labels. Close to one in four object pixels the discovery variant trains on
is a pixel the human reference calls background. A contamination of that magnitude is a sufficient
--- and measured rather than inferred --- explanation for a 9.4-point deficit; what remains
unproven is only that it is the \emph{operative} one, which a geometry-gated variant would settle.
Weighting by pixels moves the balance slightly in discovery's favour rather than against it, as
genuine recoveries are larger than average and rise from 6.1\,\% of candidates to 9.3\,\% of added
pixels while edge bleed falls from 52.0\,\% to 47.5\,\%.

\subsection{Human Cleaning Pays Out of Proportion to Its Size}

The ladder cannot answer whether human cleaning itself helps, because the human labels cover
1{,}001 frames and the automated variants 4{,}110, so a human row inside it would confound label
quality with annotation volume. Table~\ref{tab:cleaning} gives that question its own controlled
experiment, in which both arms train on the same 651 frames and differ in exactly one field:
whether the masks the human rejected are erased or kept.

The intervention is deliberately small --- deleting the rejected masks removes 5.3\,\% of the object
pixels, and adds nothing --- yet it is worth $+2.5$ mIoU (36.3 $\to$ 39.0, CI $[+0.7, +4.0]$,
$P(\Delta\leq0)=0.003$). For scale, as an order-of-magnitude remark rather than a paired comparison,
erasing one object pixel in twenty is worth about two thirds of what the Swin filter is worth on a
training set four times larger.

The lower block of Table~\ref{tab:cleaning} is the more informative half, because it reads each
class's IoU change against the share of that class the human actually deleted --- the dose that
class received. The three doses differ by a factor of six and the response follows them: vehicle,
at a 3.5\,\% deletion rate, moves $+0.9$ IoU, while sign (12.2\,\%) moves $+3.4$ and the human
classes (20.9\,\%) $+2.4$. The two heavily-cleaned classes gain three to four times what the
lightly-cleaned one does, so the aggregate is not a diffuse regularisation effect but a response
localised to where the deletions were. That sign gains slightly more than the human classes despite
a smaller dose is consistent with the class composition of the corpus rather than with a reversal,
signs being 50.5\,\% of judged masks. The direction and the ordering against dose are what this
experiment supports; the exact per-class magnitudes are not separately powered. Label \emph{purity}
rather than label \emph{volume} is therefore the binding constraint on this corpus, at this dose:
the arms differ by 5.3\,\% of object pixels, and the result does not extrapolate to an arm in which
every mask was redrawn rather than deleted.

\subsection{Where Annotation Effort Should Go}
\label{sec:practice}

\textbf{Verification cost must be compared against human cost, not against zero.} Annotating the
flagged partition cost 132.3 GPU-hours with LLaVA-1.6-34B and 426.9 with Qwen2.5-VL-72B over
4{,}110 frames, or 109\,s and 352\,s of GPU time per frame. A human labeller judging every proposal
in a frame, presented as pixels and toggled in one pass, takes 27\,s per frame at the median
(37\,s as a trimmed mean), extrapolating to $\approx$31--42 hours for the entire partition. Per
frame, the large VLM spends $\approx$13$\times$ more machine time than the human spends human time
on the same frame ($\approx$4$\times$ for the 34B model), while the human is by construction the
reference the VLM is scored against. Zero-shot mask verification at this model scale is not a cheap
approximation of human verification; on this corpus it is the expensive option.

\textbf{Keep the deterministic quality signal; do not deploy VLM triage as an automatic filter.}
It is the only automated stage measured with a positive downstream effect, $+3.9$ mIoU for one
forward pass per frame, it dominates VLM triage on the false-accept axis (23.5\,\% vs.\ 54.9\,\%),
and its operating point does not move as the human reference tightens. Layering VLM triage on top
costs $-3.0$ and $-2.7$ mIoU and buys nothing measurable over not filtering at all: not a marginal
purchase at this price, a negative one at any price.

\textbf{Gate discovery on geometry, which is free, rather than on a second VLM, which is not.}
The corrective the decomposition points to is not a better confirmer but a cheaper filter applied
earlier. Whether a candidate is a rim is decidable from geometry alone --- does it abut a SAM mask
of its own class --- with no model call and no human label, so it applies to all 4{,}110 frames. A
practitioner keeping this stage should apply that test at proposal time and send only what survives
to whatever verifier they have; it costs nothing, it is the axis on which the candidate pool
actually separates, and it leaves the 6.1\,\% of candidates that are genuine recoveries intact.

\textbf{Spend the freed budget on human verification, targeted by mask rather than by frame.}
Table~\ref{tab:review_targeting} quantifies the targeting. Ranking masks by the free Swin signal
alone reaches 44.0\,\% of all human-rejected masks within the top 20\,\% of masks, against 20.0\,\%
for random order; adding the large VLM as a \emph{ranker} rather than a decision-maker raises that
to 47.1\,\%, and more sharply at the smallest budget (27.0\,\% vs.\ 21.7\,\% at 10\,\%), while
adding nothing by 30\,\%. That gain is confined to small budgets and costs one crop call per mask,
so on this hardware it does not pay for itself; and the verdict must be used as a tie-break rather
than as the primary key, since ordering by it first is \emph{worse} than the free signals alone
past a 20\,\% budget, because it promotes high-agreement masks the model disliked ahead of
low-agreement masks it did not. Spending the budget on whole frames is far weaker --- 88\,\% of
frames contain at least one rejected mask, so frame-level ranking barely separates (25.2\,\% vs.\
19.9\,\% random at 20\,\%). Errors are spread thin across frames and concentrated within them,
which argues for mask-level review queues over frame sampling, and class priority follows the same
evidence: the low-Swin subset of the cyclist and pedestrian classes is 55\,\% of their masks while
holding 81\,\% of their errors.

\textbf{A confirm-or-reject stage cannot repair a boundary.} The structural limit behind all of the
above is that every automated stage here can only delete a mask or add a coarse one. It has no
mechanism to redraw a boundary, and boundary quality is what the human verdicts most often turn on.
Pipelines aimed at pseudo-label quality for complex scenes should therefore invest in correction
mechanisms, whether SAM re-prompting or human redrawing, rather than in further verification rules,
whose ceiling is set by the 20.4\,\% of proposals that are wrong in the first place.

\section{Limitations}

The reference drifts by twenty points of acceptance rate across the pass, which bounds what any
absolute rate measured against it can mean; every ranking we report is therefore paired, and
absolute rates are given at the final calibration alongside the pooled value. It is also one
annotator's work, so a downstream gap says a model agrees more closely with that annotator on
held-out frames, not that it is closer to a neutral ground truth. An object neither SAM nor
discovery ever proposed is absent from it, so a model that segments one is penalised; this bias
runs only against the discovery variants, in the direction the candidate-level measurement already
establishes. The interface records a rejection without its reason, so we cannot report what share
turn on boundary quality. Finally, the two full-pipeline ladder rows are pending, so the interaction
between triage and discovery is not separately measured; the triage comparison this paper turns on
is closed at both mask level and downstream.

\section{Conclusion}

We built a human reference over every SAM proposal and every discovery candidate of a flagged
partition, in one blind pass whose verdicts score every automated signal at once, and used it to
audit a multi-signal refinement pipeline. Because only 20.4\,\% of proposals are actually wrong,
verification is a purity--volume trade-off rather than a free improvement, and the metric normally
used to rank such rules orders them backwards: F1 rewards whichever rule deletes least, which is
the opposite of what a downstream loss rewards. Scored on the two error directions separately, a
free per-frame segmentation signal dominates zero-shot VLM triage on the direction that matters and
is worth $+3.9$ mIoU downstream, while rule engineering within a fixed signal set moves the
operating point by about a point on both axes. The ladder closes that comparison in the same
direction: layering either VLM backend on the free signal costs $-3.0$ and $-2.7$ mIoU and leaves
labels indistinguishable from unfiltered SAM --- not a poor buy but a net loss.

The object-discovery stage fails in a way its aggregate precision conceals. Joining candidate
geometry to human verdicts shows it recovers 841 genuine objects here, some 3{,}400 extrapolated,
but they are 6.1\,\% of what it proposes: 78.6\,\% are rims on objects already segmented and two
thirds of those rims a human rejected, an exchange rate of 0.85 recoveries against 9.35 rejected
regions per frame. In pixels the contamination is sufficient on its own to explain the $-9.4$ mIoU
it costs --- 61.7\,\% of what it adds is material a human rejected, amounting to 23.1\,\% of all
object pixels in the resulting labels. The property that separates that candidate pool is geometric
and free, which is the one clearly actionable fix this audit produces.

Against all of this, deleting the 5.3\,\% of object pixels a human rejected, at fixed training-set
size, is worth $+2.5$ mIoU, with a per-class response tracking the per-class deletion dose. On this
corpus label purity is the binding constraint and human deletion is the cheapest way to buy it.
Future work follows from the limits this study reached rather than from its successes: a protocol
that pins the labelling standard rather than more labelling under a drifting one; the
geometry-gated discovery variant, one training run that would show whether suppressing the
contamination recovers the deficit; and correction mechanisms that can redraw a boundary, for which
the pixel accounting already supplies a target, since confirmed boundary growth is 10.8\,\% of all
object pixels.
