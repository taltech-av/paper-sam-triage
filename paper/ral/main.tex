\begin{abstract}
Vision--language models (VLMs) are increasingly proposed to verify the masks the Segment Anything Model (SAM) produces at pseudo-labeling scale, yet such verifiers are almost always validated against another automated signal.
Agreement between two automated judges cannot say which of them is right where they disagree, and says nothing about whether the labels they produce train a better model.
We supply both missing measurements: on the Zenseact Open Dataset a human judges every SAM proposal and every discovery candidate in a frame in one blind pass, so a single pass scores every automated signal at once, and each annotation variant then trains a camera--LiDAR fusion model scored only on frames carrying that human reference rather than a SAM-derived one.
Only 20.4\,\% of proposals are actually wrong, so a rejection rule buys purity by deleting correct labels --- and a pooled accuracy score rewards whichever rule deletes least, which is the opposite of what downstream training rewards.
Ranked instead on the two error directions separately, a free per-frame segmentation signal dominates zero-shot VLM triage: it is worth $+3.9$ mIoU, while layering either VLM backend on top gives back three quarters of that gain and leaves the labels indistinguishable from no filtering at all --- for 109--352\,s of GPU time per frame, against the 27\,s a human needs to check the same frame by hand.
Which of the two backends sits closer to the human inverts by class, so no agreement statistic settles that choice either.
Object discovery is measurably a boundary editor rather than an object finder: 78.6\,\% of its candidates are rims on objects the pipeline had already segmented, and it costs more than twice what filtering won, three fifths of which is recovered by discarding rims on geometry alone, before any model call.
Deleting the 5.3\,\% of object pixels a human rejected, at fixed training-set size, is worth $+2.5$ mIoU: label purity, not verification and not volume, is the binding constraint.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{P}{ixel-level} annotations for autonomous driving are expensive, and the Segment Anything Model (SAM)~\cite{kirillov2023sam} has made large-scale pseudo-labeling feasible.
The challenge is reliability: SAM masks drift at boundaries, hallucinate in texturally uniform regions, and miss small traffic participants, with errors concentrated in safety-critical cyclists, pedestrians, and signs.
Geometric filtering~\cite{zou2019confidence,lee2013pseudo,jaritz2020xmuda} cannot detect such semantic failures: a mask with a plausible shape but the wrong class passes every geometric check and degrades downstream training.
Vision--language models (VLMs)~\cite{liu2024llava,wang2024qwen2vl} offer richer semantic priors, and the LLM-as-judge paradigm~\cite{zheng2023judging} suggests they can serve as semantic verifiers over mask proposals.

The difficulty is how such verification is validated.
A VLM judge is typically scored by how far it agrees with the segmentation signal it is meant to complement, which measures concordance between two automated judges rather than the accuracy of either: when they disagree, agreement statistics cannot say which is right, and when they fail together they look consistent.
Every design decision that follows is then made against a yardstick that cannot detect a shared error.

We supply the missing yardstick.
On the Zenseact Open Dataset (ZOD)~\cite{alibeigi2023zod} we construct a pipeline whose signals are deliberately independent --- a domain-adapted Swin Transformer~\cite{liu2021swin} scoring mask quality, a zero-shot VLM giving an object-level verdict on tight crops, a deterministic LiDAR support check, and a Swin-driven module proposing objects SAM missed --- and then verify a quarter of its flagged partition by hand, every proposal and every candidate in a covered frame judged in one pass with the labeller never told which model produced what.
Downstream effect is measured by training CLFTv2~\cite{gu2024clft}, a camera--LiDAR fusion model, on each annotation variant under a reference independent of every variant's own labels.

The result is not a better pipeline.
It is a measurement of what the pipeline was worth, and the measurement is largely negative in a way that is specific and actionable.
Our contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A human-verified reference for SAM pseudo-labels}, each region carrying the decision every automated signal made about it, so one pass scores all of them and attribution is performed in software rather than asked of the labeller.
  \item \textbf{An error-budget criterion for annotation rules}, separating masks wrongly kept from masks wrongly deleted, which ranks rules in the opposite order to F1 and in the same order as their downstream effect.
  \item \textbf{A geometric decomposition of object discovery} showing the stage to be predominantly a boundary editor, quantifying the pixel contamination that results, and identifying a filter that separates the candidate pool for free.
  \item \textbf{Evidence that cross-model agreement is not a proxy for accuracy}: scored against human verdicts, two open backends invert their ranking by class and their aggregate ordering follows the corpus class mix.
  \item \textbf{A downstream study under a single admissible reference} with paired bootstrap intervals, a controlled experiment holding training-set size fixed while only human cleaning varies, and \textbf{a cost accounting against human effort}.
\end{itemize}

Except where a cross-model contrast is stated explicitly, all VLM figures in this paper are LLaVA-1.6-34B.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

\textbf{Pseudo-labeling and geometric filtering.} Segmentation for driving~\cite{long2015fcn,zhao2017psp,chen2018deeplab}, camera--LiDAR fusion included~\cite{valada2019selfsupervised,jaritz2019multimodal}, depends on pixel-accurate supervision that is expensive at scale.
Pseudo-labeling and self-training~\cite{lee2013pseudo,zou2019confidence} reduce that cost, and cross-modal consistency~\cite{jaritz2020xmuda} and LiDAR-projection checks~\cite{behley2019semantickitti,caesar2020nuscenes} filter erroneous labels by geometric redundancy, but a fixed threshold cannot detect a semantic error --- and SAM~\cite{kirillov2023sam}, producing class-agnostic proposals without per-class prompts, by the same design cannot verify their semantic correctness, which makes post-hoc validation a necessary complement.

\textbf{VLM judges and multi-signal filtering.} The LLM-as-judge paradigm~\cite{zheng2023judging} shows language models can evaluate other models' outputs under constrained formats, and VLMs extend it to images~\cite{liu2024llava,ye2023mplug,wang2024qwen2vl}; a complementary line requires agreement between independent classifiers before rejecting a sample~\cite{malach2017decoupling,han2018co}, which motivates the concordance rule we evaluate.
What this literature does not supply is a human reference at the granularity the decisions are made at: validation is typically model-against-model, and how many correct labels a rule destroys to remove one wrong one is not recoverable from an agreement statistic.

Two further VLM agents exist in the system and are not evaluated here: a correction agent reached only on the refine path, and a diagnostic failure-mode classifier whose output never reaches the triage rule.
The triage pipeline, the offline replay that produces every annotation variant, and the human-verification analysis are released as open source, with the upstream annotation generator, the downstream fusion trainer, and the processed datasets and training logs.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/taltech-av/paper-vlm-annotation-pipeline}, \url{https://github.com/taltech-av/paper-aim2026-zod-sam-generator}, \url{https://github.com/taltech-av/paper-aim2026-fusion-trainer}, \url{https://app.visin.eu/datasets}, \url{https://app.visin.eu/projects/sam-vlm}}

\subsection{Signals}

The \textbf{consistency check} is deterministic and runs first: the fraction of mask pixels carrying projected LiDAR returns must exceed $\tau{=}0.1$, which catches masks leaking into sky or void before any model is called.
The \textbf{quality agent} is a Swin Transformer~\cite{liu2021swin} trained on 2{,}319 manually curated clean ZOD frames; it emits a per-pixel class map in one forward pass per frame, so per-mask agreement $\alpha$ is read off a prediction the pipeline already has, at no additional cost.
Thresholds are class-specific, $\tau_q^{\text{large}}{=}0.30$ and $\tau_q^{\text{small}}{=}0.15$, because Swin is stronger on large objects than on cyclists and pedestrians.
The \textbf{bbox agent} is the one signal that costs a model call per mask: a zero-shot VLM is shown the mask's bounding box as a tight crop and asked to name what it contains from a closed list carrying decoy categories, and the answer reduces to \texttt{valid}, \texttt{background}, or \texttt{invalid}.
It is not told the expected class and is given neither the overlay nor the depth channel, which is what keeps its verdict independent of the signals it is fused with.

\subsection{Deterministic Triage}
Rejection is destructive --- deleted pixels cannot be recovered --- so instead of weighted scoring we require concordant evidence.
With $\mathcal{N}=\{\texttt{invalid},\texttt{bad},\texttt{fail}\}$ the negative signals, a mask is \textbf{rejected} on at least two signals in $\mathcal{N}$ (a \texttt{background} verdict counts as two), \textbf{accepted} on three positive signals, routed to \textbf{refinement} when it is valid and of good quality but fails the consistency check (1.4\,\% of masks), and otherwise retained but flagged for \textbf{human review}, so no single erroneous judgment silently deletes a valid label; \texttt{bbox=invalid} paired with \texttt{quality=good} is downgraded to one negative signal to avoid over-rejecting small objects.
Prompts, thresholds and rules are fixed before evaluation and every outcome traces to explicit agent outputs, so all annotation variants are reproducible offline from stored responses without re-running VLM inference.

\subsection{Object Discovery and Candidate Geometry}
\label{sec:geometry}
Triage removes false positives but cannot recover objects SAM missed.
A discovery step compares the Swin class map to the SAM annotation at $384{\times}384$; pixels where Swin predicts a non-background class and SAM predicts background form candidates.
Components below 20~px are discarded and the largest remaining ones are cropped and confirmed by a class-specific VLM prompt.
Confirmed regions become new masks, modifying only pixels still labeled background.

Because SAM's coverage is subtracted \emph{before} components are formed, this stage produces two geometrically distinct things: a candidate on an object SAM already found is only the rim left over, and the rim never overlaps the mask it surrounds, so a rejected rim is paint applied beside a correct object rather than a harmless duplicate; a candidate on an object SAM missed entirely is the whole object as Swin sees it.
These are different findings and a pooled precision hides the difference.
The reliable test asks whether the ring of SAM pixels around a candidate contains a mask of the candidate's \emph{own class}; if it does, the candidate is a rim.\footnote{%
A cheaper ring-share threshold --- how much of the neighbourhood is SAM-covered at all --- is one-sided in its errors: 97.6\,\% of fringes abut a same-class mask, but so do 33.3\,\% of what it calls standalone.}
The vocabularies are reconciled first, since SAM proposals carry ZOD's four classes and candidates Swin's three, in which cyclist and pedestrian are one \texttt{human} class; both are folded to the coarser one --- the fold the downstream mIoU uses --- before any class is compared.

\section{The Human Reference}
\label{sec:human_verification}

Mask agreement and downstream mIoU are both indirect measures of annotation quality: the first compares two automated signals to each other, the second passes the annotations through a training run before anything is observed.
We therefore verify the annotation set directly.
The job presents every SAM proposal and every discovery candidate of the flagged partition --- 145{,}428 regions over 4{,}110 frames --- painted as pixels in the frame it belongs to, for a human to mark the errors in a single pass.
It closed at \humancoverage, and every figure below is computed on that closed export.

\textbf{The realised sample estimates the partition.} Labelling proceeds in job order, which is not stratified by scene content, so rather than assume the sample is random we test the realised subset against the frames never reached.
It is not distinguishable from a random draw of the same size on weather condition (permutation test, $p{=}0.36$), on cyclist or pedestrian presence ($p{=}0.47$), or on annotated pixel volume per frame ($p{=}0.47$), and its class composition matches to within $0.9$ points on every class.

\textbf{One property does not average out, and we report it rather than leave it to be discovered.} The standard is \emph{non-stationary}: the share of SAM proposals the labeller accepts falls monotonically with labelling order, by 2.25 points per 100 frames ($r{=}-0.902$), and the second half of the pass is steeper than the first rather than converging.
It is a change of standard, not of material --- scene composition, mask size and mean Swin agreement are flat across the pass, yet within every fixed band of Swin agreement the acceptance rate still falls, from 95.9\,\% to 91.6\,\% on the most confident band and from 65.2\,\% to 41.8\,\% on the least.
Two consequences follow.
Absolute rates pooled over the pass are a mixture over a moving standard, so wherever one carries an argument we give it for the final 200 frames as well.
And \emph{paired comparisons are immune}, both sides being judged by the same person on the same region at the same moment --- every ranking in this paper rests on one.

\textbf{Blindness is structural rather than procedural.} Mask proposals are extracted deterministically, so every run scored the same proposals and a human verdict is run-independent.
Each region carries the decision every automated source made about it, so one pass scores all of them, and attribution is a join performed afterwards rather than a question put to the labeller.
Redundancy is one labeller per region by design, the task being a click-what-is-wrong toggle rather than an expert adjudication, so no inter-annotator statistic is available and the drift above is the property of the reference we report in its place.
The verdicts serve two purposes: a direct precision measurement for each automated decision (Table~\ref{tab:human_agreement}), and a human-verified annotation set that is itself trainable.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Setup}
A previous annotation campaign inspected every candidate frame and either accepted or discarded it, producing a \emph{clean} partition of 2{,}319 frames, used to train the Swin quality agent, and a \emph{flagged} partition of 4{,}110 frames carrying 90{,}176 SAM proposals, which is the object of study here.
Frames are grouped into ten strata --- weather condition (day-fair, day-rain, night-fair, night-rain, snow) crossed with rare-class presence --- so that every split mirrors the corpus, with 10\,\% of each stratum held out for validation and a floor of 40 frames per weather category in the test set.
Two open-weight backends are served via Ollama on a single A100-80GB, LLaVA-1.6-34B~\cite{liu2024llava} and Qwen2.5-VL-72B~\cite{bai2025qwen25vl}, under byte-identical prompts with no per-model calibration.
Of 4{,}135 flagged frames, 25 could not be annotated cleanly and are excluded; the excluded set is not distinguishable from a random draw of the same size by mask count (permutation test, $p{=}0.30$).

\paragraph{Evaluation protocol} Comparing annotation variants requires a reference independent of every variant's own training labels: scoring a model against the pseudo-labels it was trained on measures label self-consistency rather than segmentation quality, and inflates precisely the variants with the most idiosyncratic labels.
The human pass supplies that reference and dictates the split.
The 1{,}001 verified frames are the only ones carrying an admissible reference, so they are the ladder's test set and nothing else can be: every variant trains on the 2{,}642 flagged frames with no human labels and is scored on all 1{,}001 that have them.
Rejected pixels map to \texttt{ignore}; mIoU averages vehicle, sign and human (cyclist $+$ pedestrian) as the mean of the five per-weather mIoUs; uncertainty comes from a paired bootstrap resampling test frames 10{,}000 times within each weather stratum with identical indices for all variants.

\subsection{Most Proposals Are Already Correct, Which Bounds What Any Filter Can Win}
\label{sec:human_results}

Humans accept 79.6\,\% of the SAM proposals they are shown (Table~\ref{tab:manual_verification}), 68.8\,\% under the final calibration.
A rejection rule therefore operates on a minority of genuinely wrong masks, and every rule we measure buys purity by deleting correct labels as well.
Table~\ref{tab:human_agreement} makes the trade-off explicit by keeping the two error directions apart.
Keeping every proposal has, by construction, a 100\,\% false-accept rate and no false rejects; the Swin threshold cuts false accepts to 23.5\,\% at the cost of deleting 26.7\,\% of the correct masks; VLM triage sits at the opposite end, retaining 54.9\,\% (LLaVA) and 59.5\,\% (Qwen) of the masks a human rejected while deleting 14.1\,\% and 11.4\,\% of the correct ones.
The Swin threshold is also the rule least disturbed by the drift: between the first and last 200 frames of the pass the base rate fell by 16.7 points, yet its two error rates moved by 2.0 and 6.2.

\textbf{This is where a single accuracy figure misleads.} On F1 the ranking is the reverse of the downstream ranking: keeping everything scores 88.6, above every triage rule, while the Swin threshold scores 81.7.
Keeping everything scores an F1 equal to the base rate by construction, which is the cleanest demonstration that the metric rewards the rule that deletes nothing.
The asymmetry F1 pools is real and directional --- a retained wrong mask paints wrong pixels into a training label, whereas a deleted correct mask only leaves those pixels unlabelled --- so annotation rules should be ranked on the two error rates separately, and we do so throughout.
The same correction applies to the deterministic LiDAR check, whose apparently strong 86.8 F1 is an artifact of passing nearly everything: it retains 93.6\,\% of the masks a human rejected, the worst false-accept rate of any rule measured.

\textbf{Errors concentrate where the pipeline is weakest.} Humans reject 33.7\,\% of cyclist and 34.1\,\% of pedestrian proposals against 18.0\,\% of vehicles and 16.2\,\% of signs: the classes with the worst SAM proposals are the safety-critical ones, and also the classes where the Swin quality signal is least reliable.

\textbf{Cross-model agreement is not a proxy for accuracy.} Under byte-identical prompts the two backends return the same verdict on 57.0\,\% of crops, and an agreement statistic can say no more than that.
Scored against the human instead, Qwen2.5-VL-72B's triage is closer to the human verdict than LLaVA-1.6-34B's by $+0.5$ to $+1.4$ F1 (paired frame-clustered bootstrap; Table~\ref{tab:human_agreement}).
Split by class the ordering inverts: Qwen leads on signs (88.6 vs.\ 85.4) and cyclists (76.7 vs.\ 71.0) while LLaVA leads on pedestrians (83.2 vs.\ 76.4) and vehicles (89.5 vs.\ 88.6).
Signs are 50.5\,\% of the judged masks, so the aggregate ordering is set by the class mix of this corpus rather than by a competence ordering that would transfer, and we do not report a cross-model winner without the class breakdown.
The gap is a function of the reference as well: it was $+1.7$ to $+2.9$ F1 over the first 450 frames and decays as the labeller tightens, because Qwen's advantage sits in signs and signs are the class whose acceptance rate fell most.
An aggregate cross-model number is a statement about a corpus and a standard together, not about either model.

\textbf{Rule engineering does not move the operating point.} Inspecting individual triage paths exposes two weaknesses of the rule: unilateral rejection on a bare \texttt{background} verdict, and a LiDAR threshold that is not class-aware.
Applying both fixes rescues 1{,}002 masks (914{,}277~px, 892 of them signs) under offline replay, but scored against the human verdicts they buy a 1.7-point reduction in masks wrongly deleted at the cost of a 1.8-point rise in masks wrongly kept --- a move along the trade-off curve rather than off it, so we do not train them as a separate variant.
Every rule we measured within this fixed signal set sits within about a point of its neighbours on both axes: what moves the operating point is which signals are consulted, not how their disagreement is arbitrated.

\subsection{Downstream: the Free Signal Is the Only Filtering Stage That Helps}

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} reports the nested ladder, in which each step adds exactly one pipeline stage so that any change is attributable to that stage.

Applying the Swin agreement threshold to raw SAM proposals is worth $+3.9$ mIoU (40.3 $\to$ 44.2, 95\,\% CI $[+2.5, +5.3]$, $P(\Delta\leq0)<10^{-4}$), and the gain is present in all three classes and all five weather conditions.
It is largest on signs ($+6.8$ IoU) and smallest on vehicles ($+2.1$), matching where SAM's proposals are worst relative to their pixel volume.
One forward pass per frame, at $<$0.01\,s per mask, buys the entire measurable filtering effect in this study.
Broken down by weather (Table~\ref{tab:weather_miou}) the margin neither widens monotonically with degradation nor stays flat ($+3.8$ day-fair, $+7.0$ night-fair, $+3.3$ night-rain), so a filter that mainly removed low-visibility label noise is not what these labels contain; the adverse strata are small (29--146 frames), which is why the overall comparison is read off the pooled bootstrap rather than off a mean over the five columns.

\input{tables/weather_miou}

\textbf{Does VLM triage add anything over Swin alone?
No --- it subtracts.} Both triage variants land significantly below the free signal they are layered on: 41.1 mIoU with LLaVA-1.6-34B ($-3.0$, CI $[-4.5, -1.6]$) and 41.5 with Qwen2.5-VL-72B ($-2.7$, CI $[-4.1, -1.5]$), both with $P(\Delta\geq0)<10^{-4}$ --- three quarters of the $+3.9$ the threshold had bought, given back.
Neither is distinguishable from doing nothing at all: against unfiltered raw SAM they are $+0.8$ (CI $[-1.0, +2.6]$) and $+1.2$ (CI $[-0.8, +2.9]$).
Nor are the two backends distinguishable from each other (paired CI $[-0.9, +1.4]$) despite a $3.2\times$ cost difference, so the backends' insensitivity at verdict level survives into the trained model.

The loss is heaviest where triage deletes most: sign IoU falls 37.8 $\to$ 35.4 (LLaVA) and 33.3 (Qwen) against 69.6 $\to$ 67.5 and 68.5 on vehicles, and both variants fall \emph{below} raw SAM in night-rain (33.1 and 34.4 against 36.4) --- crops are least legible exactly where labels most need protecting.
A stage costing 109--352\,s of GPU time per frame had to beat 44.2 mIoU to have earned it; it does not reliably beat 40.3.

\textbf{Discovery is strongly negative, and the human pass had already bounded it.} Adding all Swin-detected discoveries to the Swin-filtered labels costs $-9.4$ mIoU (44.2 $\to$ 34.8, CI $[-10.4, -8.4]$) --- more than twice the size of the filtering gain, and negative even against the unfiltered raw-SAM baseline ($-5.5$, CI $[-6.8, -4.1]$).
The damage is not confined to the classes discovery adds: vehicle IoU falls 9.0 points and sign 12.6, so the added regions degrade the model globally rather than trading one class against another.
The deficit is also uniform across weather (Table~\ref{tab:weather_miou}), between 7.4 and 10.3 points in every condition, consistent with a mechanism --- coarse upsampled regions painted into the labels --- that does not depend on visibility.

Running both stages together costs more than either alone --- 34.7 mIoU with LLaVA and 35.5 with Qwen, $-6.4$ and $-5.9$ against the same backend's triage-only variant --- so discovery dominates the interaction, and the two backends remain indistinguishable at the end of the ladder as they were in the middle of it (paired difference $-0.7$, CI $[-1.4, +0.1]$).

The discovery rows carry an evaluation caveat the others do not: an object that neither stage ever proposed is absent from the human reference, so a model that segments one is penalised.
This biases the discovery comparison conservatively, in the direction the mask-level evidence already points, and a $-9.4$ point deficit is far outside what a penalty of that kind can plausibly explain.

\subsection{Discovery Is a Boundary Editor Sold as an Object Finder}

An aggregate precision does not say what a wrong candidate \emph{was}, and the two possibilities carry opposite implications.
We therefore join every candidate's geometry to the human verdict on it (Table~\ref{tab:discovery_geometry}), asking whether the ring of SAM pixels around it contains a mask of the candidate's own class.

Four out of five candidates (78.6\,\%) are rims on an object the pipeline had already segmented, and two thirds of those rims the human rejected: 52.0\,\% of all candidates are \emph{edge bleed}, a rim on a same-class mask judged not to be the object, against 6.1\,\% that are objects SAM missed entirely and a human confirmed (64.0\,\% and 1.8\,\% under the end-of-pass calibration).
The stage was designed and described as object recovery; measured against a human it is predominantly boundary editing, and predominantly wrong when it edits.
The mechanism is visible in the per-class split: vehicles, which SAM finds reliably, yield the most boundary growth --- 39.3\,\% of their candidates and 42.5\,\% of their added pixels --- because on an object the pipeline already has there is nothing left to propose but its edge.

This also explains why confirming candidates cannot rescue the stage.
Standalone candidates are 27.3\,\% real before any model sees them, and only 7.4\,\% under the final calibration; confirmation moves that by $+0.2$ points for LLaVA and $+1.4$ for Qwen, within noise of doing nothing.
On fringe candidates the signal inverts outright: the stratum neither model confirmed is 43.4\,\% real against 34.1\,\% for the stratum both confirmed.
A gate uninformative on a third of what it is shown and actively misleading on the rest cannot justify one VLM call per candidate --- and because the human judged the candidate region itself, this measurement is free of the SAM-derived reference bias affecting the downstream comparison.

\textbf{Discovery is not useless, and the aggregate hides what it recovers.} The same join isolates 841 candidates --- 0.85 per frame, $\approx$3{,}400 extrapolated --- that a human confirmed as objects SAM never proposed, overwhelmingly signs (662 of 841, 10.7\,\% of sign candidates against 1.7\,\% of vehicle ones).
What sinks the stage is the exchange rate rather than an absence of finds: 0.85 confirmed recoveries per frame are bought with 9.35 human-rejected regions painted into the same labels, and the ladder scores the net.

\textbf{In pixels, which is what the loss sees, the contamination is severe.} Candidate counts and painted pixels are different currencies: a rim on a bus and a rim on a distant sign are one candidate each.
We therefore re-derive what every candidate actually painted by reproducing the pipeline's own operation --- the $384{\times}384$ component map upsampled to full resolution, claiming only pixels triage left as background --- which agrees with the shipped annotation to 108\,px per frame against some 10{,}800 added.
Discovery adds 10.7\,M pixels to a set already holding 28.5\,M object pixels, inflating the object-pixel budget by 37.5\,\%, and 61.7\,\% of what it adds is material a human marked as not the object --- 6.6\,M px, equal to 23.1\,\% of the object pixels the set held before discovery ran, or one pixel in six of the labels it produces.
Weighting by pixels moves the balance slightly in discovery's favour, genuine recoveries being larger than average and rising from 6.1\,\% of candidates to 9.3\,\% of added pixels while edge bleed falls from 52.0\,\% to 47.5\,\%.

\textbf{Gating on geometry recovers most of the deficit, which identifies the contamination as its operative cause.} The pixel accounting above is a \emph{sufficient} explanation for the $-9.4$; that it is the operative one is a separate claim, and it is testable without a model call or a human label.
We therefore train the variant the geometry implies: discard every candidate that abuts a same-class SAM mask at proposal time --- the 78.6\,\% of the pool that Table~\ref{tab:discovery_geometry} shows to be rims --- and paint only what survives.
It recovers $+5.6$ mIoU against ungated discovery (34.8 $\to$ 40.4, CI $[+4.7, +6.3]$, $P(\Delta\leq0)<10^{-4}$), so roughly three fifths of the deficit was the rim contamination and not some other property of the added regions: a free geometric test applied before any verification buys back more than either VLM backend's triage was worth.

It does not, however, rescue the stage.
Gated discovery is still $-3.8$ mIoU against the free signal alone (CI $[-4.8, -2.9]$) and is indistinguishable from not running discovery at all ($+0.05$ against raw SAM, CI $[-1.6, +1.6]$).
The gate converts an actively harmful stage into one that is merely not worth its inference budget --- a real improvement for a practitioner already committed to running it, and not an argument for adding it.

\subsection{Human Cleaning Pays Out of Proportion to Its Size}

The ladder cannot answer whether human cleaning itself helps, because the human labels cover 1{,}001 frames and the automated variants 4{,}110, so a human row inside it would confound label quality with annotation volume.
Table~\ref{tab:cleaning} gives that question its own controlled experiment, in which both arms train on the same 651 frames and differ in exactly one field: whether the masks the human rejected are erased or kept.
The intervention is deliberately small --- deleting them removes 5.3\,\% of the object pixels, and adds nothing --- yet its paired bootstrap difference is $+2.5$ mIoU (36.3 $\to$ 39.0, CI $[+0.7, +4.0]$, $P(\Delta\leq0)=0.003$).
For scale, as an order-of-magnitude remark rather than a paired comparison, erasing one object pixel in twenty is worth about two thirds of what the Swin filter is worth on a training set four times larger.

The lower block of Table~\ref{tab:cleaning} is the more informative half, because it reads each class's IoU change against the share of that class the human deleted --- the dose that class received.
The three doses differ by a factor of six and the response follows them: vehicle, at a 3.5\,\% deletion rate, moves $+0.9$ IoU, while sign (12.2\,\%) moves $+3.4$ and the human classes (20.9\,\%) $+2.4$.
The heavily-cleaned classes gain three to four times what the lightly-cleaned one does, so the aggregate is not a diffuse regularisation effect but a response localised to where the deletions were.
That sign gains slightly more than the human classes despite a smaller dose is consistent with the corpus class composition rather than with a reversal, signs being 50.5\,\% of judged masks; the direction and the ordering against dose are what this experiment supports, not the exact per-class magnitudes.
Label \emph{purity} rather than \emph{volume} is therefore the binding constraint at this dose --- the arms differ by 5.3\,\% of object pixels, and the result does not extrapolate to an arm in which every mask was redrawn rather than deleted.

\subsection{Where Annotation Effort Should Go}
\label{sec:practice}

\textbf{Verification cost must be compared against human cost, not against zero.} Annotating the flagged partition cost 132.3 GPU-hours with LLaVA-1.6-34B and 426.9 with Qwen2.5-VL-72B, a median 109\,s and 352\,s per frame.
A human judging every proposal in a frame, presented as pixels and toggled in one pass, takes 27\,s per frame at the median, $\approx$31--42 hours for the whole partition.
Per frame the large VLM therefore spends $\approx$13$\times$ more machine time than the human spends human time ($\approx$4$\times$ for the 34B model), while the human is by construction the reference the VLM is scored against.
Zero-shot verification at this scale is not a cheap approximation of human verification; on this corpus it is the expensive option.

\textbf{Keep the deterministic quality signal; do not deploy VLM triage as an automatic filter.} It is the only automated stage with a positive downstream effect, it dominates VLM triage on the false-accept axis (23.5\,\% vs.\ 54.9\,\%), and its operating point does not move as the reference tightens.

\textbf{Gate discovery on geometry, which is free, rather than on a second VLM, which is not.} The corrective is not a better confirmer but a cheaper filter applied earlier: whether a candidate is a rim is decidable from geometry alone, with no model call and no human label, and trained it is worth $+5.6$ mIoU over ungated discovery --- more than either backend's triage was worth in either direction.
A practitioner already committed to discovery should apply it at proposal time; one who is not should not add the stage, since even gated it does not separate from skipping it entirely.

\textbf{Spend the freed budget on human verification, targeted by mask rather than by frame.} Ranking masks by the free Swin signal reaches 44.0\,\% of all human-rejected masks within the top 20\,\% of masks, against 20.0\,\% for random order (Table~\ref{tab:review_targeting}); adding the large VLM as a \emph{ranker} rather than a decision-maker raises that only to 47.1\,\% and only at small budgets, at one crop call per mask.
Spending the budget on whole frames is far weaker --- 88\,\% of frames hold at least one rejected mask, so frame-level ranking barely separates (25.2\,\% vs.\ 19.9\,\% random at 20\,\%).
Errors are spread thin across frames and concentrated within them, which argues for mask-level review queues, and class priority follows the same evidence: the low-Swin subset of the cyclist and pedestrian classes is 55\,\% of their masks while holding 81\,\% of their errors.

\textbf{A confirm-or-reject stage cannot repair a boundary.} The structural limit behind all of the above is that every automated stage here can only delete a mask or add a coarse one, with no mechanism to redraw a boundary.
Pipelines aimed at pseudo-label quality should therefore invest in correction mechanisms, whether SAM re-prompting or human redrawing, rather than in further verification rules, whose ceiling is set by the 20.4\,\% of proposals that are wrong to begin with.

\subsection{Limitations}

The reference drifts (Section~\ref{sec:human_verification}), which bounds what any absolute rate measured against it can mean.
It is one annotator's work, so a downstream gap says a model agrees more closely with that annotator on held-out frames, not that it is closer to a neutral ground truth.
An object neither SAM nor discovery ever proposed is absent from it, so a model that segments one is penalised --- a bias running only against the discovery variants.
The interface records a rejection without its reason, so we cannot report what share turn on boundary quality.
Each ladder row is a single training run, so its interval covers test-frame resampling and not training stochasticity.
And the geometry gate is one trained variant at one threshold: we do not sweep the ring radius or the class-fold, so the $+5.6$ is one point on an unmapped curve.

\section{Conclusion}

We built a human reference over every SAM proposal and every discovery candidate of a flagged partition, in one blind pass whose verdicts score every automated signal at once, and used it to audit a multi-signal refinement pipeline.
Because only 20.4\,\% of proposals are actually wrong, verification is a purity--volume trade-off rather than a free improvement, and the metric normally used to rank such rules orders them backwards: F1 rewards whichever rule deletes least, the opposite of what a downstream loss rewards.
Scored on the two error directions separately, a free per-frame segmentation signal dominates zero-shot VLM triage on the direction that matters and is worth $+3.9$ mIoU downstream, while layering either VLM backend on it costs $-3.0$ and $-2.7$ and leaves labels indistinguishable from unfiltered SAM.
Nor is the choice between those backends settled by how far they agree: scored against the human their ranking inverts by class and their aggregate ordering follows the corpus class mix, so an agreement statistic answers a question about a corpus and a standard rather than about either model.

The object-discovery stage fails in a way its aggregate precision conceals.
Joining candidate geometry to human verdicts shows it recovers 841 genuine objects here, but they are 6.1\,\% of what it proposes, against 78.6\,\% rims on objects already segmented of which two thirds a human rejected.
In pixels the contamination explains the $-9.4$ mIoU it costs on its own, and it is the \emph{operative} cause rather than merely a sufficient one: discarding candidates that abut a same-class mask --- a geometric test needing no model call and no human label --- recovers $+5.6$ of the 9.4 points, more than either backend's triage was worth.
It does not make the stage pay, since gated discovery still does not separate from skipping discovery altogether, but it is the one clearly actionable fix this audit produces.

Against all of this, deleting the 5.3\,\% of object pixels a human rejected, at fixed training-set size, is worth $+2.5$ mIoU, with a per-class response tracking the per-class deletion dose: label purity is the binding constraint and human deletion is the cheapest way to buy it.
Future work follows from the limits reached rather than the successes --- a protocol that pins the labelling standard rather than more labelling under a drifting one, a sweep of the geometric gate we have mapped at a single operating point, and correction mechanisms that can redraw a boundary, for which confirmed boundary growth at 10.8\,\% of object pixels already supplies a target.
