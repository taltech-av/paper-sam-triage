\begin{abstract}
Automated checks for Segment Anything Model pseudo-labels are usually validated against another automated signal, and agreement between two models cannot tell which one is correct or whether their labels lead to better training outcomes.
We address this by having a human judge every SAM proposal and every discovery candidate in 1{,}001 Zenseact Open Dataset frames, producing 35{,}984 verified regions independent of every automated signal.
Only 20.4\,\% of proposals are actually wrong, so every rejection rule buys purity by deleting correct labels, and a single accuracy score ranks such rules in the opposite order to their downstream effect.
Separating the two error directions instead, a free per-frame segmentation signal is the only filter that helps, at $+3.9$ mIoU, while adding either of two open vision--language backends gives most of that gain back and leaves labels indistinguishable from no filtering, at four to thirteen times the machine time a human spends judging the same frames by hand.
Which backend aligns better with the human depends on the class, so cross-model agreement cannot decide between them.
Object discovery behaves mostly as a boundary editor rather than an object finder, with 78.6\,\% of its candidates forming rims around objects SAM already segmented, costing more than twice what filtering gains, three fifths of which a free geometry gate recovers without any model call.
Removing the 5.3\,\% of object pixels the human rejected, at fixed training-set size, yields $+2.5$ mIoU, so label purity rather than verification volume is the main limiting factor.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Pixel-level annotations for autonomous driving are expensive, and the Segment Anything Model (SAM)~\cite{kirillov2023sam} has made large-scale pseudo-labeling feasible.
What SAM has not made feasible is knowing which of those labels to trust.
SAM masks drift at boundaries, hallucinate in uniform regions, and miss small traffic participants, with errors concentrated in the safety-critical cyclist, pedestrian, and sign classes.
Refinement pipelines therefore add verification stages such as geometric consistency checks, learned quality scores, and increasingly vision--language models (VLMs)~\cite{liu2024llava,wang2024qwen2vl} that act as semantic judges.
Each stage can delete or add labels on its own authority.

How much any of these stages help is rarely measured.
A verifier is usually scored by how often it agrees with the signal it was meant to complement, which reports how far two automated judges agree rather than how accurate either one is.
Where they disagree, the statistic cannot say which is correct.
Where they fail together, they appear consistent.
Agreement also does not connect to the outcome that matters: whether a model trained on the resulting labels performs better.
Two ingredients are missing.
One is a reference the pipeline did not produce.
The other is a downstream measurement that is not scored on the pipeline’s own labels.

We supply both.
On the Zenseact Open Dataset (ZOD)~\cite{alibeigi2023zod} we take a deployed pipeline whose signals are different: a deterministic LiDAR support check, a domain-adapted camera--LiDAR fusion model scoring mask quality, a zero-shot VLM judging a tight crop, and a module proposing objects SAM missed.
We put a quarter of its flagged partition in front of a human.
Every proposal and every candidate in a covered frame is judged in one pass, and the labeller is never told which model produced which region.
Each resulting annotation variant then trains CLFTv2~\cite{gu2024clft}, a camera--LiDAR fusion model, under a reference independent of every variant’s own labels.

Our contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A human-verified reference for SAM pseudo-labels} (Section~\ref{sec:human_verification}), in which every region carries the decisions made by every automated signal, so one pass scores all of them and attribution is handled in software rather than by the labeller.
  \item \textbf{An error-budget criterion for annotation rules} (Section~\ref{exp:triage}), separating masks wrongly kept from masks wrongly deleted, which reverses F1's verdict on the two rules whose downstream effect differs and tracks that effect where a single score does not.
  \item \textbf{A backend-substitution test} (Section~\ref{exp:backends}) showing that cross-model agreement is not a proxy for accuracy, since two open backends invert their ranking by class against human verdicts and their overall ordering follows the class mix of the data.
  \item \textbf{A downstream training} (Section~\ref{exp:ladder}), in which every annotation variant is trained on frames carrying no human labels and scored only on frames that do, with paired bootstrap intervals.
  \item \textbf{A geometric decomposition of object discovery} (Section~\ref{exp:geometry}), measuring how much of the stage is boundary editing rather than object finding, in candidates and in painted pixels, and identifying a free filter that recovers three fifths of its downstream cost.
  \item \textbf{A purity experiment at fixed training-set size} (Section~\ref{exp:purity}), isolating what human deletion is worth from how many frames carry human labels.
  \item \textbf{A cost accounting against human effort} (Section~\ref{exp:cost}), pricing automated verification in the human time it is meant to save and showing where a review budget buys the most errors found.
\end{itemize}


\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

Segmentation for driving~\cite{long2015fcn,zhao2017psp,chen2018deeplab}, including camera–LiDAR fusion~\cite{valada2019selfsupervised,jaritz2019multimodal}, depends on pixel-accurate supervision that is expensive at scale~\cite{cordts2016cityscapes}.  
Pseudo-labeling and self-training~\cite{lee2013pseudo,zou2019confidence} reduce annotation cost, and cross-modal consistency~\cite{jaritz2020xmuda} together with LiDAR-projection checks~\cite{behley2019semantickitti,caesar2020nuscenes} filter erroneous labels by geometric redundancy.  
A fixed geometric threshold cannot detect a semantic error, and SAM~\cite{kirillov2023sam}, which produces class-agnostic proposals without per-class prompts, cannot verify semantic correctness either, which makes post-hoc validation necessary.  
The annotation set audited in this work was produced by such a pipeline~\cite{tahves2026samenhancedsegmentationroaddatasets}.

Work on using an LLM as a judge~\cite{zheng2023judging} shows that language models can evaluate other models' outputs under constrained formats, and VLMs extend this idea to images~\cite{liu2024llava,ye2023mplug,wang2024qwen2vl}.  
A complementary line of work requires agreement between independent classifiers before rejecting a sample~\cite{malach2017decoupling,han2018co}, which motivates the agreement rule evaluated here.  
What this work does not provide is a human reference at the level where the decisions are actually made, because validation is typically model-against-model, and an agreement statistic cannot reveal how many correct labels a rule destroys to remove one wrong one.

It is well established that training tolerates some label noise and degrades past a threshold~\cite{song2022noisysurvey}, and that auditing datasets for residual errors can reorder benchmark rankings~\cite{northcutt2021labelerrors}.  
Confident learning estimates which labels are wrong from model disagreement~\cite{northcutt2021confident}, but its unit is an image-level class, where a wrong label is one symbol and deleting it costs one sample.  
In dense segmentation, a retained wrong mask paints thousands of incorrect pixels into a training target, while a deleted correct mask relabels those pixels background, which the loss weights two orders of magnitude lower, so the two errors are not worth the same and a single accuracy score cannot express the difference.  
This is why we rank rules on the two error directions separately.  
The error estimates in prior work are also model-derived, which is the circularity a human pass over the same regions is needed to break.

Dense annotation cost motivates pseudo-labeling in the first place~\cite{cordts2016cityscapes}, and active learning studies how to spend a fixed human budget by choosing which samples to label~\cite{settles2009active}.  
The question here is adjacent: given labels a pipeline has already produced, which of them are worth a human's attention, and how that human budget compares to the machine time spent avoiding it.  
Verification cost is usually reported only in GPU time, without the human baseline that would indicate whether the automated stage is actually the cheaper option.  
We report both costs on the same regions.

\subsection{Signals}

Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture} outlines the pipeline.
Each proposal is scored by three judges that come from different parts of the system, starting with the cheapest. 
Proposals with an extreme aspect ratio or too few pixels for their class are removed before any judge is consulted.

LiDAR support is a simple arithmetic check. 
A mask must have at least $\tau = 0.1$ of its pixels covered by projected LiDAR returns. 
This catches masks that drift into sky or empty regions and uses a projection that the pipeline already computes.

Dense class agreement comes from CLFTv2~\cite{gu2024clft}, the camera--LiDAR cross-fusion transformer that is also used downstream. 
Two SwinV2-Large~\cite{liu2021swin} encoders process the camera image and the projected LiDAR map in parallel, exchanging features at every stage. 
A dense head produces a class map at $384 \times 384$. 
The tables refer to this model as Swin; it is the Large variant, while the downstream ladder trains the Base one. 
It is trained on 2{,}319 curated clean frames and never on the partition it evaluates. 
It runs once per frame, so the per-mask score, the share $\alpha$ of a mask's pixels that the class map assigns to its annotated class, comes at no extra cost. 
Support is declared when $\alpha$ passes a class-specific threshold: $0.30$ for vehicles and signs, and $0.15$ for cyclists and pedestrians. 
The network is more reliable on large rigid objects, and cyclists and pedestrians form a single class in its vocabulary, so $\alpha$ measures the combined one. 
Because depth is one of its inputs, this judge is not fully independent of the LiDAR check.

Crop recognition is the only judge that requires a model call per mask. 
A zero-shot VLM receives a tight RGB crop of the bounding box, without overlays or depth, and names the content using a closed vocabulary that mixes annotation classes with background surfaces and decoys. 
The expected answer is not provided, which keeps this verdict independent of the other two. 
The reply is interpreted in three ways. 
If it names the annotated class, or a traffic light when the annotation is a sign, the crop is considered recognised. 
If it names a background surface such as vegetation, building, sky, or snow, it is taken as evidence that no object lies under the mask. 
Any other reply counts as a failure to recognise, while a reply that cannot be read at all is scored as recognition, so that a serving fault never removes pixels.

\subsection{Deterministic Triage}

Rejection removes pixels permanently, so the system does not combine the three verdicts into a weighted score. 
Instead, it requires agreement and counts only negative signals.

A mask is accepted and written as SAM produced it when all three judges support it. 
This means the crop is recognised, the dense agreement passes its threshold, and the LiDAR check holds.

Rejection requires two negative signals. 
If the crop names a background surface, this is treated as a direct contradiction and counts as two negatives on its own. 
A failure to recognise counts twice when the dense map also withholds support, and only once when the dense map supports the class, which prevents thin signs or distant people from being removed because of an uncertain crop.

All other cases are kept and marked for human review. 
A single dissenting judge never removes pixels, so the mask stays unchanged and the disagreement becomes a request for attention rather than an edit. 
Flagging does not change any pixels, so its value cannot be inferred from the annotations alone. 
Section~\ref{sec:human_verification} measures it by having a human review every region, flagged or not.

Prompts, thresholds, and rules are fixed before evaluation, and every outcome comes from explicit agent outputs. 
All annotation variants can therefore be reproduced offline from stored responses without running VLM inference again.


\subsection{Object Discovery and Candidate Geometry}
\label{sec:geometry}

Triage removes false positives but cannot recover objects that SAM fails to detect. 
The discovery stage compares the class map to the SAM annotation at $384 \times 384$ and groups pixels into connected components wherever the network predicts an object class and SAM predicts background. 
Components smaller than 20 pixels are discarded, and the largest remaining ones are sent to the VLM as padded crops. 
Vehicle and sign candidates are posed as a single-class confirmation question, while human candidates use a three-way choice because the segmentation model merges cyclists and pedestrians into one class. 
A candidate is confirmed only when the reply names a class. 
An unreadable reply discards the candidate, which mirrors the convention used for crop recognition. 
Confirmed components become new masks and modify only pixels that SAM still marks as background.

Because SAM's coverage is removed before components are formed, discovery produces two different geometric cases. 
If SAM already found the object, the candidate is only the leftover rim, and this rim never overlaps the original mask. 
Rejecting such a rim paints pixels beside a correct object rather than duplicating it. 
If SAM missed the object entirely, the candidate is the full object as seen by the network. 

The vocabularies are aligned before any comparison. 
SAM proposals use the four ZOD classes, while discovery candidates use the three classes of the segmentation model, where cyclists and pedestrians form a single human class. 
Both are mapped to the coarser vocabulary used by the downstream mIoU before any class is checked.


\subsection{The Human Reference}
\label{sec:human_verification}

Mask agreement and downstream mIoU are indirect measures of quality, so we verify the annotations directly. 
Every SAM proposal and every discovery candidate in the flagged partition, 145{,}428 regions over 4{,}110 frames, is shown as pixels in its frame for a human to mark errors in a single pass. 
The job closed at \humancoverage, and all results use that export.
The labelled subset matches the rest of the partition on weather, presence of cyclists or pedestrians, and annotated pixel volume. 

Every region is shown regardless of what the pipeline decided about it, including masks triage deleted and candidates no backend confirmed, which is what makes a false-reject rate measurable at all.
The labeller judges the proposal, not the annotation that shipped.
Proposals are extracted deterministically, so every run scores the same regions and human verdicts do not depend on model order. 
Each region carries the decisions of all automated sources, and attribution is done afterwards. 
A labeller reviews each region, since the task is a simple click-to-mark error check rather than expert judgement.
The resulting verdicts provide direct precision estimates for each automated decision and a human‑verified annotation set that can be used for training.

\subsection{Setup}

\subsubsection{Dataset}
A previous annotation pass \cite{tahves2026samenhancedsegmentationroaddatasets} inspected every candidate frame and either accepted or discarded it, producing a clean partition of 2{,}319 frames that trains the quality signal and a flagged partition of 4{,}110 frames carrying 90{,}176 SAM proposals.
The flagged partition is the focus of the present study.
Frames are grouped by weather condition (day-fair, day-rain, night-fair, night-rain, snow) and split into training, validation, and test sets using the same procedure as in the earlier work.

\subsubsection{Backends}
Two open-weight vision-language backends are served through Ollama on a single A100--80GB GPU: LLaVA-1.6-34B \cite{liu2024llava} and Qwen2.5-VL-72B \cite{bai2025qwen25vl}.
Both run under identical prompts without per-model calibration.

\subsubsection{Reference and split}
Comparing annotation variants requires a reference independent of every variant's own training labels, since scoring a model against the pseudo-labels it was trained on measures only the internal consistency of those labels.
The human pass supplies that reference and determines the split: the 1{,}001 verified frames are the only frames carrying usable ground truth, so they are the test set for every downstream experiment, and every variant trains on the remaining 2{,}642 flagged frames, none of which contain human labels.

\subsubsection{Metric}
A mask a rule rejects is erased from the annotation, so its pixels revert to background.
mIoU averages the vehicle, sign, and human classes (cyclist and pedestrian combined) as the mean over the five weather sets.

\subsection{Triage Error Rates Against the Human Reference}
\label{exp:triage}

\subsubsection{How often SAM is wrong}
A human judged 22{,}289 SAM masks and called 20.4\,\% of them wrong (Table~\ref{tab:manual_verification}).
Most masks are therefore already correct, which limits what any filter can win: to remove the bad ones it has to delete good ones too.

\subsubsection{Two kinds of mistake}
A rule can be wrong in two ways, and they do not cost the same.
Keeping a bad mask writes wrong pixels into the training data.
Deleting a good mask turns real object pixels into background instead.
Table~\ref{tab:human_agreement} reports both rates, never one combined score.
Combining them hides the result: keeping every mask scores the highest F1 there, 88.6, and trains the worst model in Table~\ref{tab:ablation}, while the dense agreement threshold scores the lowest, 81.7, and trains the best.

\subsubsection{Where each rule sits}
Table~\ref{tab:human_agreement} places each rule on the two axes.
The dense agreement threshold is the strict one: it keeps 23.5\,\% of the bad masks, and deletes 26.7\,\% of the good ones.
VLM triage does the opposite, keeping 54.9\,\% (LLaVA-1.6-34B) and 59.5\,\% (Qwen2.5-VL-72B) of the bad masks while deleting only 14.1\,\% and 11.4\,\% of the good ones.
The LiDAR check hardly filters at all and keeps 93.6\,\% of the bad masks.

\subsubsection{Which masks are wrong}
The errors are not spread evenly (lower block of Table~\ref{tab:manual_verification}).
Humans rejected 33.7\,\% of cyclist masks and 34.1\,\% of pedestrian masks, against 18.0\,\% of vehicles and 16.2\,\% of signs.
The worst masks are on the smallest and most safety-critical objects, which are also the ones the dense signal reads least reliably.

\subsubsection{Tuning the rule does not help}
The rule can be made stricter or looser, but not better (Table~\ref{tab:operating_points}).
Asking for more LiDAR support, a threshold of $0.7$ instead of $0.1$, cuts the bad masks it keeps from 54.9\,\% to 28.5\,\%.
The price is that the good masks it deletes rise from 14.1\,\% to 33.7\,\%.
Deleting a mask as soon as any one of the three signals is negative, the crop unrecognised, the agreement below its threshold, or the LiDAR support too low, pushes the same trade to its end, keeping 11.6\,\% of the bad masks and deleting 47.5\,\% of the good ones.
Using fewer signals is what changes the trade rather than tuning these three: the dense threshold on its own deletes about a quarter of the good masks to keep a quarter of the bad ones, a corner no setting in the table reaches.

\subsection{Downstream mIoU of the Annotation Ladder}
\label{exp:ladder}

Each row of Table~\ref{tab:ablation} adds one stage to the one above it, so any change belongs to that stage.
All rows train on the same 2{,}642 frames and are scored on the same 1{,}001 human-verified frames.

\subsubsection{Filtering on the free signal helps: $+3.9$ mIoU}
Keeping only the masks the dense signal agrees with raises mIoU from 40.3 to 44.2, with a paired interval of $[+2.5, +5.3]$.
All three classes improve, and so do all five weather conditions (right-hand block of Table~\ref{tab:ablation}).
It costs one forward pass per frame, under $0.01$\,s per mask, and it is the only filtering step in this study that helps at all.
The gain does not grow as the weather gets worse, so the labels it removes are not mainly bad-visibility ones.


\subsubsection{Adding a VLM takes most of that back: $-3.0$ and $-2.7$}
Layering LLaVA-1.6-34B on the same labels drops mIoU from 44.2 to 41.1 and Qwen2.5-VL-72B to 41.5, paired differences of $-3.0$ $[-4.5, -1.6]$ and $-2.7$ $[-4.1, -1.5]$.
Both land where doing nothing lands: against unfiltered SAM they are $+0.8$ and $+1.2$, and both intervals include zero.
The two backends are also indistinguishable from each other, although one costs $3.2\times$ more to run.
The damage is worst on signs, the class triage deletes most, and in night rain both variants fall below unfiltered SAM (33.1 and 34.4 against 36.4), which is where crops are hardest to read and labels most need protecting.

\subsubsection{Discovery is much worse: $-9.4$}
Adding the discovered regions to the filtered labels drops mIoU from 44.2 to 34.8, a paired $-9.4$ $[-10.4, -8.4]$, more than twice what filtering had gained and below unfiltered SAM as well.
Every class falls together rather than one gaining at another's expense, and the deficit is between 7.4 and 10.3 points in each of the five weather conditions.
A stage that failed only in poor visibility would not look like this; coarse regions painted into the labels everywhere would.
Running triage and discovery together ends at 34.7 and 35.5, $-9.4$ and $-8.7$ against the free signal, so discovery decides the result.

\subsubsection{One bias in these rows, and it runs the other way}
An object that neither SAM nor discovery ever proposed is missing from the human reference, so a model that segments one is counted wrong.
That penalty falls on the discovery rows, and it is far too small to account for nine points.

\subsection{Candidate Geometry and the Geometry Gate}
\label{exp:geometry}

\subsubsection{Most candidates are rims, which follows from how they are formed}
SAM's coverage is subtracted before the components are formed, so on an object the pipeline already has, the only thing left to propose is its edge.
Joining each candidate's geometry to the human verdict on it (Table~\ref{tab:discovery_geometry}) puts a size on that: four candidates in five are rims around objects already segmented, the human rejected two thirds of those rims, and only 6.1\,\% are objects SAM missed and a human confirmed, 841 of them in the judged set.
Fig.~\ref{fig:geometry_cases} shows one real candidate from each case.
Counted in pixels, which is what the training loss sees, the stage inflates the object-pixel budget by 37.5\,\% and 61.7\,\% of what it adds is material a human marked as not the object.

\subsubsection{The fix is geometric rather than semantic: $+5.6$ mIoU}
Asking a backend to confirm the candidates does not filter them: standalone candidates are 27.3\,\% real before any model sees them and confirmation moves that by at most $1.4$ points, while among fringe candidates the ones neither backend confirmed are more often real than the ones both confirmed.
Discarding every candidate that abuts a same-class SAM mask at proposal time recovers $+5.6$ mIoU $[+4.7, +6.3]$ instead, three fifths of the deficit, with no model call and no human label.
It still does not make the stage pay, since gated discovery is $-3.8$ $[-4.8, -2.9]$ against the free signal alone and does not separate from skipping discovery altogether.

\subsection{Label Purity at Fixed Training-Set Size}
\label{exp:purity}

Human cleaning cannot be a row in the ladder, because the human labels cover a quarter of the frames and the comparison would confound label quality with annotation volume.
Table~\ref{tab:cleaning} holds size fixed instead: both arms train on the same 651 frames and differ only in whether the masks the human rejected are erased, which is worth $+2.5$ mIoU for deleting 5.3\,\% of the object pixels and adding nothing.
The per-class gains follow the share deleted from each class, so the effect sits where the deletions were, and it is a result about deleting masks rather than redrawing them.

\subsection{Verification Cost and Review Targeting}
\label{exp:cost}

\subsubsection{Machine time against human time}
Table~\ref{tab:verification_cost} prices one pass over the flagged partition.
Per frame the large backend spends about $13\times$ more machine time than the human spends human time, and the smaller one about $4\times$, in order to approximate the reference it is scored against.
At this scale zero-shot verification is not a cheap approximation of human verification but the expensive option.

\subsubsection{Targeting a review budget}
If the budget is human attention rather than GPU hours, it should be spent by mask rather than by frame.
Ranking masks by the free signal reaches 44.0\,\% of all human-rejected masks inside the top fifth of the queue against 20.0\,\% for random order (Table~\ref{tab:review_targeting}), while adding the large VLM as a \emph{ranker} rather than a decision-maker adds three points at one crop call per mask.
Frame-level ranking barely separates from random, because 88\,\% of frames hold at least one rejected mask: errors are spread thin across frames and concentrated within them.
The same evidence sets class priority, the low-agreement subset of the cyclist and pedestrian classes being 55\,\% of their masks and holding 81\,\% of their errors.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}

\subsection{What the results suggest}
On this data the deterministic quality signal looks worth keeping: it is the only automated stage with a positive downstream effect, and the only one whose operating point holds as the labelling standard tightens.
VLM triage is harder to justify as an automatic filter, since it returns most of that gain in exchange for a model call per mask, although it remains useful as a ranker for human review.
For discovery the cheaper corrective is the geometric gate rather than a second model, and since the gated stage does not separate from skipping discovery altogether, leaving it out is equally defensible.
These readings hold for one pipeline on one dataset, and what transfers is the way of measuring rather than the particular verdicts.

\subsection{The structural ceiling}
Every automated stage here can only delete a mask or add a coarse one, and none of them can redraw a boundary.
Pipelines aimed at pseudo-label quality should therefore invest in correction mechanisms, whether SAM re-prompting or human redrawing, rather than in further verification rules, whose ceiling is fixed by the fifth of proposals that are wrong to begin with.

\subsection{Limits of the reference}
It drifts (Section~\ref{sec:human_verification}), which bounds what any absolute rate measured against it can mean, and it is one annotator's work, so a downstream gap says a model agrees more closely with that annotator rather than with a neutral ground truth.
An object neither SAM nor discovery ever proposed is absent from it, a bias running only against the discovery variants, and the interface records a rejection without its reason, so we cannot report what share of rejections turn on boundary quality.

\subsection{Limits of the training runs}
Each ladder row is a single run, so its interval covers test-frame resampling and not training stochasticity, and the geometry gate is one trained variant at one threshold rather than a swept curve.

\section{Conclusion}

We audited a multi-signal refinement pipeline for SAM pseudo-labels twice over: against a human reference covering every proposal and every discovery candidate it produced, and against what a fusion model learns from the labels each of its stages writes.
Because most proposals are already correct, verification is a purity--volume trade-off rather than a free improvement, and the metric normally used to rank such rules orders them backwards: F1 rewards whichever rule deletes least, and a training loss rewards the opposite.
Separating masks wrongly kept from masks wrongly deleted is what makes a mask-level measurement predict the downstream one.

Read that way, the free per-frame segmentation signal is the only filtering stage that pays, at $+3.9$ mIoU.
Zero-shot VLM triage layered on it returns most of that gain, and it spends more machine time per frame than a human spends judging the same frame by hand.
Nor does it settle which backend to buy: scored against the human, two open backends invert their ranking by class, so their agreement describes the data and the labelling standard rather than either model.

Object discovery fails in a way its aggregate precision conceals.
Most of what it proposes are rims around objects the pipeline had already segmented, and most of those rims are wrong, so a stage sold as an object finder is in practice a boundary editor.
The corrective is geometric rather than semantic: discarding candidates that abut a same-class mask needs no model call and no human label, and recovers three fifths of the deficit.
That does not make the stage pay, but it is the one clearly actionable repair this audit produces.

What does pay is human deletion: erasing the object pixels a human rejected, at fixed training-set size, is worth $+2.5$ mIoU, with per-class gains that track the per-class dose.
What limits the model here is label purity, not the number of labels and not the amount of verification.

Future work follows from the limits reached rather than from the successes: a protocol that pins the labelling standard rather than more labelling under a drifting one, a sweep of the geometric gate we have mapped at a single operating point, and correction mechanisms able to redraw a boundary, for which confirmed boundary growth already supplies a target.
